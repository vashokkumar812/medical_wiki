## headache
- Character
  - Flashing lights or zigzag lines before headache + photophobia → [[migraine]] with aura
  - Pulsatile headache → Migraine.
  - Tightness over the head without other symptoms → Tension-type headache.
  - Sudden, severe headache → Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH).
  - Sudden severe headache during intercourse or near orgasm → Coital headache; exclude SAH.

- Severity
  - Disabling headache → Migraine (especially with other POUND features).
  - Sudden, extremely severe headache → Consider SAH.

- Site
  - One eye or over the temple → Cluster headache.
  - Occiput (back of the head) → Cervical spondylosis, particularly with neck symptoms.
  - Temporal region in patients over 50 → Giant cell arteritis (temporal arteritis).
  - Cheekbones, cheeks or forehead → Acute sinusitis.
  - Behind the eyes → Sinusitis or other ocular causes.

- Duration
  - 4–72 hours → Migraine.
  - Minutes to hours → Cluster headache.
  - Severe pain lasting approximately 15 minutes, with milder discomfort for hours → Coital headache.
  - Hours → Tension-type headache.

- Frequency
  - Attacks occurring in clusters over several weeks, recurring periodically → Cluster headache.
  - Recurrent headache with similar migraine features → Migraine.
  - Frequent episodes of bilateral tightness lasting hours → Tension-type headache.
  - Headache waking the patient at a similar time each night → Cluster headache ("alarm clock" headache).

- Radiation
  - Ask whether the pain spreads to the neck, face or other areas.
  - No specific radiation pattern provided in the source.

- Aggravating factors
  - During intercourse, close to or at orgasm → Coital headache; exclude SAH.
  - Worse in the morning, especially with coughing → Raised intracranial pressure (ICP) or idiopathic intracranial hypertension.
  - Associated with drowsiness or vomiting → Consider raised ICP.
  - Fever + photophobia + neck stiffness → Meningitis.
  - Worse with walking or routine physical activity → Supports migraine over tension-type headache.

- Relieving factors
  - Ask about relief with rest, sleep, darkness and medication.
  - No specific relieving factors provided in the source.

- Associated symptoms
  - Flashing lights or zigzag lines → Migraine with aura.
  - Photophobia → Migraine; also consider meningitis if fever and neck stiffness are present.
  - Lacrimation, rhinorrhoea and flushing of the forehead → Cluster headache.
  - Drowsiness or vomiting → Raised ICP.
  - Fever and neck stiffness → Meningitis.
  - Jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, blurred vision or diplopia → Giant cell arteritis.
  - Visual loss with morning headache worsened by coughing → Consider idiopathic intracranial hypertension.
  - Bilateral tightness without nausea, vomiting or neurological symptoms → Tension-type headache.
  - Neck stiffness with sudden severe headache → Consider SAH.

- Common in
  - Males → Cluster headache.
  - Middle-aged males → Coital headache.
  - Patients over 50 → Consider giant cell arteritis when temporal headache and associated symptoms are present.

- Important mnemonic
  - POUND → Migraine:
    - P — Pulsatile headache.
    - O — Duration of 4–72 hours.
    - U — Unilateral.
    - N — Nausea and/or vomiting.
    - D — Disabling headache.

- Red flags
  - Sudden, severe headache reaching maximum intensity immediately → Urgent assessment for SAH.
  - Headache with altered consciousness, new neurological deficit, or fever and neck stiffness → Urgent assessment.
  - New temporal headache with visual symptoms or jaw claudication in a patient over 50 → Urgent assessment for giant cell arteritis.
  - Headache during sexual activity → Exclude SAH before diagnosing a primary coital headache.

## Faints and fits (syncope vs seizure)

  - History of the episode
    - Complete loss of consciousness → Syncope or seizure.
    - Duration of unconsciousness → Ask how long the episode lasted.
    - Frequency of episodes → Recurrent syncope or seizures.
    - Witness description or mobile phone video → Helps classify the episode.
    - Injury during the episode → May occur with seizures or sudden falls.
    - Medications → Antihypertensives, antiarrhythmics and antiepileptic drugs.

  - Prodrome / warning symptoms
    - Nausea + stuffy room → Vasovagal syncope.
    - Blackout or dizziness on standing → Postural hypotension.
    - Strange smell, peculiar taste or déjà vu → Focal seizure aura.
    - Sweating + weakness + confusion → Hypoglycaemia.
    - No warning before sudden fall → Consider atonic seizure, cardiac syncope or drop attack.

  - Triggers
    - Standing up quickly → Postural hypotension.
    - Heavy exercise → Consider cardiac causes, including aortic stenosis.
    - Passing urine at night → Micturition syncope.
    - Exercise-associated loss of consciousness → Consider cardiac syncope.
    - Emotional or situational trigger with nausea → Vasovagal syncope.

  - Associated features during the episode
    - Tonic stiffening → Sustained muscle contraction of limbs or trunk.
    - Clonic movements → Brief, rhythmic jerking movements.
    - Tonic–clonic movements → Tonic stiffening followed by clonic jerking.
    - Atonic seizure → Sudden loss of muscle tone and fall, often without warning.
    - Myoclonic seizure → Brief jerks of the limbs; objects may be dropped.
    - Absence seizure → Brief loss of awareness with staring, without loss of motor tone.
    - Urinary or faecal incontinence → May occur during generalised tonic–clonic seizures; not diagnostic alone.
    - Tongue biting → Supports seizure but is not diagnostic alone.
    - Jerking movements → Suggest seizure but may also occur in cardiac syncope.

  - Recovery after the episode
    - Wakes up feeling normal → More suggestive of syncope.
    - Drowsiness or confusion after the episode → More suggestive of seizure.
    - Retrograde amnesia → Common after generalised tonic–clonic seizures.
    - Transient limb weakness after focal motor seizure → Todd's palsy.

  - Syncope
    - Nausea + stuffy environment → Vasovagal syncope.
    - Dizziness on standing → Postural hypotension.
    - During exercise → Consider cardiac causes, including aortic stenosis.
    - During urination at night → Micturition syncope.
    - Brief jerking movements may occur → Convulsive syncope; does not automatically mean epilepsy.

  - Focal seizures
    - Focal aura → Strange smell, peculiar taste, déjà vu, jamais vu, familiar music, fear or abdominal sensations.
    - Focal motor seizure → Rhythmic jerking of the opposite face, arm or leg.
    - Jerking spreading from one body part to another → Jacksonian seizure.
    - Blank staring + repetitive movements such as lip-smacking or picking at clothes → Focal impaired-awareness seizure.
    - Drowsiness or confusion afterwards → Supports a seizure.
    - Prolonged focal motor seizure → May be followed by Todd's palsy.

  - Generalised seizures
    - Generalised tonic–clonic seizure → Abrupt loss of consciousness with tonic stiffening and clonic jerking.
    - Absence seizure → Brief, frequent episodes of staring and loss of awareness, especially in children and adolescents.
    - Myoclonic seizure → Brief jerks, often when tired or in the morning.
    - Atonic seizure → Sudden loss of tone and collapse.
    - Urinary/faecal incontinence and tongue biting → May occur during tonic–clonic seizures.

  - Other causes of transient loss of consciousness
    - Hypoglycaemia → Particularly in patients taking insulin or oral hypoglycaemic drugs; sweating, weakness and confusion may precede unconsciousness.
    - Brainstem TIA → Blackouts are uncommon; focal neurological symptoms or signs may occur.
    - Drop attack → Sudden fall without loss of consciousness.
    - Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES) → May resemble epileptic seizures; correct diagnosis is important to avoid inappropriate anticonvulsant treatment.

  - Questions to ask a patient with suspected epilepsy
    - Has the diagnosis been confirmed? → Clinical features and investigations, such as EEG.
    - Has a cause been identified? → Developmental abnormality, CT findings, trauma.
    - When was the last seizure? → During the day or sleep.
    - How often do episodes occur?
    - Has there been any injury?
    - Was consciousness lost?
    - Has treatment been started, and has it been effective?
    - Are there medication side effects or difficulties with adherence?
    - Does the patient drive, and are they permitted to drive?
    - Has work or education been affected?
    - Is there a family history of epilepsy?

  - Important distinction
    - Seizure → An epileptic event.
    - Epilepsy → A condition characterised by an enduring predisposition to epileptic seizures.
    - Syncope → Transient loss of consciousness due to temporary global cerebral hypoperfusion.
    - Jerking movements can occur in both seizures and syncope; assess the entire episode and recovery.

## dizziness
  - Character
    - Sensation of self or surroundings moving (vertigo).
    - Clarify exactly what the patient means by dizziness.
  - Onset and duration
    - Brief, recurrent episodes → BPPV.
    - Vertigo lasting longer than a few minutes → Consider other vestibular causes.
    - Hours to days → Consider migraine or persistent vestibular causes.
  - Triggers
    - Lying down, rolling over in bed, looking up or down → BPPV; consider vestibular migraine.
    - Vertigo occurring at rest → Consider vestibular neuritis, labyrinthitis or central causes.
  - Associated auditory symptoms
    - Hearing loss → Labyrinthitis or inner-ear stroke.
    - Episodic vertigo + tinnitus + progressive deafness → Ménière disease.
    - Progressive unilateral deafness ± tinnitus → Vestibular schwannoma.
    - Normal hearing → Vestibular neuritis.
  - Associated neurological symptoms
    - Diplopia, limb ataxia or sensory disturbance → Suspect stroke.
    - Other neurological symptoms → Consider multiple sclerosis or cerebellar tumour.
  - Associated symptoms
    - Nausea, vomiting, pallor, sweating or headache.
    - Migraine history or associated migraine symptoms → Consider vestibular migraine.
  - Medication history
    - Aminoglycoside antibiotic use → Consider bilateral vestibular damage.
    - Visual surroundings jumping or slipping during head movement → Oscillopsia.
  - Age
    - Age over 50 years with episodic vertigo, tinnitus and progressive deafness → Consider Ménière disease.

## distrubed sensation or weakness in limbs
- Disturbed sensation or limb weakness
  - Sensory symptoms
    - Pins and needles in hands or feet → Nerve entrapment or peripheral neuropathy; sensory pathway lesions at any level.
    - Pain and paraesthesia in hand and wrist → Carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve entrapment).
    - Pain extending to arm or shoulder with paraesthesia limited to fingers → Consider carpal tunnel syndrome.
    - Symptoms worse at night and relieved by dangling the arm or shaking the hand (flick sign) → Carpal tunnel syndrome.
    - Sensory disturbance with limb weakness → Consider nerve root, spinal cord or cerebral pathology.
  - Limb weakness — Upper motor neurone (UMN) lesion
    - Increased muscle tone and peripheral reflexes
    - Pyramidal pattern of weakness, predominantly affecting antigravity muscles
    - Little or no muscle wasting
  - Limb weakness — Lower motor neurone (LMN) lesion
    - Reduced muscle tone and reflexes
    - Fasciculations may be present
    - Prominent muscle wasting
  - Limb weakness — Muscle disease
    - Weakness affecting particular muscles or muscle groups.
    - Muscle wasting and reduced tone.
    - Reflexes reduced or absent.
  - Limb weakness — Neuromuscular junction disorder
    - Generalised weakness that worsens with repetition.
    - Reflexes and muscle tone often normal.
    - Example: Myasthenia gravis.
  - Limb weakness — Non-organic weakness
    - Weakness follows a non-anatomical pattern.
    - Normal tone and power on examination may be demonstrated.
    - Muscle bulk usually normal unless prolonged disuse has occurred.


## Tremor and involuntary movements
  - Timing and triggers
    - Tremor predominantly at rest → [[parkinson's  disease]]
    - Tremor when arms are stretched out or during voluntary movement; disappears at complete rest → Essential/familial tremor ( postural tremor )
    - Fine tremor during posture or slow movement, worsened by fright or fatigue → Physiological tremor ( postural tremor )
    - Tremor worsened by anxiety or fatigue → Common feature of tremors.
    - Tremor triggered by cold → Shivering.
  - Associated symptoms and distribution
	  - Essential/familial tremor.
		  - sites
		    - Head tremor (34%)
		    - arm tremor (95%)
		    - voice tremor (12%)
		    - lower-limb tremor (30%) → 
	    - Postural tremor without other signs; usually inherited (autosomal dominant) → Essential/familial tremor.
  - Exacerbating factors and medication history
    - Caffeine or beta-agonists used for asthma → Exaggerated physiological tremor.
    - Symptoms suggestive of thyrotoxicosis → Consider exaggerated physiological tremor.
    - Fine tremor that is difficult to see → May be physiological tremor; examine specifically.

- Involuntary movements
  - Akathisia
    - Motor restlessness; constant semipurposeful movements of arms and legs.
  - Asterixis
    - Sudden loss of muscle tone during sustained contraction of an outstretched limb.
  - Athetosis
    - Slow, sinuous, writhing movements, especially of hands and wrists.
  - Chorea
    - Small, rapid, jerky movements.
    - Movements may be disguised as purposeful actions, e.g. scratching the head.
  - Dyskinesia
    - Continuous, purposeless movements, often involving face and mouth.
    - Frequently associated with major tranquillisers used for psychotic illness.
  - Dystonia
    - Sustained contractions of agonist and antagonist muscle groups.
    - Usually produces flexion or extreme extension and bizarre postures.
  - Hemiballismus
    - Exaggerated chorea affecting one side of the body.
    - Wild, flinging movements that may injure the patient or bystanders.
  - Myoclonic jerk
    - Brief muscle contraction causing sudden, purposeless jerking of a limb.
  - Myokymia
    - Repeated contractions of a small muscle group.
    - Often involves orbicularis oculi.
  - Tic
    - Repetitive, irresistible, purposeful or semipurposeful movement.
  - Tremor
    - Rhythmical, alternating movement.

## past history
  - Previous neurological conditions
    - Meningitis or encephalitis.
    - Head or spinal injuries.
    - Epilepsy or convulsions.
    - Previous operations.
  - Sexually transmitted infections
    - Previous STI history.
    - Risk factors for HIV infection or syphilis.
  - Cerebrovascular disease risk
    - Ask about risk factors predisposing to cerebrovascular disease.
    - Previous peripheral vascular disease → Increased risk of cerebrovascular disease.
    - Previous coronary artery disease → Increased risk of cerebrovascular disease.
    - Chronic or paroxysmal atrial fibrillation → Increased risk of embolic stroke, especially age >70 years.

## drug history
  - Antihypertensives
    - Therapeutic use: Reduce stroke risk.
    - Adverse effects: Postural dizziness, syncope; depression with methyldopa.
  - Antiplatelets and anticoagulants
    - Therapeutic use: Reduce stroke risk.
    - Adverse effect: Cerebral haemorrhage.
  - Statins
    - Therapeutic use: Reduce stroke risk.
    - Adverse effect: Myopathy.
  - Major tranquillisers
    - Therapeutic use: Treat psychoses.
    - Adverse effects: Ataxia, sedation, Parkinsonian tremor.
  - Drug-associated neurological symptoms
    - Headache → Nitrates, sildenafil.
    - Deafness → Aminoglycosides, aspirin, frusemide.
    - Peripheral neuropathy → Amiodarone, isoniazid, metronidazole.
    - Non-Parkinsonian tremor → Lithium, sodium valproate, bronchodilators, amphetamines.
    - Dysphagia → Bisphosphonates.
    - Confusion and memory loss → Major/minor tranquillisers, anticholinergics (e.g. amitriptyline).
    - Seizures → Pethidine.
