- Bedside manner and establishing rapport
    - Build trust through empathy, active listening and respectful communication.     
    - Practise empathy while maintaining professional objectivity.
    - Follow hospital rules regarding dress, identification and permission to see patients.
    - Maintain confidentiality; share identifiable information only when clinically appropriate and legally permitted.
    - Introduce yourself, state your role and explain the purpose of the consultation.
    - Ensure privacy by closing the door or drawing curtains.
    - Sit near the patient at eye level and avoid appearing rushed.
    - Address the patient respectfully by their preferred name/title; maintain eye contact and avoid focusing only on the computer.
    - Use appropriate small talk to ease anxiety without being patronising.
    - Avoid insensitive questioning or examination; follow the principle of “first, do no harm.”
## Obtaining the history 
- Start with an open-ended question; listen actively without interrupting.
- Allow the patient to describe the illness in their own words, then ask focused questions to clarify missing details.
- Explore symptom progression, previous similar episodes, functional limitations and impact on daily life.
- Explain note-taking; maintain eye contact and rapport while documenting.
- Avoid focusing entirely on the computer; prioritise patient engagement.
- Record an accurate, chronological account of the illness.
- Initial consultations require a comprehensive history; follow-up consultations focus on active problems and relevant changes.

- Introductory questions
    
    - Establish rapport; listen actively and observe non-verbal cues.
        
    - Begin with open-ended questions
        
        - “What has been troubling you recently?”
            
        - “When were you last feeling completely well?”
            
        - “What made you come to the hospital today?”
            
    - Follow-up consultation
        
        - “How have things been since your last visit?”
            
        - “What has happened since we last met?”
            
    - Encourage further information
        
        - “What else?”
            
        - Summarise the patient's story and invite them to continue.
            
    - Ask personal or sensitive questions when relevant and at an appropriate time.
        
    - Guide the conversation
        
        - Allow the initial story without interruption.
            
        - Gently redirect patients who go off-topic.
            
    - Ask focused questions to test diagnostic possibilities
        
        - Example: ask about exertional chest pain when assessing for angina.
            
        - Offer descriptive options if needed (e.g. sharp, dull, heavy, burning).
            
    - Clarify symptom descriptions
        
        - Ask the patient to point to the exact site and explain what they mean by terms such as “sharp” or “dull”.
            
        - Clarify medical terminology used by the patient rather than assuming its meaning.
            
    - Maintain an open mind
        
        - Avoid premature diagnostic conclusions before hearing the full history.
            
        - Recheck important details if the patient's description changes.
            
    - Use appropriate reassuring gestures without exaggeration.
        
    - Adapt the interview to communication difficulties
        
        - Recognise hearing, speech or memory problems and adjust your approach.


- Presenting (principal) symptom
    
    - Identify the symptom that prompted the consultation and the patient's main concern.
        
    - Prioritise potentially serious symptoms even if mentioned incidentally (e.g. haemoptysis).
        
    - Record each presenting symptom in the patient's own words; avoid medical terminology initially.
        
    - Explore four diagnostic dimensions
        
        - Anatomical diagnosis: Where is the problem?
            
        - Pathological diagnosis: What is the nature of the symptom?
            
        - Physiological/functional diagnosis: How does it affect the patient?
            
        - Aetiological diagnosis: Why did it develop?
            
    - Address the patient's main concern, provide appropriate reassurance and explain the likely diagnosis, prognosis and management.

- History of the presenting illness (HPI)
    
    - Allow the patient to describe the problem initially; then ask focused questions.
        
    - Document events in chronological order; organise separately by system if multiple systems are involved.
        
    - Character
        
        - Clarify exactly what the patient means by the symptom.
            
        - Dizziness: vertigo (spinning) vs presyncope (feeling faint).
            
        - Indigestion: pain, heartburn, postprandial fullness, excess wind or altered bowel habit.
            
        - Pain: sharp, dull, stabbing, boring, burning or cramping.
            
    - Radiation
        
        - Ask whether pain spreads elsewhere; specific patterns may suggest particular diagnoses.
            
    - Alleviating factors
        
        - Ask what relieves symptoms, including posture, food, medication or rest.
            
        - Document analgesics used and whether opioids were required.
            
    - Timing and onset
        
        - Establish exact onset, progression and previous similar episodes.
            
        - Ask when the patient last felt completely well.
            
        - For longstanding symptoms, ask why they sought medical attention now.
            
    - Exacerbating factors
        
        - Identify triggers such as movement, exertion, food or posture.
            
    - Severity and functional impact
        
        - Assess interference with daily activities and sleep.
            
        - Mild: noticeable but does not interfere with activities.
            
        - Moderate: cannot be ignored but does not limit daily activities.
            
        - Severe: interferes with daily activities.
            
        - Very severe: markedly limits most activities.
            
        - Use a 0–10 pain scale when appropriate; avoid distracting patients in severe pain.
            
        - Quantify functional capacity where relevant (e.g. walking distance before breathlessness).
            
        - Symptoms that seem mild to the patient may still indicate serious disease.

- Associated symptoms
    
    - Systematically ask about symptoms associated with the affected system.
        
    - Explore the presenting system first, then other relevant systems.
        
    - Diagnose using the combination of symptoms, not an isolated symptom.
        
- Effect of illness
    
    - Impact on work, education, daily activities and family.
        
    - Physical and psychological effects, including confidence and self-esteem.
        
    - Coping strategies and available family support.
        
    - Patient's concerns, expectations and hopes for recovery.
        
    - Previous explanations received, including information from the internet.
        
    - Explain the likely disease course, prognosis and treatment realistically and empathetically.
        
- Drug and treatment history
    
    - Current medications
        
        - Ask the patient to show all medicines, including packages or medication lists.
            
        - Record name, dose, indication, duration, adherence and side effects.
            
        - Verify whether medicines on old records are still being taken.
            
    - Specifically ask about
        
        - Antihypertensives, statins, diabetes medications, arthritis medications.
            
        - Antidepressants/anxiolytics, epilepsy medications, anticoagulants, antibiotics.
            
        - Contraceptive pills, hormone replacement therapy, erectile dysfunction medications.
            
        - Inhalers, patches, implants and injections.
            
        - OTC drugs (e.g. aspirin, antihistamines, vitamins).
            
    - Important medication risks
        
        - Aspirin and NSAIDs: gastrointestinal bleeding.
            
        - Opioids (e.g. codeine, morphine): dependence risk; document dose, duration and quantity.
            
        - Past corticosteroids, chemotherapy and radiotherapy.
            
    - Allergies and adverse reactions
        
        - Ask the suspected drug and exact reaction.
            
        - Differentiate true allergy from side effects.
            
    - Complementary and recreational substances
        
        - Ask about herbal/natural remedies and supplements; potential adverse effects or adulteration.
            
        - Ask about recreational drugs when relevant.
            
        - Intravenous drug use: needle sharing, blood-borne viruses and bacterial infections.
            
        - Cocaine: myocardial infarction risk.
            
        - Amphetamine-type drugs: dehydration, electrolyte disturbances and psychosis.
            
        - Suspected overdose: establish substance, quantity, timing and possible unknown ingredients.
            
    - Non-drug treatments
        
        - Physiotherapy, rehabilitation, dietary restrictions and supplements (e.g. pancreatic enzymes for chronic pancreatitis, gluten avoidance in coeliac disease).

- Past history
    
    - Introduce the section: “I need to ask about your past medical problems, as they may affect your current investigations and treatment.”
        
    - Previous illnesses and hospital admissions
        
        - Serious or chronic illnesses, childhood illnesses, previous investigations and treatments.
            
        - Previous surgeries, including bowel, cancer and cardiac/valve surgery.
            
        - Previous obstetric and gynaecological problems.
            
        - Blood transfusions: date and indication.
            
    - Implanted devices and investigation risks
        
        - Prostheses or implanted devices: assess infection risks and MRI compatibility.
            
        - Chronic kidney disease: consider risks associated with iodinated or gadolinium contrast.
            
        - Possible pregnancy: assess before radiation-based investigations and potentially harmful medications.
            
    - Chronic disease management
        
        - Treating doctors, specialist clinics and healthcare team involvement.
            
        - Current treatment, monitoring records and medication adjustments.
            
        - Understanding of the condition and confidence in managing treatment.
            
        - Knowledge of emergency action plans and when to seek help.
            
    - Immunisation history
        
        - Check whether recommended routine and risk-based vaccinations are up to date.
            
    - Other healthcare providers
        
        - Ask which other practitioners the patient sees.
            
        - Obtain consent before sharing reports with other practitioners.
            
    - Female-specific history (when relevant)
        
        - Menstrual history: age at menarche, last menstrual period (LMP), cycle regularity and menopause.
            
        - Relationship of symptoms to the menstrual cycle.
            
        - Possibility of pregnancy and history of miscarriages.
            
        - Record gravida (total pregnancies) and para (births beyond 20 weeks' gestation, as defined in the source).
