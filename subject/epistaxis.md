- Epistaxis
	- Majority arise from nasal septum
	- Most common site
		- Little’s area (anterior septum)

	- Less commonly associated with:
		- Turbinate injury
		- Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia
		- Blood dyscrasias
		- Anticoagulation therapy
		- Hypertension
		- Raised venous pressure
			- Mitral stenosis

- Blood supply of nasal septum
	- Internal carotid system
		- Anterior ethmoidal artery
		- Posterior ethmoidal artery

	- External carotid system
		- Greater palatine artery
		- Sphenopalatine artery
		- Superior labial artery

- General features
	- Often messy
	- Distressing if copious
	- Majority manageable in general practice
	- >80% are anterior bleeds
	- Posterior bleeds
		- Less common
		- Usually more severe
		- Best managed in equipped facility

- Initial management
	- Direct digital pressure
		- Pinch lower soft cartilaginous nose
		- Maintain for ~10 minutes

	- Patient position
		- Sitting upright
		- Leaning forward
		- Mouth breathing

	- Ice sucking may help

	- Purpose
		- Stem bleeding
		- Allow preparation for examination
		- Reduce mess/distress

- Persistent bleeding despite anterior pressure
	- Suggests posterior bleed

- Examination

	- Light source
		- Hands-free light preferred
			- Headlight
			- Mirrored head loop

	- Nasal speculum
		- Thudicum nasal speculum preferred

	- Alternative
		- Large otic speculum + otoscope
		- Careful insertion needed
			- Avoid re-triggering bleeding

	- Allows:
		- Visualisation of anterior septum
		- Identification of bleeding point
		- Cautery access

- Suction
	- Needed if bleeding uncontrolled
	- Preferred device
		- Angled Fraser sucker
			- 10–12 French gauge

	- Purpose
		- Clear anterior/middle nasal cavity

- Analgesia
	- Usually not required in anterior bleeding

	- Topical analgesia
		- Useful in posterior bleeding
		- Especially before packing

	- Sedation
		- May help if hypertension contributing

	- Narcotics used historically
		- Morphine
		- Pethidine

	- Avoid narcotics until bleeding controlled if possible

	- Sedation precautions
		- Maintain airway safety
		- Insert IV line early

- Local anaesthetic agents
	- Cophenylcaine forte
		- Xylocaine + decongestant

	- Xylocaine 10% + adrenaline
	- Xylocaine 10% spray
	- Xylocaine 2% + adrenaline injection
	- Cocaine 4% solution
	- Cocaine 25% paraffin ointment

- Equipment useful in epistaxis management
	- Jobson Horne wool carrier
	- Long-handled cotton buds/swabs
	- Silver nitrate applicator
	- Electrocautery device
	- Foley catheter
	- Sponge foam stay
	- Clamp
	- Nasal packing forceps
	- Angled Fraser sucker
	- Triamcinolone ointment ± antiseptic/antibiotic
		 - Reduces crusting after cautery
		 - Reduces recurrent bleeding during healing

- Cautery
	- Used for small anterior septal bleeds
	- Requires:
		- Adequate visualisation
		- Good lighting
		- Suction if needed

- Chronic epistaxis
	- Medical/surgical interventions trialled
	- Variable success
	- Use caution in general practice
- Anterior epistaxis
	- Most common type of epistaxis
	- >80% of all cases
	- Usually arises from:
		- Little’s area
		- Kiesselbach plexus

	- Often triggered by:
		- Mucosal infection

	- Common in children
		- Nose picking
		- Associated organisms
			- Staphylococci
			- Streptococci

- Conservative treatment
	- Weekly application
		- Triamcinolone 0.025% cream
			- Aristocort
			- Nemdyn
			- Nasalate

	- Nightly petroleum jelly
		- Small amount to septum before bedtime

- Chemical cautery
	- Silver nitrate sticks applied to Little’s area
	- Topical anaesthetic before procedure
		 - Spray or soaked swabs

- Chemical cautery technique
	- Lightly wipe silver nitrate over bleeding site
	- Endpoint
		- Grey-white discoloration

	- May trigger additional bleeding

	- Excess blood/chemical
		- Dab gently with cotton bud

	- Important precautions
		- Cauterise one side only
			- Prevent septal perforation

		- Silver nitrate may stain nostril skin
			- Can last ~3 weeks

		- Avoid nose blowing for several hours

- Electrocautery
	- Requires:
		- Topical anaesthesia
		- Local anaesthetic injection

	- Technique
		- Heated loop passed near vessels
		- Cautery by heat approximation
			- Not direct contact

	- Precautions
		- Protect anterior nares with speculum
			- Prevent burns/scarring

	- Aftercare
		- Topical antibiotic ointment
		- Petroleum jelly

- Other forms of bleeding
	- Bleeding point may not be visualised

	- Causes
		- Posterior bleeding
		- Nasal fractures
		- Systemic causes
		- Turbinate injury

	- Main initial treatment
		- Tamponade
			- Balloon catheter
			- Nasal packing

- Preparation for packing
	- Topical/local anaesthetic first

	- Patient positioning
		- Sitting forward
		- Holding kidney tray
		- Mouth breathing

	- Splash protection
		- Plastic gown
		- Goggles/glasses
		- Gloves

- Balloon catheters
	- Double balloon catheters
		- Easy insertion
		- Expensive

	- Technique
		- Insert fully
		- Inflate posterior balloon first
			- Occludes postnasopharyngeal space

		- Pull forward
		- Inflate anterior balloon
			- Compresses nasal cavity

	- Inflation medium
		- Normal saline preferred
			- Air may gradually deflate

	- Avoid overinflation
		- Pain
		- Balloon rupture
		- Pressure necrosis

- Nasal packing

	- Pack preparation
		- Six-foot Vaseline gauze
		- Mixed with:
			- Triamcinolone
			- Antibiotic cream
				- Kenacomb ointment

	- Alternatives
		- Paraffin + neotracin
		- BIPP paste

- Anaesthesia before packing
	- Cophenylcaine spray/swabs
	- Cocaine spray/swabs

	- Important
		- Severe blood loss + sedation
			- Risk hypovolaemic shock

- Packing technique
	- Foley catheter inserted
		 - Along floor of nasal cavity
		 - Under inferior turbinate
		 - Into nasopharynx

	- Confirm
		- Not in oropharynx

	- Inflate balloon
		- Up to 8 mL saline
		- Size should match choana

	- Apply gentle traction

	- Correct placement
		- No blood entering pharynx

	- Gauze packing
		- Insert inferior to catheter first
		- Subsequent loops parallel to floor of nose

	- Foam stay
		- Protects alar margin from erosion

	- Clamp
		- IV line clamp
		- Umbilical clamp

	- Tension
		- Enough to secure catheter
		- Avoid skin ischaemia

	- Pack duration
		- Leave 1–3 days
		- Catheter tension often released after 24 hours

- Continuing/severe bleeding
	- Requires specialist management
	- May require:
		- Resuscitation
		- IV tranexamic acid
		- Carotid ligation

	- Best managed in tertiary centre

- Key facts
	- >60% population experience epistaxis sometime in life
	- Environmental conditions influence frequency
	- Most cases manageable safely in general practice
