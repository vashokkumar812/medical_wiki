# Asthma in adults and adoloscents
- Asthma Phenotypes
	- Allergic asthma
	- Non-allergic asthma
	- Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease
	- Asthma with obesity
	- Adult-onset asthma
	- Cough-predominant asthma
	- Asthma with persistent airflow limitation
- Diagnosis
	- **No single conclusive diagnostic test**
	- based on cluster of symptoms + ix
- Features Supporting Asthma Diagnosis
	- More than one symptom present
		- Wheeze
		- Breathlessness
		- Chest tightness
		- Cough
	- Recurrent or seasonal symptoms
	- Symptoms worse:
		- At night
		- Early morning
	- Triggered by:
		- Exercise
		- Cold air
		- Irritants
		- Viral infections
		- Allergens
		- Aspirin
		- Beta blockers
	- Personal history of atopy
		- Allergic rhinitis
		- Atopic dermatitis
	- Family history of:
		- Asthma
		- Allergy
	- Widespread wheeze on auscultation
	- Reduced FEV1 or PEF without alternative explanation
	- Eosinophilia
	- Raised IgE
	- Rapid symptom relief with bronchodilator
		- SABA
		- ICS-formoterol
- Features Against Asthma Diagnosis
	- Dizziness
	- Light-headedness
	- Peripheral tingling
	- Isolated cough without other respiratory symptoms
	- Chronic sputum production
	- Change in voice
	- Heavy smoking history
	- Cardiovascular disease
	- Normal spirometry during symptoms despite repeated testing
	- Repeatedly normal chest examination when symptomatic
- Special Clinical Situations
	- Adolescents with exercise-related symptoms
		- Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction
		- Poor cardiopulmonary fitness
		- Vocal cord dysfunction
		- Hyperventilation
		- Dysfunctional breathing
		- Anxiety
	- Adolescents with uncertain diagnosis
		- specialist referral

	- Good control
		- Daytime symptoms ≤2 days/week
		- No activity limitation
		- No night symptoms
		- Reliever use ≤2 days/week
	- Poor control
		- Daytime symptoms >2 days/week
		- Any activity limitation
		- Any night symptoms
		- Reliever use >2 days/week

		- Smoking

		- Passive smoke exposure

# Selecting Inhalers for Adults and Adolescents

- Inhaler Types
	- Pressurised metered-dose inhaler (pMDI)
	- Breath-actuated pMDI
	- Dry powder inhaler (DPI)
		- Single-dose capsule DPI
		- Multi-dose DPI
		- Multidose reservoir DPI
	- Soft mist inhaler (SMI)
- Device Selection
	- Dexterity and Coordination
		- pMDI
			- Requires coordination of actuation and inhalation
			- Not required if spacer used
			- Requires shaking before use
			- Requires regular cleaning
		- Breath-actuated pMDI
			- No coordination required
		- DPI
			- No coordination required
			- Requires correct loading technique
			- Must not breathe into device
			- Single-dose capsule DPI
				- Capsule inserted before each dose
	- Inspiratory Flow Requirements
		- pMDI
			- Slow and steady inhalation
			- Does not require high inspiratory flow
			- Suitable during acute bronchoconstriction
			- Suitable for:
				- Frail adults
				- Patients with poor inspiratory effort
		- DPI
			- Quick and deep inhalation
			- Requires moderate-high inspiratory flow
			- May be unsuitable during acute bronchoconstriction
	- Airway Drug Deposition
		- pMDI
			- Significant oropharyngeal deposition without spacer
			- Spacer improves lung deposition
			- Slow inhalation over 3–5 seconds
		- SMI
			- Slow inhalation over 3–5 seconds
		- DPI
			- Full exhalation first
			- Forceful deep inhalation over 2–3 seconds
	- Cognitive Considerations
		- Prefer familiar device
		- Multiple inhaler types increase errors
		- Capsule DPIs carry risk of swallowing capsule
	- Environmental Impact
		- Lower carbon footprint:
			- DPI
			- SMI
		- Higher carbon footprint:
			- pMDI
	- Storage
		- Multidose reservoir DPI
			- More sensitive to humidity
- Inhaler Technique Training
	- Essential for all patients
	- Reading package leaflet alone is inadequate
	- Use:
		- Verbal instruction
		- Physical demonstration
		- Placebo inhaler
		- Written instructions
	- Assess technique against device-specific checklist
	- Reassess regularly
			- Even in long-term users
	- Re-demonstrate and correct errors
	- Provide written reminders
- Spacer Use
	- ICS delivered by pMDI
			- Spacer recommended
	- Benefits
			- Reduced oropharyngeal deposition
			- Increased lung deposition
			- Reduced oral candidiasis
			- Reduced dysphonia
	- Reliever delivered by pMDI
			- Spacer recommended
			- Essential when symptoms are frequent or worsening
- Spacer Technique
	- Preferred
			- Single-breath technique
				- One actuation at a time
				- Slow deep breath
				- Hold breath for 5 seconds
	- Acute asthma
			- Tidal breathing technique
				- Multiple breaths through spacer
				- May use up to 2 actuations
	- After discharge from ED
			- Return to single-breath technique
- Nebulisers
	- Do not routinely prescribe
	- Use only if necessary in:
			- Severe acute asthma
			- Life-threatening asthma
	- pMDI with spacer:
			- At least as effective
			- Faster delivery
	- Nebulisers may increase infection transmission
- Prescribing Principles
	- If separate ICS and reliever inhalers required
			- Use same inhaler type where possible
	- Ideally prescribe only one inhaler type
			- Reduces errors
			- Improves adherence
	- If multiple inhaler types unavoidable
			- Provide clear instructions for each device
			- Emphasise differences:
				- Shake vs do not shake
				- Slow inhalation vs quick inhalation
				- Clean device vs keep dry
- Ongoing Review
	- Assess inhaler technique at every review
	- Have patient physically demonstrate use
	- Correct errors immediately
	- Repeat training until technique correct
- Reducing ICS Adverse Effects
	- Rinse mouth after each ICS dose
			- Reduces oral candidiasis
			- Reduces dysphonia
- Special Situations
	- Unable to seal lips around mouthpiece
			- Use pMDI + spacer + adult mask
			- Preferable to nebuliser
# Asthma Self-Management Education
- Core Components
	- Provide asthma self-management education
	- Include:
		- Trigger avoidance and management
		- Self-monitoring of symptoms and risk
		- Correct inhaler technique
		- Written asthma action plan
		- Importance of regular asthma review
	- Benefits
		- Reduced hospitalisations
		- Reduced ED visits
		- Reduced urgent medical care
		- Reduced work and school absence
- Written Asthma Action Plan
	- Required for every adult and adolescent with asthma
	- Individualised according to:
		- Treatment regimen
		- Exacerbation risk
		- Language
		- Culture
		- Literacy level
		- Ability to self-manage
	- Must include:
		- Regular asthma medicines
		- Allergy medicines
		- Instructions for worsening symptoms
		- Reliever adjustment instructions
		- Oral corticosteroid instructions
		- Emergency instructions
		- When and how to seek medical care
		- Author of plan
		- Date
	- Review:
		- Annually
		- After major treatment changes
		- After significant change in asthma status
- Action Plan Review Checklist
	- Does patient know where plan is kept?
	- Has patient used it?
	- Can patient follow instructions easily?
	- Are medicines current?
	- Are instructions current?
	- Are emergency contacts current?
- Adjustment During Worsening Symptoms
	- AIR-only regimen
			- Continue usual budesonide-formoterol dose as needed
			- Repeat when symptoms recur
			- Seek medical care if daily maximum exceeded
			- Maintenance ICS increase occurs automatically through increased use
	- MART regimen
			- Continue usual ICS-formoterol dose as needed
			- Repeat when symptoms recur
			- Seek medical care if daily maximum exceeded
			- Maintenance ICS increase occurs automatically through increased use
	- Maintenance ICS or ICS-LABA + SABA reliever
			- Increase reliever dose during worsening symptoms
			- Continue usual maintenance dose
			- Do not increase maintenance ICS or ICS-LABA dose independently
- Oral Corticosteroids in Action Plans
	- Include clear instructions for when to start
	- Consider when:
			- Symptoms worsen over several days
			- Symptoms fail to improve
			- Reliever requirements increase significantly
	- Short courses do not require tapering
	- Avoid repeated unnecessary courses
			- Risk of cumulative adverse effects
- Emergency Advice
	- Seek urgent medical care immediately if:
			- Severe breathing difficulty
			- Symptoms worsen rapidly
			- Reliever ineffective
			- Difficulty speaking sentences
			- Blue lips
			- Drowsiness
	- Patient and family should:
			- Call ambulance
			- Commence asthma first aid
- Patient Education Principles
	- Encourage active self-management
	- Assess ability to self-manage
	- Tailor education to:
			- Age
			- Culture
			- Preferred language
			- Literacy level
			- Self-management capability
	- Reinforce key messages regularly
- Adolescents
	- Encourage independence in asthma management
	- Provide age-appropriate education
	- Consider:
			- Electronic action plans
			- Online resources
	- Repeat key information at each visit
	- Assess understanding directly
			- Do not assume good childhood management continues
	- Transition to adult respiratory services when appropriate
- Older Adults
	- Assess factors affecting self-management:
			- Visual impairment
			- Hearing impairment
			- Poor coordination
			- Osteoarthritis
			- Cognitive impairment
			- Mental health conditions
			- Comorbid disease
- Language and Accessibility
	- Provide information in preferred language where possible
	- Consider translated asthma action plans
	- Consider pictorial action plans for:
			- Poor literacy
			- Visual impairment
			- Difficulty using written plans
- Medication Access
	- Ensure patients have access to medicines required by action plan
	- Consider travel requirements
	- Prevent unnecessary oral corticosteroid overuse
	- Prescribe only quantity required for one course when appropriate
- Asthma Review Triggers
	- Arrange review if:
			- Daytime symptoms >2 days/week
			- Activity limitation
			- Night symptoms
			- Waking with asthma symptoms
			- Poor symptom control over several weeks
- Additional Plans
	- Patients with anaphylaxis risk
			- Provide written anaphylaxis action plan


# Initial asthma treatment for adults and adolescents after diagnosis


- Asthma treatment principles

	- Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) required for ALL adults/adolescents with asthma

	- Never use
		- Short-acting beta₂-agonist (SABA)-only treatment
		- Long-acting beta₂-agonist (LABA) monotherapy

	- Preferred delivery
		- Inhaler

	- Avoid
		- Nebulisers
			- Unless unavoidable
			- Severe acute asthma

- Initial treatment decision

	- Frequent symptoms OR
	- Previous severe asthma exacerbation OR
	- Risk factors for severe exacerbation

		→ Start maintenance inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing treatment

		- Preferred
			- Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)

		- Alternative
			- Maintenance inhaled corticosteroid + long-acting beta₂-agonist (ICS-LABA)
			- Plus short-acting beta₂-agonist (SABA) as needed

- Severe acute asthma at diagnosis

	- Start maintenance inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) immediately
		- Even if oral corticosteroids prescribed

	- Preferred
		- Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)

	- Benefits
		- Rapid symptom control
		- Reduced exacerbations
		- Dose adjusts to symptoms

- Inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol pathways

	- Anti-Inflammatory Reliever (AIR)

		- As-needed inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol only
		- No maintenance inhaled corticosteroid

		- Typical option
			- As-needed budesonide–formoterol

	- Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)

		- Same inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol inhaler used for:
			- Daily maintenance treatment
			- Symptom relief

		- Advantages
			- Better symptom control
			- Fewer exacerbations
			- Easy step-down

		- Step-down
			- MART
				→ AIR-only
			- No new inhaler required

- Reliever use

	- Carry inhaler at all times

	- Use
		- When symptoms occur
		- Before predictable triggers

			- Exercise
			- Allergen exposure

- Special presentations

	- First thunderstorm asthma

	- Drug-triggered bronchoconstriction
		- Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
		- Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease

- Inhaler management

	- Select device patient can use correctly

	- Teach inhaler technique

	- Recheck technique regularly

	- As-needed budesonide–formoterol only
		- Consider inhaler shelf-life
		- Reduce wastage

- AMC exam pearls

	- Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) for every asthma patient

	- Short-acting beta₂-agonist (SABA)-only therapy
		→ Not recommended

	- Long-acting beta₂-agonist (LABA) alone
		→ Contraindicated

	- Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART) preferred if
		- Frequent symptoms
		- Severe exacerbation history
		- High exacerbation risk

	- Anti-Inflammatory Reliever (AIR)
		= As-needed inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol only

	- Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)
		= Maintenance + reliever using same inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol inhaler

	- Inhalers preferred over nebulisers

## treatment level for adults and adoloscents
- Core principle
	- Every treatment level includes inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)
	- Never use short-acting beta₂-agonist (SABA) alone

- Recommended treatment ladder

| Level   | Treatment                                                         |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 | Anti-Inflammatory Reliever (AIR): as-needed budesonide-formoterol |
| Level 2 | Low-dose Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)                  |
| Level 3 | Medium-dose Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)               |
| Level 4 | Targeted intensive treatment                                      |

- Alternative treatment ladder

| Level   | Alternative                                 |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 | Low-dose ICS maintenance + PRN SABA         |
| Level 2 | Low-dose ICS-LABA maintenance + PRN SABA    |
| Level 3 | Medium-dose ICS-LABA maintenance + PRN SABA |

- Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)
	- Same ICS-formoterol inhaler used for
		- Maintenance
		- Symptom relief
- Step-down strategy
	- Medium-dose MART
		→ Low-dose MART
	- Low-dose MART
		→ AIR-only

	- ICS-formoterol reliever maintained throughout
		- Preserves exacerbation protection

- Level 4: Targeted intensive treatment

	- Used when symptoms/exacerbations persist despite medium-dose ICS-LABA

	- Usually specialist care

- Level 4 options

| Treatment                              | Role                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Monoclonal antibody (biologic) therapy | Phenotype-directed anti-inflammatory treatment                                            |
| High-dose ICS-LABA + PRN SABA          | Short-term trial while investigating uncontrolled asthma or awaiting biologic eligibility |
| ICS-LABA-LAMA + PRN SABA               | Trial if eosinophils/FeNO normal; long-term option if beneficial                          |
| Montelukast                            | Selected patients, especially aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease                     |
| Azithromycin                           | Add-on option for persistent exacerbations despite medium-dose ICS-LABA                   |

- Monoclonal antibodies (biologics)

	- Specialist prescribed

	- Target
		- Allergic asthma
		- Eosinophilic inflammation

	- Used in severe asthma

- High-dose ICS-LABA

	- Usually temporary
	- Duration
		- 3–6 months

	- Indications
		- Persistent symptoms
		- Frequent exacerbations
		- Awaiting biologic therapy

- ICS-LABA-LAMA

	- LAMA
		- Long-acting muscarinic antagonist

	- Consider if
		- Normal eosinophils
		- Normal fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)

	- Uses
		- Treatment trial
		- Long-term selected patients
		- Not eligible for biologics

- Montelukast

	- Limited role in severe asthma

	- Consider
		- Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease

	- Important adverse effect
		- Neuropsychiatric effects
			- Counsel patients/parents

- Azithromycin

	- Specialist-guided therapy

	- Consider if
		- Persistent exacerbations despite medium-dose ICS-LABA

	- Requires
		- Screening
		- Monitoring
		- Caution with adverse effects

- Why avoid repeated oral corticosteroids?

	- Increased risk of
		- Osteoporosis
		- Pneumonia
		- Cardiovascular disease
		- Cerebrovascular disease
		- Cataracts
		- Sleep apnoea
		- Renal impairment
		- Depression/anxiety
		- Type 2 diabetes
		- Weight gain
## treatment levels for adults and adoloscents


- Treatment adjustment
	- Step up one level
	- Step down one level
	- Consider switching from:
		- Inhaled corticosteroid + short-acting beta₂-agonist regimen
		- Inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist + short-acting beta₂-agonist regimen
	- To:
		- Inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol reliever regimen

- Before stepping up treatment
	- Check adherence
	- Check inhaler technique
	- Assess trigger exposure
	- Assess alternative/comorbid diagnoses
		- Allergic rhinitis
		- Rhinosinusitis
		- Obesity
		- Deconditioning
		- Heart disease
		- Inducible laryngeal obstruction

- Step-up pathway

	- Current: maintenance inhaled corticosteroid + as-needed short-acting beta₂-agonist
		→ Switch to as-needed budesonide–formoterol

	- Current: low-dose maintenance inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist + as-needed short-acting beta₂-agonist
		→ Switch to low-dose Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)

	- Current: medium-dose maintenance inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist + as-needed short-acting beta₂-agonist
		→ Switch to medium-dose Maintenance And Reliever Therapy (MART)

	- Asthma uncontrolled despite medium-dose MART
		- Confirm:
			- Good adherence
			- Correct inhaler technique
			- Trigger management
			- Comorbidity management

		- Check blood eosinophil count

		- Refer to specialist

		- If specialist delayed
			- Arrange spirometry

- Step-down criteria

	- Good control maintained
		- ≥2–3 months

	- Low exacerbation risk

- Do NOT step down if

	- Respiratory infection

	- Significant allergen exposure

	- Travelling

	- Pregnant

- Step-down pathway

	- Reduce treatment intensity by one level

	- Review after 4–8 weeks

	- Maintenance inhaled corticosteroid + as-needed short-acting beta₂-agonist
		- If well controlled and wishes to stop daily maintenance treatment
			→ Switch to as-needed budesonide–formoterol

	- Maintenance inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist + as-needed short-acting beta₂-agonist
		- Reduce inhaled corticosteroid dose gradually
		- Review every 4–8 weeks

- Inhaled corticosteroids

	- Do not withdraw completely

	- Minimum treatment
		- As-needed low-dose budesonide–formoterol

- Escalation within inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol regimens

	- As-needed budesonide–formoterol
		→ Add daily maintenance dose
		→ Low-dose MART

	- Low-dose MART
		→ Increase maintenance dose
		→ Medium-dose MART

	- Reliever doses remain low-dose

- Exacerbation management

	- Increase reliever use as needed if:
		- Symptoms more frequent
		- Symptoms recur after reliever
		- Symptoms not promptly relieved

	- Written asthma action plan should include:
		- Maximum single reliever dose
		- Maximum 24-hour reliever dose
		- When to seek medical care
		- When to call ambulance

- Oral corticosteroids

	- Indications
		- Symptoms recur repeatedly within 4 hours of reliever use
		- Symptoms fail to resolve promptly after reliever use

	- Principles
		- Short course only
		- Avoid unless clinically significant exacerbation

	- Prescribing
		- No repeat prescriptions
		- Prescribe exact tablet quantity required

- Pregnancy

	- Manage as for other adults

	- Aim
		- Maintain best possible control
		- Prevent exacerbations

	- Step up treatment if required

	- Do not step down during pregnancy
		- Unless dose clearly excessive

	- Counsel
		- Good control protects mother and fetus
		- Asthma control may change during pregnancy
		- Treatment adjustment may be required

- Important treatment precautions

	- Long-acting beta₂-agonists
		- Never use without inhaled corticosteroid

	- Inhaled corticosteroid adverse effects
		- Dysphonia
		- Oral candidiasis

	- Prevention
		- Spacer with pressurised metered-dose inhalers
		- Rinse mouth and spit after maintenance inhaled corticosteroid doses

	- Mouth rinsing generally unnecessary after occasional as-needed low-dose budesonide–formoterol

- Monitoring

	- Assess adherence non-judgementally

	- Check dispensing records if available

	- Review after treatment changes
		- 4–8 weeks

- Trigger point
	- Asthma not controlled on:
		- Medium-dose inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist (ICS-LABA)

		- Major life events

	- Obesity
	- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease
	- Obstructive sleep apnoea

- Step 5: Exclude alternative diagnoses

	- Anxiety disorders

	- Good adherence

	- Trigger management
	- Comorbidity management

	- Blood eosinophil count

		- Accredited respiratory laboratory

	- Assess suitability for biologic therapy

		→ Switch to medium-dose MART

	- Obtain blood eosinophil count

		- Cataracts
		- Diabetes

		- Blood glucose

---

	- Avoid maintenance oral corticosteroids

	- If frequent oral corticosteroid courses needed
			→ Urgent specialist referral

---

		- Longstanding asthma
		- Forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV₁) <80% predicted

		- Good adherence
		- Correct technique

---

		- Primary care

		- Nasal polyps

		- Oral corticosteroids

---

	- Specialist-directed add-on therapy

		- Drug interactions

# specialist assessment treatment for severe disease
![[Pasted image 20260605122703.png]]
- Severe asthma

	- Definition
		- Uncontrolled despite:
			- High-dose inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist (ICS-LABA)
			- Maintenance oral corticosteroids
		- OR requires these treatments to remain controlled

	- Uncontrolled asthma
		- Frequent symptoms
		- Activity limitation
		- Night waking
		- Exacerbations requiring systemic corticosteroids

	- Important
		- High-dose ICS and maintenance oral corticosteroids are not recommended long term

---

- Most patients with persistent symptoms are NOT severe asthma

	- Common causes of poor control
		- Poor adherence
		- Incorrect inhaler technique
		- Ongoing trigger exposure
			- Smoking
			- Environmental triggers
		- Untreated comorbidities
			- Chronic rhinosinusitis

	- Correcting these factors often improves control

---

- Type 2 airway inflammation

	- Mechanism
		- Eosinophilic inflammation
		- Mucus hypersecretion
		- Airway hyperresponsiveness

	- Most asthma
		- Type 2 inflammation present
		- Usually responds to low-dose inhaled corticosteroids

	- Severe asthma subset
		- Persistent Type 2 inflammation despite:
			- High-dose inhaled corticosteroids
			- Oral corticosteroids

	- Biomarkers
		- Elevated blood eosinophils
		- Elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)
		- Elevated total immunoglobulin E (IgE) ± atopy

	- Clinical significance
		- Strong predictor of severe exacerbations

	- Highest-risk profile
		- Uncontrolled on medium/high-dose ICS-LABA
		- Previous-year exacerbations
		- Elevated eosinophils
		- Elevated FeNO

---

- Specialist investigations

	- Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)
		- ≥20 ppb despite high-dose ICS/oral corticosteroids
			- Refractory Type 2 inflammation

	- Blood eosinophils
		- ≥150 cells/µL
			- ICS-refractory Type 2 inflammation
		- ≥1500 cells/µL
			- Consider:
				- Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)
				- Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA)

	- Sputum eosinophils
		- ≥2%
			- Refractory Type 2 inflammation

	- Additional tests
		- Full blood count
		- C-reactive protein
		- Total IgE
		- IgG
		- IgA
		- IgM
		- Fungal precipitins (e.g. Aspergillus)

	- Allergy assessment
		- Skin prick testing
		- Allergen-specific IgE

	- Imaging
		- Chest X-ray
		- High-resolution chest CT
		- CT sinuses

---

- Monoclonal antibody (biologic) therapy

	- First-choice treatment
		- Severe allergic asthma
		- Severe eosinophilic asthma
		- Persistent Type 2 inflammation despite ICS-LABA

	- Benefits
		- Improves symptoms
		- Reduces exacerbations
		- Reduces oral corticosteroid use

	- Refer early
		- Asthma uncontrolled on medium-dose ICS-LABA despite:
			- Good adherence
			- Correct inhaler technique
			- Management of triggers/comorbidities

---

- Available biologics

	- Benralizumab
		- Anti–IL-5 receptor α
		- Severe eosinophilic asthma

	- Mepolizumab
		- Anti–IL-5
		- Severe eosinophilic asthma

	- Dupilumab
		- Anti–IL-4 receptor α
		- Type 2 inflammatory asthma
			- Elevated eosinophils and/or elevated FeNO

	- Omalizumab
		- Anti-IgE
		- Moderate–severe allergic asthma

---

- PBS requirements

	- Specialist care required

	- Uncontrolled despite optimised treatment

	- Phenotype criteria

		- Allergic asthma
			- Omalizumab
			- Dupilumab

		- Eosinophilic asthma
			- Benralizumab
			- Mepolizumab
			- Dupilumab

---

- Other specialist severe asthma treatments

	- Azithromycin

	- Breathing exercises

	- Bronchial thermoplasty

			- Early summer

			- Avoid outdoor exposure when thunderstorms approach

		- Stay indoors
			- Windows closed
			- Air-conditioning off or recirculation mode

			- Windows closed

			- Updated asthma action plan available

			- Rhinorrhoea

## acute excerbation of astham in adults


- Acute asthma (primary care)

	- Immediate assessment

		- Rapid severity assessment
			- Clinical observation
			- Pulse oximetry

	- Severe/life-threatening asthma

		- Call ambulance immediately

		- Start:
			- Bronchodilator
			- Oxygen (if indicated)

		- Transfer to acute care if:
			- Unable to speak in full sentences
			- Oxygen saturation ≤94%

---

- Initial treatment

	- Salbutamol
		- Pressurised metered-dose inhaler (pMDI) + spacer
		- Tidal breathing method

		- Mild–moderate exacerbation
			- 4–12 actuations

		- Severe exacerbation
			- 12 actuations

		- Repeat
			- Every 20 minutes during first hour if symptoms persist

	- Nebuliser
		- Avoid if possible

		- Severe exacerbation
			- 5 mg

		- Life-threatening exacerbation
			- 10 mg

		- Follow aerosol infection-control precautions

---

- Oxygen

	- If oxygen saturation <92%
		- Start supplemental oxygen

	- Target saturation

		- Most patients
			- 93–95%

		- Hypercapnia risk
			- 88–92%

	- Avoid over-oxygenation
		- SpO₂ >95%

	- Hypercapnia risk factors
		- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
		- Obesity hypoventilation syndrome
		- Bronchiectasis
		- Cystic fibrosis
		- Neuromuscular disease
		- Chest wall deformity

---

- Reassessment

	- If dyspnoea/work of breathing persists after 5 minutes
		- Repeat bronchodilator
		- Arrange emergency department transfer

---

- Complete assessment (after initial treatment)

	- Examination
		- Vital signs
		- Auscultation
		- Ongoing pulse oximetry

	- Brief history

		- Reliever use before presentation
			- Dose
			- Number of doses
			- Time of last dose

		- Oral corticosteroid use

		- Current asthma medications
			- Regular
			- As-needed
			- Device type

		- Adherence to preventer therapy

		- Trigger assessment
			- Allergy
			- Hypersensitivity
			- Medication
			- Respiratory infection

		- Comorbid disease
			- COPD
			- Cardiac disease
			- Other lung disease

		- Smoking/vaping exposure

	- Important
		- Food allergy rarely triggers acute asthma
		- Confirmed food allergy increases risk of fatal/life-threatening asthma

---

- Systemic corticosteroids

	- Start in all acute asthma episodes
		- Unless contraindicated

	- Prednisone/prednisolone
			- Short course

---

- Observation

	- After symptoms stabilise
		- Observe ≥4 hours

	- If symptoms recur during observation
		- Give salbutamol
		- Call ambulance

---

- Discharge / follow-up

	- Follow-up
		- Within 3–5 days

	- Comprehensive asthma review
		- At 2 weeks

	- Provide interim asthma action plan

	- Written instructions

		- Continue/start maintenance inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist (ICS-LABA)
			- At least medium-dose ICS

		- Reliever
			- Use as needed
			- Not scheduled

		- Oral corticosteroid
			- Next dose
			- Duration

		- Recurrence plan
			- When to attend emergency department
			- When to call ambulance

---

- Anaphylaxis

	- If suspected/confirmed
		- Intramuscular adrenaline

	- >12 years or >50 kg
		- 0.01 mg/kg
		- Maximum 0.5 mg
		- Lateral mid-thigh

---

- Peri-arrest / unable to use inhaler

	- Consider intramuscular adrenaline
		- Auto-injector 300 micrograms

---

- Inhaler technique during acute asthma

	- Preferred
		- Single-breath technique

			- One actuation at a time

			- Slow deep inhalation

			- Hold breath 5 seconds

	- If unable to coordinate
		- Tidal breathing technique

			- Up to 2 actuations into spacer

			- Multiple breaths through spacer
				- Aim 4 breaths

	- After recovery
		- Return to single-breath technique

---

- Pregnancy

	- Manage acute asthma as in other adults

	- Treat promptly
		- Protect mother
		- Protect fetus

	- Reassess after every bronchodilator dose

		- Reassess at 15 minutes
		- Consider repeat bronchodilator

			- Inline pMDI delivery
			- Nebuliser within ventilator circuit

		- Mechanical ventilation

	- Avoid
		- Sedatives
		- Oral salbutamol
		- Intravenous salbutamol

	- Life-threatening asthma
		- Blood gas analysis

	- Monitor for salbutamol toxicity
		- Tachycardia
		- Metabolic acidosis
		- Hypokalaemia

---

- Observation

	- After symptom stabilisation
		- Observe ≥4 hours

	- Repeat spirometry before discharge

---

	- Consider admission if:

		- Hypoxia at presentation

		- Forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV₁) <60% predicted
			- Or <50% usual value

		- Persistent respiratory distress after 1–2 hours

		- Unable to lie flat due to dyspnoea

		- Previous intensive care unit (ICU) admission for asthma

		- Acute asthma presentation within previous 4 weeks

		- Frequent exacerbation presentations

		- High recent beta₂-agonist use

		- Inadequate home monitoring/support

		- Unable to return easily if deteriorates

		- Other risk factors for adverse outcomes

---

- Discharge treatment

	- Ensure inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing regimen

	- Complete oral corticosteroid course

	- Preferred discharge regimens

		- Inhaled corticosteroid–formoterol
			- Maintenance + reliever therapy

		OR

		- Inhaled corticosteroid–long-acting beta₂-agonist (ICS-LABA)
			- Maintenance
			- Short-acting beta₂-agonist (SABA) reliever

	- If previously on maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART)

		- Discharge on medium-dose MART

		- Do not switch reliever to salbutamol

---

- Discharge plan

	- Interim asthma action plan

		- Oral corticosteroid instructions

		- Maintenance inhaler instructions

		- Reliever instructions

		- Worsening symptom plan

			- Contact general practitioner

			- Return to ED

			- Call ambulance

	- Written recurrence instructions

---

- Follow-up

	- General practitioner review
			- Within 3 days

	- Comprehensive review
			- 2–4 weeks
			- General practitioner or respiratory physician

---

- Special situations

	- Anaphylaxis

		- Intramuscular adrenaline auto-injector

	- Pregnancy

		- Treat as other adults

		- Prompt treatment essential

		- Oral corticosteroids if indicated

	- Recurrent exacerbations

		- Consider respiratory physician referral

		- Especially:
			- Multiple presentations
			- Multiple oral corticosteroid courses

- **Definition**
	- Asthma
		- Chronic inflammatory airway disease
		- Reversible airway obstruction
		- Bronchospasm
		- Airway hyperresponsiveness

- **Epidemiology**
	- Respiratory viral infections = most common trigger
	- Atopy commonly coexists
	- Children <12 months with wheeze
		- More likely bronchiolitis
		- Usually do not respond to bronchodilators

- **AMC High-Yield Principles**
	- If unsure between asthma and anaphylaxis
		- Treat as anaphylaxis first
	- Salbutamol (SABA) = cornerstone of acute treatment
	- Steroids should be given early in:
		- Moderate asthma
		- Severe asthma
		- Life-threatening asthma
	- MDI + spacer preferred over nebuliser
		- Faster delivery
		- Similar efficacy
		- Fewer side effects
	- Silent chest = life-threatening sign

- **Triggers**
	- Viral upper respiratory tract infection
	- Exercise
	- Cold air
	- Passive smoking
	- Aeroallergens
	- Dust mites
	- Pollens
	- Animal dander

- **Risk Factors for Severe Asthma**
	- Previous ICU admission
	- Previous intubation
	- Previous IV asthma treatment
	- ≥2 hospital presentations
	- Poor preventer adherence
	- Excess reliever use
		- SABA >2 times/week
		- >3 inhalers/year
	- Coexisting anaphylaxis
	- Allergic rhinitis

- **History**
	- Duration of symptoms
	- Trigger factors
	- Previous asthma admissions
	- Previous ICU/intubation
	- Current reliever use
	- Preventer use
		- Adherence
		- Technique
	- Nocturnal symptoms
	- Exercise-induced symptoms
	- Morning cough
	- School attendance impact
	- Physical activity limitation

- **Features Suggesting Anaphylaxis**
	- Sudden onset after:
		- Food exposure
		- Medication exposure
		- Insect sting
	- Associated:
		- Urticaria
		- Angioedema
		- Hypotension
		- Airway swelling

- **Examination**
	- Assess severity using:
		- General appearance
		- Mental status
		- Activity level
		- Work of breathing
	- Assess:
		- Respiratory rate
		- Accessory muscle use
		- Retractions
		- Air entry

- **Signs Not Reliable for Severity Assessment**
	- Wheeze intensity
	- Pulsus paradoxus
	- Peak expiratory flow rate

- **Important Clinical Signs**
	- Silent chest
		- Imminent respiratory collapse
	- Tachycardia
		- May indicate severity
		- May be salbutamol side effect
	- Persistent unilateral findings
		- Consider foreign body
		- Consider pneumothorax

- **Red Flags for Alternative Diagnoses**
	- Productive cough
	- Isolated cough
	- Paraesthesia
	- Chest pain
	- Clubbing
	- Persistent asymmetry

- **Severity Classification**

| Severity | Clinical Features |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Mild** | Mild ↑ work of breathing, normal RR, alert, active |
| **Moderate** | Moderate ↑ work of breathing, ↑ RR, alert and active |
| **Severe** | Marked ↑ work of breathing, ↑ RR, agitation, pallor |
| **Life-threatening** | Maximal work of breathing, confusion, drowsiness, cyanosis, exhaustion, silent chest |

- **Investigations**
	- Usually not required
	- Chest X-ray
		- Not routinely indicated
	- Blood tests
		- Rarely required
	- Blood gas
		- Usually unnecessary
		- Clinical assessment more important
	- Serum potassium
		- Consider after prolonged salbutamol therapy

- **Mild Asthma Treatment**
	- Salbutamol MDI + spacer
	- Observe response
	- Consider oral steroids if worsening

- **Moderate Asthma Treatment**
	- Salbutamol MDI + spacer
	- Oral corticosteroids early
			- Prednisolone
			- Dexamethasone
	- Reassess frequently

- **Severe Asthma Treatment**
	- Oxygen if required
	- Salbutamol MDI + spacer frequently
	- Ipratropium bromide
	- Systemic corticosteroids
	- Early paediatric consultation

- **Life-Threatening Asthma Treatment**
	- Immediate senior assistance
	- Oxygen
	- Continuous bronchodilator therapy
	- Ipratropium bromide
	- IV magnesium sulfate
	- Consider IM adrenaline
		- Especially poor respiratory effort
	- ICU/PICU involvement

- **Medications**

	- **Salbutamol**
		- SABA
		- Standard MDI:
			- 100 mcg/puff

	- **Ipratropium Bromide**
		- Anticholinergic
		- Standard MDI:
			- 21 mcg/puff

	- **Dexamethasone**
		- Less vomiting than prednisolone
		- Longer half-life

- **IV Magnesium Sulfate**
	- Dose
		- 50 mg/kg IV
		- 0.2 mmol/kg
		- 0.1 mL/kg of 50% solution
	- Maximum
		- 8 mmol
	- Used in:
		- Severe asthma
		- Life-threatening asthma

- **Salbutamol Toxicity**
	- Clinical features
		- Tachycardia
		- Tachypnoea
		- Fine tremor
	- Biochemical abnormalities
		- Hypokalaemia
		- Hyperlactataemia
		- Metabolic acidosis
	- Improves when salbutamol reduced

- **Consult Paediatrics**
	- Moderate asthma
	- Severe asthma
	- Life-threatening asthma
	- Oxygen requirement
	- Poor salbutamol response

- **Transfer Indications**
	- IV treatment required
	- Respiratory support required
	- Escalating oxygen needs
	- Failure to improve
	- Need for higher-level care

- **Discharge Criteria**
	- Resolution of signs of severity
	- Normal activity level
	- Adequate oral intake
	- Clinically stable
	- Able to tolerate:
		- 3 hours without salbutamol
	- Mild hypoxia (SpO₂ 90–94%)
		- Does not preclude discharge if otherwise well

- **Discharge Planning**
	- Asthma education
		- Symptom recognition
		- Emergency management
		- Inhaler technique
		- Reliever use
		- Preventer use
	- Updated Asthma Action Plan
	- GP/specialist follow-up
	- Manage associated atopic disease
		- Allergic rhinitis
		- Anaphylaxis

- **AMC High-Yield Pearls**
	- Treat possible anaphylaxis before asthma if uncertain
	- MDI + spacer preferred to nebuliser
	- Steroids early in moderate, severe and life-threatening asthma
	- Silent chest = impending respiratory failure
	- Chest X-ray not routinely required
	- Blood gases rarely needed
	- Salbutamol = first-line acute therapy
	- Add ipratropium in severe asthma
	- IV magnesium sulfate for severe/life-threatening asthma
	- Salbutamol toxicity causes hypokalaemia and lactic acidosis
	- Previous ICU admission/intubation = major risk factor for severe asthma
	- Children <12 months with wheeze are more likely to have bronchiolitis
