## Key points
- Consider a serious eye injury when a child presents with a painful eye or blurred vision
- Adequate analgesia will aid examination and procedural sedation may be required in the younger child
- Seek senior clinician or ophthalmology assistance early if eye assessment is proving difficult in the distressed child
- Chemical burns, especially alkali, need immediate irrigation
- If a penetrating eye injury is identified or suspected, stop examination, place an eye shield over the eye, keep nil by mouth and urgently refer to ophthalmology, see Penetrating eye injury



- conditions that threaten vision:
	- Ruptured globe
	- Retrobulbar haematoma
	- Foreign body, either intraocular or corneal
	- Large hyphaemas ( ==> causing acute glaucoma)
	- Retinal detachment
	- Corneal burns, either chemical or thermal  
	  - *alkalis penetrate deeper and have greater potential for serious injury and delayed burns*
	- Contact lens-related corneal infections (bacterial keratitis), see Acute red eye
- what to do
	- Primary survey
	- Ensure structural integrity of the globe
	- Assess vision in the injured and uninjured eye
	- Seek ophthalmology assessment where necessary


-  Eye examination
	- Topical anaesthetic 
		- tetracaine (amethocaine) 0.5% or 1% 
		- One drop into the affected eye
		-  20 seconds to 20 minutes (longer with 1%)  
		- direct epithelial toxicity of anesthesia => delay corneal healing ==> Avoid repeated administration of anaesthetic eye drops 


- Assess for globe rupture or laceration:
	- Severe loss of vision or loss of red reflex
	- Loss of ocular motility
	- Asymmetric, irregular pupil
	- Hyphaema
	- Distorted appearance of globe
	- Localised conjunctival haemorrhage or chemosis (bulging of the conjunctiva)

- management
	- If a penetrating eye injury is identified or suspected:
		- Stop examination
		- Place an eye shield over the eye
		- Keep nil by mouth
		- Urgently refer to ophthalmology  
	- Retrobulbar haematoma
		- Occurs due to blunt trauma leading to compartment syndrome and optic nerve compression
		- Tense proptosis, limited eye movements, decreased visual acuity and Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect (RAPD)
		- Emergent ophthalmology referral, may require urgent decompression

## Orbital blow out fracture
- Occurs due to high energy blunt trauma that increases intra-orbital pressure
- Symptoms: pain and diplopia (particularly on upward gaze), tenderness, eyelid swelling, nausea, vomiting
- Signs: ptosis, tenderness, relative enophthalmos, decreased cheek sensation, lower lid crepitus
- Order CT of the orbit and refer to ophthalmology

## Corneal abrasions and foreign bodies
Seek ophthalmology advice and removal of:
- Embedded or metallic foreign bodies (avoid removing)
- Large, deep, or central corneal foreign bodies

If large (>2 mm) or deep defect seen → presume full thickness abrasion → manage as penetrating eye injury

Steps:
- Anaesthetise conjunctiva with one drop tetracaine (amethocaine) 1% or oxybuprocaine 0.4%
- Examine surface + under lids (evert eyelid); use slit lamp if possible
- Stain with fluorescein  
  - Apply 1 drop or  
  - Wet strip with saline and touch to lower lid only
- Remove small FB with moistened cotton bud  
  - For older cooperative children: 25G needle bevel up using slit lamp, approaching from temporal side

Treatment:
- Larger defects (>2 mm): chloramphenicol 1% ointment + double eye pad overnight  
- Smaller defects (<2 mm): chloramphenicol 0.5% drops QID for 5–7 days

Follow-up:
- Daily review (VA + slit lamp) until epithelium healed  
- Persistent pain >48 hrs or no improvement → ophthalmology review

## Chemical (strong acid/alkali) burns
**Medical emergency — urgent irrigation required**

- Urgent, copious irrigation after topical anaesthetic  
- Remove particulate alkaline matter completely with cotton bud  
- Use 3 L of 0.9% saline through open giving set over ~15 minutes  
- Irrigate from 3–5 cm above cornea; ask child to look in all directions  
- Continue until pH normal (6–8) measured with indicator paper in conjunctival fornix  
- Discuss all burns with ophthalmology

## Thermal burns
- First aid and resuscitation take priority  
- Local anaesthetic + copious irrigation (same as chemical burns)  
- Discuss all thermal burns with ophthalmology  

---

## Lid laceration
- Treat as potential penetrating injury until proven otherwise  
- Superficial away from lid margin → may be closed with standard suturing  
- Any laceration involving lid margin → ophthalmologist repair required  

---

## Contact lens associated red eye
- Anaesthetise eye and remove contact lenses if possible
- Stain eye and check for corneal abrasions or FB
- Swab if discharge present  
  - Gram-negatives (esp. Pseudomonas aeruginosa) common → need broad antibiotics
- If corneal ulcer identified → urgent ophthalmology referral
- Start chloramphenicol (1% ointment or 0.5% drops) and arrange follow-up

---

## Subconjunctival haemorrhage
- If localised → may suggest penetrating injury (though also may be Valsalva)  
- If cannot visualise posterior extent → consider orbital or base of skull fracture  
- In infants → may indicate non-accidental injury → see Child abuse

# Consider consultation with local paediatric team when
A child with:
- Suspected child abuse or neglect  
- Significant associated trauma  
- Medical instability  

---

# Consider consultation with ophthalmology when
A child with:
- A sight threatening condition  
- Large corneal foreign body or difficult FB  
- Need for complete exam in distressed child  
- Need retinal assessment/photography in suspected child abuse  

---

# Consider transfer when
- Sight threatening condition and management exceeds local capacity  
- Significant other major injuries requiring tertiary care  

For emergency advice and paediatric/neonatal ICU transfers → see Retrieval Services

---

# Consider discharge when
- Normal eye examination and nil other symptoms  
**OR**
- Sight threatening conditions excluded **AND**  
- Appropriate treatment for minor injuries started **AND**  
- Follow-up or early review criteria provided to family
