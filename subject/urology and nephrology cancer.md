# bladder cancer
- Epidemiology
	- Most common in
		- 5th–7th decade
	- More common in men
	- Most tumours at diagnosis
		- Nonmuscle invasive

- clinical features
	- Haematuria
		- Microscopic
		- Macroscopic
		- May be intermittent or persistent
		- Cancer rates
			- ~25% in macroscopic haematuria
			- ~9.4% in microscopic haematuria
	- Other urinary symptoms
		- Frequency
		- Urgency
		- Dysuria
	- Advanced disease
		- Ureteric obstruction
		- Hydronephrosis
		- Deteriorating renal function
- Risk factors
	- Tobacco smoking
		- Risk related to duration + quantity
		- Risk gradually decreases after cessation
	- Aromatic amines
	- Benzene derivatives
	- Aniline dyes
	- Drugs
		- Cyclophosphamide
		- Phenacetin
		- Arsenic
	- Previous external beam radiotherapy
		- Gynaecological cancer
		- Prostate cancer
	- Chronic urothelial irritation
		- UTI
		- Kidney stones
		- Parasitic infection
	- Chronic irritation may lead to SCC

	- Not implicated
		- Caffeine
		- Artificial sweeteners
		- Dietary factors

- Histological classification
	- Papilloma
		- Essentially benign
		- Does not recur

	- Papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP)
		- Very low malignant transformation risk
		- May recur
		- Requires surveillance

	- True malignancies
		- Low-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma
		- High-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma
		- Carcinoma in situ (CIS)

	- Carcinoma in situ
		- Confined by basement membrane
		- Technically noninvasive
		- Poorly differentiated
		- Aggressive
		- Associated with muscle-invasive disease
		- May progress to muscle invasion

- Staging
	- Based on TNM classification
		- Tumour
		- Nodes
		- Metastasis

- Nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer
	- Includes
			- Ta
			- T1
			- CIS
	- Previously called
			- Superficial bladder cancer
	- Heterogeneous prognosis
	- Risk stratification
			- Low risk
			- Intermediate risk
			- High risk

	- Five-year recurrence rates
			- Low risk
					- ~31%
			- Intermediate risk
					- ~62%
			- High risk
					- ~78%

	- Progression to muscle invasion
			- Up to 48% in T1 tumours

- Histological types
	- Most common
			- Urothelial carcinoma
					- Previously transitional cell carcinoma (TCC)

	- Other primary bladder cancers
			- Squamous cell carcinoma
			- Adenocarcinoma
			- Neuroendocrine tumours
			- Mesenchymal tumours

- Diagnosis and evaluation
	- All macroscopic haematuria requires evaluation

	- Evaluation includes
			- Urine cytology
			- Cystoscopy
			- Upper tract imaging

	- Aim
			- Detect urothelial carcinoma

- Benign causes of haematuria
	- UTI
	- Foreign bodies
			- Bladder calculi
			- Ureteric calculi
			- Renal calculi
			- Ureteric stents
	- Benign prostatic enlargement

- Microscopic haematuria
	- Defined as
			- 2–3 RBC/high-power field

	- Confirmation required
			- Repeat urine microscopy
			- ≥2 of 3 positive samples

- High-risk microscopic haematuria
	- Requires full evaluation if no primary renal explanation

	- High-risk features
			- Age >40 years
			- Smoking history
			- Chemical exposure
			- Irritative voiding symptoms

- Low-risk microscopic haematuria
	- Persistent haematuria
			- Requires imaging
			- Plus either
					- Cytology
					- Cystoscopy

	- If initial investigations negative
			- May monitor
			- However medicolegal concerns may lower threshold for further investigation

- Urine cytology
	- Technique
		- Voided urine samples
		- Collected on 3 separate days
		- Use fresh urine

	- Avoid
		- Frankly bloody urine
		- First morning urine sample

	- Diagnostic performance
		- Specificity may reach ~90%
			- With experienced cytopathologist

	- Positive cytology
		- Highly suggestive of
			- High-grade urothelial carcinoma
			- Carcinoma in situ (CIS)

	- Low-grade urothelial carcinoma
		- Lower likelihood of positive cytology

	- Positive/atypical cytology
		- May indicate urothelial carcinoma anywhere in urinary tract
		- Requires full evaluation

	- Negative cytology
		- Does not exclude bladder cancer
		- High-risk patients still require full evaluation

- Imaging studies
	- Purpose
		- Assess renal parenchyma
		- Assess upper tract urothelium

	- First-line imaging
		- Three-phase CT urography

	- Advantages of CT urography
		- Delayed contrast phase outlines
			- Renal collecting system
			- Ureters
			- Bladder
		- Detects
			- Tumour size
			- Lymphadenopathy

- MRI
	- Second-line investigation
	- Expensive
	- Indications
			- Contrast allergy
			- Detailed pelvic soft tissue assessment
			- Pregnancy

	- Limitation
			- Renal impairment limits contrast use

- Ultrasonography
	- Sometimes used in low-risk patients
	- Limitation
			- Poor urothelial detail

- Bone scan
	- Nuclear whole-body bone scan

	- Indications
		- Large muscle-invasive bladder tumour
		- Bone pain
		- Bony lesions on CT

	- Purpose
			- Detect skeletal metastases

- Positron emission tomography (PET)
	- Second-line investigation
	- Used to further characterise
			- CT lesions
			- Bone scan lesions


- Treatment of bladder cancer

- Cystoscopy
	- Indications
		- Radiological evidence of bladder lesion
		- Positive urine cytology

	- Procedure
		- Cystoscopy + biopsy + resection
		- Performed under general anaesthesia

	- Flexible cystoscopy
		- Not required before definitive cystoscopy if lesion already identified radiologically/cytologically

- Positive cytology with no visible bladder lesion
	- Evaluate upper urinary tract during cystoscopy
		- Retrograde pyelograms
		- Selective urine cytology from each kidney
		- Flexible ureteroscopy if indicated

- Normal imaging + negative cytology
	- Flexible cystoscopy under local anaesthesia reasonable

	- If abnormal findings
		- Proceed to
			- Biopsy
			- TUR under general anaesthesia

- Transurethral resection (TUR)
	- Goals
		- Remove all visible tumours
		- Obtain staging information

	- Important principle
		- Resection into detrusor muscle essential
			- Determines muscle invasion

	- Erythematous areas
		- May represent
			- CIS
			- Dysplasia
		- Should be biopsied

	- Importance of staging
		- Different treatment for
			- T1 disease
			- T2 disease

- Immediate postoperative intravesical chemotherapy
	- Single instillation after TUR
	- Reduces recurrence by ~12%
	- Minimal side effects

- Adjuvant treatment for nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer
	- High-grade NMIBC and CIS
		- Intravesical BCG immunotherapy

	- Benefits of BCG
		- Prevents recurrence
		- Delays recurrence/progression

	- Regimen
		- Weekly instillations
		- For 6 weeks
		- Begin after bladder healing

	- Follow-up
		- Repeat cystoscopy
			- 6 weeks after final instillation

	- Maintenance BCG
		- Required for maximal benefit
		- Optimal schedule unclear

- Low-grade nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer
	- BCG not recommended
			- Poor response

	- Recurrent low-grade tumours
		- Intravesical mitomycin C

- Cystectomy for muscle-invasive disease and BCG failure
	- Indications
		- T2 tumours
		- BCG-refractory NMIBC

	- Procedure
		- Cystectomy
		- Urinary diversion
		- Lymphadenectomy

	- Lymphadenectomy
		- Small survival advantage

- Urinary diversion types
	- Orthotopic neobladder
	- Ileal conduit

	- Diversion choice depends on
		- Patient preference
		- Manual dexterity
		- Renal function
		- Hepatic function
		- Body habitus
		- Age
		- Previous surgery
		- Previous radiotherapy

- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy
	- Used for large tumours
	- Benefits
		- ~5% survival benefit at 5 years
		- May shrink tumour
		- May convert unresectable → resectable

	- Limitation
		- Delays cystectomy
		- Nonresponders may have worse survival

- Adjuvant chemotherapy
	- Indications
		- Positive lymph nodes after cystectomy
		- pT3/pT4 disease

	- Prognostic factors
		- Tumour stage
		- Nodal involvement

	- Positive lymph nodes
		- 5-year survival may be as low as ~33%

- Palliative management
	- Palliative cystectomy/urinary diversion
			- For symptom control only

	- Suitable indications
		- Intractable pain
		- Severe haematuria requiring transfusions
		- Bilateral ureteric obstruction

	- Palliative ileal conduit
		- May leave bladder in situ

	- Palliative radiotherapy
		- May relieve symptoms

	- Metastatic disease
		- Cystectomy not curative

- Radiotherapy
	- Alternative to cystectomy in selected patients
			- Elderly
			- Frail/infirm patients

	- Bladder preservation strategy
		- Combined chemotherapy + radiotherapy

	- Where possible
		- Repeat TUR
			- Exclude residual macroscopic tumour

- Follow-up
	- Nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer
		- Requires
			- Repeat imaging
			- Repeat cystoscopy
		- Frequency based on
			- Recurrence risk
			- Progression risk

	- Post-cystectomy follow-up
		- 6-monthly
			- Contrast CT abdomen/pelvis
			- Chest X-ray or CT chest

	- Monitor
		- Renal function
			- Anastomotic strictures may occur

	- Duration of surveillance
		- Generally 5 years after cystectomy
		- neobladders should have annual cytology and cystoscopy to ensure there is no urethral recurrence

| WHO grading | Features |
|---|---|
| Urothelial papilloma | - Papillary lesion with no abnormal histological features  <br> - Classified as benign  <br> - Very rare  <br> - May occur with urothelial carcinoma  <br> - Do not recur after resection |
| Papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP) | - Papillary lesion without cytologic malignancy features  <br> - Negligible progression risk  <br> - May recur |
| Low-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma | - Moderately differentiated papillary lesion  <br> - Cytologic malignant features present |
| High-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma | - Poorly differentiated tumour  <br> - Marked cytologic abnormalities |

| TNM Component | Stage | Description |
|---|---|---|
| T – Primary tumour | Tx | Primary tumour cannot be assessed |
|  | T0 | No evidence of primary tumour |
|  | Ta | Noninvasive papillary carcinoma |
|  | Tis | Carcinoma in situ |
|  | T1 | Tumour invades subepithelial connective tissue (lamina propria) |
|  | T2 | Tumour invades muscle (muscularis propria) |
|  | T2a | Invades superficial muscle (inner half) |
|  | T2b | Invades deep muscle (outer half) |
|  | T3 | Tumour invades perivesical tissue |
|  | T3a | Microscopic invasion |
|  | T3b | Macroscopic invasion (extravesical mass) |
|  | T4 | Invasion of adjacent structures |
|  | T4a | Invades prostate, uterus or vagina |
|  | T4b | Invades pelvic or abdominal wall |
| N – Lymph nodes | Nx | Regional lymph nodes cannot be assessed |
|  | N0 | No regional lymph node metastasis |
|  | N1 | Metastasis in single node ≤2 cm |
|  | N2 | Metastasis in single or multiple nodes 2–5 cm |
|  | N3 | Metastasis in lymph node >5 cm |
| M – Metastasis | Mx | Distant metastasis cannot be assessed |
|  | M0 | No distant metastasis |
|  | M1 | Distant metastasis present |

| Risk group | Features |
|---|---|
| Low risk | - Solitary tumour  <br> - Primary low-grade Ta tumour |
| Intermediate risk | - Multiple low-grade tumours  <br> - Recurrent low-grade tumours |
| High risk | - Any T1 tumour  <br> - High-grade disease  <br> - Carcinoma in situ (CIS) |

| Risk group | Surveillance protocol |
|---|---|
| Low risk | - Cystoscopy at 3 months  <br> - If negative → repeat cystoscopy at 9 months  <br> - If negative → annual cystoscopy for 5 years |
| High risk | - Cystoscopy + urine cytology at 3 months  <br> - If negative → cystoscopy + cytology every 3 months for 2 years  <br> - If negative → every 4 months during 3rd year  <br> - If negative → every 6 months during 4th and 5th years  <br> - Annual cystoscopy thereafter  <br> - Annual upper tract evaluation |
| Intermediate risk | - Combination of low-risk and high-risk surveillance strategies  <br> - Tailored to individual patient risk |
