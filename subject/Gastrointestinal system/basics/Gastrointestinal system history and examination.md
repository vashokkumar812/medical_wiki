# History
- Abdominal pain
    - Onset
	    - questions
		    - When did the pain start?
		- Sudden onset
			- Perforated peptic ulcer, bowel ischaemia, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm or other acute abdominal emergencies.
		- Gradual onset
			- Appendicitis, cholecystitis, diverticulitis or other inflammatory conditions.
	- duration
		- questions
			- How long have you had the pain?
			- Is it constant or intermittent?
			- Is it getting worse, improving or remaining the same?
    - Site and migration
	    - questions
	        - Where exactly is the pain?
	        - Can you point to the site with one finger?
	        - Did the pain start somewhere else and then move?
		- Periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa
			- Acute appendicitis.
		- Epigastric pain localising to the right upper quadrant
			- Acute cholecystitis or biliary disease.
		- Flank or renal angle pain radiating towards the groin
			- Renal or ureteric colic.
		- Generalised abdominal pain
			- Gastroenteritis, bowel obstruction, peritonitis or bowel ischaemia, depending on associated features.
    - Character
	    - questions
	        - How would you describe the pain?
		- Dull or burning epigastric pain
			- Peptic ulcer disease
			- functional dyspepsia.
		- Severe, steady epigastric pain
			- Acute pancreatitis
			- biliary pain.
		- Cramping or colicky pain occurring in waves
			- Bowel obstruction
			- ureteric colic.
		- Sudden, severe pain that becomes generalised
			- Perforated viscus or another acute abdominal emergency.
		- Pain out of proportion to the abdominal examination
			- Acute mesenteric ischaemia, particularly with vascular risk factors.
    - Severity and progression
		- questions        
			- How severe is the pain on a scale of 0–10?
			- Is it worsening in severity?
			- Does the pain prevent you from walking or performing daily activities?
			- Is it severe enough to wake you from sleep?
		- Severe or rapidly progressive pain
			- Acute surgical or vascular abdominal emergency.
    - Radiation
	    - questions
	        - Does the pain spread anywhere else?
		- Epigastric pain radiating to the back
			- Acute pancreatitis or peptic ulcer disease with complications.
		- Right upper quadrant pain radiating to the right shoulder or scapula
                - Biliary colic or acute cholecystitis.
            - Flank pain radiating to the groin
                - Renal or ureteric colic.
            - Epigastric pain radiating to the chest, arm or jaw
                - Acute coronary syndrome as a potentially non-abdominal cause.
    - Relationship to meals
	    - questions
	        - Does eating trigger or worsen the pain?
	        - Does the pain improve when you eat?
		- Epigastric burning or dull pain partially relieved by food or antacids
			- Peptic ulcer disease.
		- Epigastric pain after meals without other specific features
			- Functional dyspepsia
			- peptic ulcer disease.
		- Right upper quadrant or epigastric pain after fatty meals
			- Biliary colic, although episodes may occur unpredictably.
		- Pain after meals with weight loss or food avoidance
			- Chronic mesenteric ischaemia in an appropriate clinical context.
        - improved after meals
            - Peptic ulcer disease, although the response to food cannot reliably distinguish gastric from duodenal ulcers.
    - Relieving and aggravating factors
	    - questions
	        - Does taking antacids relieve the pain?
		- Partial relief
			- Peptic ulcer disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux. 
        - Does vomiting relieve the pain?
            - Peptic ulcer disease or gastric outlet obstruction, depending on the clinical context.
        - Does passing stool or flatus relieve the pain?
            - Colonic disease or functional bowel disorders.
        - Does sitting up and leaning forwards relieve the pain?
            - Acute pancreatitis.
        - Does movement, coughing or walking worsen the pain?
            - Peritoneal irritation, particularly with localised tenderness.
        - Do you feel compelled to move or roll around because of the pain?
            - Colicky pain, such as renal colic or bowel obstruction.
        - Do you prefer to lie perfectly still?
            - Peritonitis, particularly with severe pain on movement.
- Nausea and vomiting
	
	- Have you felt nauseated or vomited?
		
	- How many times have you vomited?
		
	- What was the vomit like?
		
		- Food-containing vomit
			
			- Gastritis, peptic ulcer disease or gastric outlet obstruction.
				
		- Green or bilious vomit
			
			- Intestinal obstruction, particularly with distension and failure to pass flatus.
				
		- Faeculent-smelling vomit
			
			- Advanced intestinal obstruction.
				
		- Blood or coffee-ground material
			
			- Upper gastrointestinal bleeding.
				
	- Did vomiting begin before or after the pain?
		
		- Pain followed by vomiting
			
			- Appendicitis, pancreatitis or bowel obstruction.
				
	- Does vomiting relieve the pain?
		
		- Gastric outlet obstruction or other upper gastrointestinal disorders.
			
	- Is vomiting persistent?
		
		- Dehydration, electrolyte disturbances or bowel obstruction, depending on other findings.

- Abdominal distension
	
	- Has your abdomen become swollen or distended?
		
	- Is the distension increasing?
		
	- Is it associated with vomiting or constipation?
		
		- Distension with colicky pain, vomiting and inability to pass flatus
			
			- Bowel obstruction.
				
		- Progressive distension with ascites or weight loss
			
			- Liver disease, malignancy or other causes of ascites.
				
- Fever and systemic symptoms
	
	- Have you had fever or chills?
		
		- Fever with right upper quadrant pain
			
			- Acute cholecystitis or cholangitis, particularly with jaundice.
				
		- Fever with right iliac fossa pain
			
			- Appendicitis.
				
		- Fever with left lower quadrant pain
			
			- Diverticulitis.
				
		- Fever with generalised abdominal pain and rigidity
			
			- Peritonitis or another serious intra-abdominal infection.
				
	- Have you experienced loss of appetite?
		
		- Appendicitis or other inflammatory abdominal conditions.
			
	- Have you lost weight unintentionally?
		
		- Malignancy, chronic inflammatory disease or other chronic pathology.
			
	- Have you experienced dizziness, fainting or collapse?
		
		- Dehydration, internal bleeding, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm or other causes of circulatory compromise.
			
- Gastrointestinal bleeding
	
	- Have you vomited blood or coffee-ground material?
		
		- Upper gastrointestinal bleeding, including peptic ulcer disease.
			
	- Have you passed black, tarry stools?
		
		- Melaena due to gastrointestinal bleeding.
			
	- Have you noticed fresh blood in your stool?
		
		- Lower gastrointestinal bleeding, colitis or other intestinal pathology.
			
	- Are you taking anticoagulants or NSAIDs?
		
		- Increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding.
			
- Jaundice and biliary symptoms
	
	- Have you noticed yellowing of your eyes or skin?
		
	- Is your urine unusually dark?
		
	- Are your stools pale or clay-coloured?
		
	- Do you have generalised itching?
		
		- Right upper quadrant pain with fever and jaundice
			
			- Acute cholangitis requiring urgent assessment.
				
		- Right upper quadrant pain with fever and local tenderness
			
			- Acute cholecystitis.
				
		- Jaundice with dark urine and pale stools
			
			- Obstructive or hepatobiliary disease.
				
		- Painless progressive jaundice with weight loss
			
			- Pancreaticobiliary malignancy.
				
- Urinary symptoms
	
	- Do you have pain or burning during urination?
		
		- Urinary tract infection.
			
	- Have you noticed blood in your urine?
		
		- Renal or ureteric calculi, urinary infection or other urinary pathology.
			
	- Do you have flank pain radiating to the groin?
		
		- Renal or ureteric colic.
			
	- Are you passing urine normally?
		
		- Reduced urine output may indicate dehydration, urinary obstruction or renal impairment.
			
	- Do you have fever with flank pain?
		
		- Pyelonephritis, particularly with urinary symptoms.
			
- Cardiovascular symptoms
	
	- Do you have chest pain or shortness of breath?
		
		- Acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism or other cardiopulmonary causes presenting as abdominal pain.
			
	- Have you experienced fainting or sudden weakness?
		
		- Haemorrhage, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm or other causes of shock.
			
	- Do you have a history of atrial fibrillation or vascular disease?
		
		- Acute mesenteric ischaemia in the appropriate clinical context.
			
- Previous episodes and past medical history
	
	- Have you experienced similar episodes previously?
		
		- Recurrent postprandial right upper quadrant pain
			
			- Biliary colic.
				
		- Recurrent epigastric burning pain
			
			- Peptic ulcer disease or functional dyspepsia.
				
		- Previous similar colicky flank pain
			
			- Recurrent renal calculi.
				
	- Do you have a history of:
		
		- Peptic ulcer disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux
			
		- Gallstones or cholecystitis
			
		- Pancreatitis
			
		- Renal stones
			
		- Bowel obstruction
			
		- Inflammatory bowel disease
			
		- Liver disease
			
		- Abdominal malignancy
			
	- Have you undergone abdominal surgery?
		
		- Previous abdominal surgery with colicky pain, vomiting and distension
			
			- Adhesive small-bowel obstruction.
				
- Drug history
	
	- Do you take NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen or diclofenac?
		
		- Gastritis, peptic ulcer disease or gastrointestinal bleeding.
			
	- Do you take anticoagulants?
		
		- Gastrointestinal or intra-abdominal bleeding.
			
	- Do you take opioids?
		
		- Medication-related constipation or ileus.
			
	- Do you take any other regular medications or supplements?
		
- Family history
	
	- Is there a family history of:
		
		- Gastrointestinal malignancy
			
		- Inflammatory bowel disease
			
		- Gallstones
			
		- Coeliac disease
			
		- Other relevant hereditary conditions
			
- Social history
	
	- How much alcohol do you consume?
		
		- Heavy alcohol use
			
			- Acute or chronic pancreatitis and liver disease.
				
	- Do you smoke?
		
		- Peptic ulcer disease and vascular risk.
			
	- Have you travelled recently or eaten potentially contaminated food?
		
		- Infective gastroenteritis or foodborne illness.
			
	- What is your usual diet?
		
- Pregnancy and gynaecological history, when relevant
	
	- When was your last menstrual period?
		
	- Is there any possibility you could be pregnant?
		
	- Have you experienced vaginal bleeding or discharge?
		
	- Do you have pelvic pain?
		
	- Is the pain associated with your menstrual cycle?
		
		- Abdominal pain with a missed period or vaginal bleeding
			
			- Ectopic pregnancy requiring urgent assessment.
				
		- Sudden unilateral pelvic pain with nausea or vomiting
			
			- Ovarian torsion.
				
		- Pelvic pain with fever or vaginal discharge
			
			- Pelvic inflammatory disease.
				
- Red flags and emergency assessment
	
	- Sudden severe abdominal pain
		
		- Perforated viscus, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, bowel ischaemia or other acute emergencies.
			
	- Severe pain out of proportion to examination findings
		
		- Acute mesenteric ischaemia.
			
	- Rigid abdomen or severe pain on movement
		
		- Peritonitis.
			
	- Persistent vomiting with distension and inability to pass flatus
		
		- Bowel obstruction.
			
	- Haematemesis, melaena or significant rectal bleeding
		
		- Gastrointestinal haemorrhage.
			
	- Fever with jaundice and right upper quadrant pain
		
		- Acute cholangitis.
			
	- Collapse, hypotension, confusion or cold clammy skin
		
		- Shock due to haemorrhage, sepsis or another critical cause.
			
- Closing the history
        
	- Summarise the pain characteristics, associated symptoms and relevant risk factors.
		
	- Ask whether the patient has any additional concerns.
		
	- Explain the need for examination and appropriate investigations.
		
	- Thank the patient.
- Appetite and weight change
    - Loss of appetite + weight loss
        - Gastrointestinal malignancy, depression, other systemic diseases.
    - Weight loss + increased appetite
        - Malabsorption, hypermetabolic states (e.g. thyrotoxicosis).
    - Document onset and amount of weight loss.
    - Altered taste in liver disease
        - May cause smokers with acute hepatitis and jaundice to stop smoking.
- Early satiation and postprandial fullness
    - Early satiation (unable to finish a normal meal)
        - Gastric cancer, peptic ulcer disease.
    - Postprandial fullness (uncomfortable fullness after eating)
        - Functional dyspepsia.

- Nausea and vomiting
    
    - Nausea: sensation of wanting to vomit; retching may occur without expulsion.
        
    - Vomiting: involuntary ejection of gastric contents through the mouth.
        
    - Acute causes
        
        - Gastrointestinal infection / food poisoning.
            
        - Small-bowel obstruction.
            
    - Chronic causes
        
        - Pregnancy; drugs (digoxin, opioids, dopamine agonists, chemotherapy).
            
        - Peptic ulcer disease with gastric outlet obstruction.
            
        - Gastroparesis (diabetes, post-gastric surgery).
            
        - Acute hepatobiliary disease; alcoholism.
            
        - Psychogenic vomiting, eating disorders (e.g. bulimia); rarely, raised intracranial pressure.
            
    - Timing
        
        - Vomiting >1 hour after meals
            
            - Gastric outlet obstruction, gastroparesis.
                
        - Early-morning vomiting before eating
            
            - Pregnancy, alcoholism, raised intracranial pressure.
                
    - Vomitus contents
        
        - Bile
            
            - Suggests communication between the stomach and duodenum.
                
        - Old food
            
            - Gastric outlet obstruction.
                
        - Blood
            
            - Possible ulceration or gastrointestinal bleeding.
                
        - Vomiting with little or no nausea
            
            - May occur in bowel obstruction.
                
    - Differentiate rumination
        
        - Effortless regurgitation of food after eating; food is spat out or reswallowed, often without an unpleasant taste.

- Heartburn and acid regurgitation (GORD)
    
    - Heartburn
        
        - Burning retrosternal discomfort radiating towards the throat.
            
        - Occurs after meals; worsens on bending, stooping or lying supine.
            
        - Temporarily relieved by antacids.
            
        - Caused by reflux of gastric contents into the oesophagus.
            
    - Acid regurgitation
        
        - Sour or bitter-tasting fluid rising into the mouth.
            
        - Strongly suggests gastro-oesophageal reflux.
            
    - Associated symptoms
        
        - Cough worsening when lying down.
            
    - Aggravating factors
        
        - Alcohol, chocolate, caffeine, fatty meals.
            
        - Drugs: theophylline, calcium channel blockers, anticholinergics (reduce lower oesophageal sphincter pressure).
            
    - Alarm features requiring further evaluation
        
        - Dysphagia.
            
        - Unintentional weight loss.
            
        - Melaena or haematemesis.
            
    - Waterbrash
        
        - Excessive saliva in the mouth; distinct from acid regurgitation.
            
        - Occasionally occurs with peptic ulcer disease or oesophagitis.


- Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)
    
    - Phases of swallowing
        
        - Oropharyngeal: mouth → pharynx → upper oesophagus.
            
        - Oesophageal: oesophagus → stomach.
            
    - Differentiate dysphagia from odynophagia
        
        - Odynophagia: painful swallowing.
            
            - Infectious oesophagitis (Candida, HSV), oesophageal ulceration, caustic injury, rarely perforation.
                
    - Oropharyngeal dysphagia
        
        - Difficulty initiating swallowing, nasal regurgitation, choking.
            
        - Causes: neurological disorders (e.g. motor neurone disease with bulbar/pseudobulbar palsy).
            
    - Oesophageal dysphagia
        
        - Sensation of food sticking in the oesophagus; ask the patient to localise the site.
            
        - Lower retrosternal sticking
            
            - Suggests distal oesophageal obstruction.
                
        - Dysphagia with heartburn
            
            - GORD, with or without oesophageal stricture.
                
    - Pattern of dysphagia
        
        - Intermittent or only with the first few swallows
            
            - Lower oesophageal ring, eosinophilic oesophagitis, rarely diffuse oesophageal spasm.
                
        - Progressive dysphagia
            
            - Oesophageal stricture, carcinoma, achalasia.
                
        - Both solids and liquids affected
            
            - Oesophageal motility disorder (e.g. achalasia, diffuse oesophageal spasm).

- Diarrhoea
    
    - Definition
        
        - More than 3 stools/day or a change from usual frequency with loose or watery stools.
            
        - Assess stool frequency, volume and consistency.
            
        - Frequent small, formed stools with normal volume
            
            - Not true diarrhoea; consider rectal pathology, incomplete rectal emptying or psychological factors.
                
    - Duration
        
        - Acute diarrhoea
            
            - More likely infectious.
                
        - Chronic diarrhoea
            
            - Consider multiple causes, including malabsorption, inflammatory disease and altered motility.
                
    - Types and causes
        
        - Watery, high-volume stools
            
            - Secretory diarrhoea
                
                - High-volume (often >1 L/day), persists during fasting; usually no blood, pus or excessive fat.
                    
                - Causes: infections (E. coli, S. aureus, Vibrio cholerae), VIP-secreting tumour, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, carcinoid syndrome, villous adenoma.
                    
            - Osmotic diarrhoea
                
                - Improves or stops with fasting; related to food intake.
                    
                - Causes: lactose intolerance, magnesium-containing antacids, gastric surgery.
                    
            - Abnormal intestinal motility
                
                - Causes: thyrotoxicosis, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS).
                    
        - Blood or mucus in stools
            
            - Exudative/inflammatory diarrhoea
                
                - Frequent, small-volume stools; may contain blood or mucus.
                    
                - Causes: inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer.
                    
        - Fatty stools (steatorrhoea)
            
            - Pale, foul-smelling, greasy stools that float and are difficult to flush.
                
            - Suggests fat malabsorption.
                
            - Defined as >7 g of faecal fat in a 24-hour stool collection.

- Constipation
    
    - Definition
        
        - Infrequent stools (<3 times/week), hard stools or difficulty evacuating.
            
    - Duration
        
        - Acute or chronic; ask about recent change in bowel habits.
            
    - Causes
        
        - Lifestyle: habitual suppression of defecation, low food intake, dietary changes, immobility, postoperative state.
            
        - Drugs: codeine, antidepressants, aluminium/calcium antacids.
            
        - Metabolic/endocrine: hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, diabetes mellitus, hypokalaemia.
            
        - Neurological: Hirschsprung disease, autonomic neuropathy, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis.
            
        - Structural: partial colonic obstruction, colorectal cancer.
            
        - Pregnancy: common, especially in later stages.
            
        - Slow-transit constipation: delayed colonic transit; more common in young women.
            
    - Evacuation difficulty
        
        - Excessive straining, sensation of anal blockage or incomplete evacuation.
            
        - Need for manual evacuation (digitation)
            
            - Pelvic floor dysfunction, anorectal disease (e.g. fissure, stricture).
                
    - Red flag
        
        - Recent change in bowel habits, especially with weight loss or rectal bleeding
            
            - Consider colorectal malignancy.
                
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
    
    - Chronic, fluctuating bowel habit with abdominal pain; constipation and diarrhoea may alternate.
        
    - Features supporting IBS
        
        - Abdominal pain relieved by defecation.
            
        - Looser or more frequent stools when pain begins.
            
        - Mucus per rectum.
            
        - Feeling of incomplete evacuation.
            
        - Visible abdominal distension.
            
    - Diagnosis
        
        - Based on recognised diagnostic criteria and symptom pattern; assess for features suggesting organic disease.

- Mucus in stool
    
    - White slime passed per rectum.
        
    - Causes: irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), solitary rectal ulcer, fistula, villous adenoma.
        
- Gastrointestinal bleeding
    
    - Haematemesis
        
        - Vomiting blood; suggests bleeding from the upper GI tract, proximal to or within the duodenum.
            
        - Causes: bleeding peptic ulcer (particularly duodenal ulcer).
            
        - Repeated vomiting followed by blood
            
            - Mallory–Weiss tear.
                
        - Exclude swallowed blood from epistaxis or oral bleeding and haemoptysis.
            
        - Peptic ulcer bleeding may occur without abdominal pain.
            
    - Melaena
        
        - Black, tarry stools; usually upper GI bleeding.
            
        - Occasionally caused by small-bowel or right-sided colonic lesions.
            
    - Haematochezia
        
        - Bright-red blood per rectum.
            
        - Blood on toilet paper, coating stool or in the toilet bowl
            
            - Haemorrhoids, anal fissure or other anorectal disease.
                
        - Blood mixed with stool
            
            - Consider colonic pathology.
                
        - Significant bleeding
            
            - Consider diverticular disease, angiodysplasia or brisk upper GI bleeding.
                
    - Occult blood
        
        - Blood detected on stool testing without visible bleeding.
            
    - Bleeding tendency
        
        - Spontaneous bruising or bleeding from the nose/mouth
            
            - Consider coagulopathy associated with liver disease.
                
- Jaundice
    
    - Yellow discoloration of the sclerae and skin due to elevated bilirubin.
        
    - Ask about urine and stool colour.
        
        - Dark urine + pale stools
            
            - Suggests cholestasis or biliary obstruction.
                
    - Ask about abdominal pain.
        
        - Pain with jaundice
            
            - Consider gallstones and biliary obstruction.
                
- Pruritus
    
    - Localised or generalised itching.
        
    - Pruritus, particularly over the extremities
        
        - May occur in cholestatic liver disease.

- Abdominal bloating and swelling
    
    - Bloating: sensation of abdominal fullness or swelling.
        
        - Causes: excess intestinal gas, visceral hypersensitivity (e.g. IBS).
            
    - Persistent abdominal swelling
        
        - Ascites; may be associated with ankle oedema.
            
- Lethargy and fatigue
    
    - Acute or chronic liver disease.
        
    - Anaemia due to GI bleeding or chronic inflammatory disease.
        
    - Nonspecific symptom; common in the general population.
        
- Treatment and medication history
    
    - NSAIDs (including aspirin)
        
        - Acute or chronic GI mucosal injury and bleeding.
            
    - Drugs affecting bowel habits
        
        - May cause constipation or diarrhoea.
            
    - Drugs associated with liver injury
        
        - Acute hepatitis: halothane, phenytoin, chlorothiazide.
            
        - Cholestasis: chlorpromazine/other phenothiazines, sulfonamides, sulfonylureas, phenylbutazone, rifampicin, nitrofurantoin.
            
        - Dose-related cholestasis: anabolic steroids, oral contraceptive pill.
            
        - Fatty liver: alcohol, tetracycline, valproic acid, amiodarone.
            
        - Peliosis hepatis: anabolic steroids, oral contraceptive pill.
            
        - Acute liver cell necrosis: paracetamol overdose.


- Past history
    
    - Previous abdominal surgery
        
        - Jaundice due to halothane anaesthesia, perioperative hypotension causing hepatic hypoxia, or bile duct injury.
            
    - Recurrent epigastric pain followed by sudden severe abdominal pain
        
        - Perforated peptic ulcer.
            
    - Previous inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease)
        
        - Possible disease flare.
            
- Social history
    
    - Occupation
        
        - Healthcare workers: potential hepatitis exposure.
            
    - Toxin exposure
        
        - Carbon tetrachloride, vinyl chloride: chronic liver disease.
            
    - Recent travel
        
        - Travel to hepatitis-endemic regions.
            
    - Alcohol history
        
        - Assess amount, frequency and duration; patients may underreport consumption.
            
    - Contact history
        
        - Contact with someone who has jaundice.
            
    - Sexual history
        
        - Assess risk factors for viral hepatitis, including sexual exposure.
            
    - Blood-borne exposure
        
        - Intravenous drug use, blood transfusion, dental procedures, tattoos.
            
        - Risk of hepatitis B and C.
            
- Family history
    
    - Colorectal cancer or familial polyps
        
        - Increased risk of colorectal malignancy.
            
    - Inflammatory bowel disease
        
        - Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis.
            
    - Coeliac disease
        
        - Familial predisposition.
            
    - Family history of jaundice, anaemia, splenectomy or cholecystectomy
        
        - Haemolytic anaemia, haemoglobin disorders, autoimmune disease or familial hyperbilirubinaemia.
## anatomy
- Liver
  - Largest organ in the abdomen.
  - Lobes
    - Large right lobe.
    - Smaller left lobe.
    - Divided into 8 segments.
    - Caudate lobe → segment I.
  - Lower border
    - Extends from the tip of the right 10th rib to just below the left nipple.
  - Palpation
    - Normally not palpable.
    - Lower edge may just be palpable in healthy people.

- Spleen
  - Lymphoid organ.
  - Lies posteriorly beneath the 9th, 10th and 11th ribs on the left.
  - Usually not palpable in health.

- Kidneys
  - Anteriorly
    - Lie four finger-breadths from the midline.
  - Posteriorly
    - Lie under the 12th rib.
  - Right kidney
    - Normally extends 2.5 cm lower than the left.
    - Lower pole may be palpable in thin healthy people.

- Gallbladder
  - Pear-shaped organ.
  - Fundus
    - Located at the tip of the right 9th costal cartilage.
  - Cannot be felt in health.

- Pancreas
  - Located in the retroperitoneum.
  - Head
    - Tucked into the C-shaped duodenum.
  - Tail
    - Extends into the spleen.
  - A huge pancreatic mass may rarely be palpable.

- Aorta
  - Lies in the midline.
  - Terminates just to the left of the midline at the level of the iliac crest.
  - Pulsatile mass in the middle of the abdomen
    - Likely arises from the aorta.
    - May indicate an aneurysm.

- Stomach
  - Usually J-shaped.
  - Lies in the left upper abdomen over the spleen and pancreas.
  - Connects with the duodenum.

- Small intestine
  - Length → 3–10 metres.
  - Upper half
    - Duodenum.
    - Jejunum.
  - Lower half
    - Ileum.
  - Lies over the middle section of the abdomen.
  - Usually impalpable.

- Colon
  - Length → approximately 1.5 metres.
  - From right to left
    - Caecum.
    - Ascending colon.
    - Hepatic flexure.
    - Transverse colon.
    - Splenic flexure.
    - Descending colon.
    - Sigmoid colon.
    - Rectum.
    - Anal canal (anorectum).
  - Caecum and ascending colon
    - Lie on the right side of the abdomen.
  - Transverse colon
    - Runs across the upper abdomen from right to left.
  - Descending colon, sigmoid and rectum
    - Lie on the left side of the abdomen.
  - Colonic masses
    - Rarely felt in the abdomen.

- Appendix
  - Usually lies in the right lower abdominal area.
  - Arises posteromedially from the caecum.

- Other important anatomical areas
  - Inguinal canal.
  - Anorectum.

## position
- Abdominal examination: patient position
  - Patient should lie flat.
  - Head should rest on a single pillow.
  - This position
    - Relaxes the abdominal muscles.
    - Facilitates abdominal palpation.
  - Helping the patient into this position
    - Provides an opportunity for general inspection.

## inspection
- General appearance
  - Jaundice
    - Yellow discoloration of the sclerae (conjunctivae) and skin due to hyperbilirubinaemia.
    - Best observed in natural daylight.
    - Depth of jaundice can be quite variable, regardless of the underlying cause.
  - Weight and wasting
    - Patient's weight must be recorded.
    - Failure of the gastrointestinal tract to absorb food normally may cause:
      - Weight loss.
      - Cachexia.
    - Weight loss and cachexia may also result from:
      - Gastrointestinal malignancy.
      - Alcoholic cirrhosis.
    - Folds of loose skin hanging from the abdomen and limbs
      - Suggest recent weight loss.
    - Obesity
      - Can cause fatty infiltration of the liver (non-alcoholic steatohepatitis).
      - May result in abnormal liver function tests.
    - Anabolic steroid use
      - Can increase muscle bulk.
      - Can cause liver tumours, including:
        - Adenomas.
        - Hepatocellular carcinomas.
  - Skin
    - Gastrointestinal tract and skin have a common origin from the embryoblast.
    - Several diseases can involve both the skin and gut.
  - Pigmentation
    - Generalised skin pigmentation
      - Can result from chronic liver disease.
      - Particularly seen in haemochromatosis.
      - Haemosiderin stimulates melanocytes to produce melanin.
    - Malabsorption
      - May cause Addisonian-type pigmentation ('sunkissed' pigmentation).
      - May involve:
        - Nipples.
        - Palmar creases.
        - Pressure areas.
        - Mouth.
  - Peutz–Jeghers syndrome
    - Freckle-like spots:
      - Discrete brown-black lesions.
      - Around the mouth.
      - Buccal mucosa.
      - Fingers and toes.
    - Associated with hamartomas of:
      - Small bowel → 50%.
      - Colon → 30%.
    - Hamartomas may present with:
      - Bleeding.
      - Intussusception.
    - Autosomal dominant condition.
    - Increased incidence of gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma.
  - Acanthosis nigricans
    - Brown-to-black, velvety elevations of the epidermis.
    - Due to confluent papillomas.
    - Usually found in:
      - Axillae.
      - Nape of the neck.
    - Rarely associated with:
      - Gastrointestinal carcinoma, particularly stomach.
      - Lymphoma.
    - Also associated with:
      - Acromegaly.
      - Diabetes mellitus.
      - Other endocrinopathies.
  - Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Rendu–Osler–Weber syndrome)
    - Multiple small telangiectasias.
    - Often present on:
      - Lips.
      - Tongue.
    - May occur anywhere on the skin.
    - Gastrointestinal telangiectasias
      - Can cause chronic blood loss.
      - May occasionally cause torrential bleeding.
    - Associated arteriovenous malformations may occur.
    - Autosomal dominant condition.
    - Uncommon.
- Porphyria cutanea tarda
  - Fragile vesicles appear on exposed areas of the skin.
  - Heal with scarring.
  - Urine is dark.
  - Chronic disorder of porphyrin metabolism.
  - Associated with:
    - Alcoholism.
    - Hepatitis C.

- Systemic sclerosis
  - Tense tethering of the skin.
  - Often associated with:
    - Gastro-oesophageal reflux.
    - Gastrointestinal motility disorders.

- Mental state
  - Assess orientation.
  - Hepatic encephalopathy
    - Organic neurological disturbance due to:
      - Decompensated advanced cirrhosis (chronic liver failure).
      - Fulminant hepatitis (acute liver failure).
    - Features depend on:
      - Aetiology.
      - Precipitating factors.
    - Patients eventually become:
      - Stuporous.
      - Comatose.
    - Combination of:
      - Hepatocellular damage.
      - Portosystemic shunting due to disturbed hepatic structure.
        - Extrahepatic.
        - Intrahepatic.
    - Probably related to failure of the liver to remove toxic metabolites from portal blood.
    - Possible toxic metabolites include:
      - Ammonia.
      - Mercaptans.
      - Short-chain fatty acids.
      - Amines.

- Hands
  - Examination of the gastrointestinal tract begins with examination of the hands.
  - Hand signs may provide clues to chronic liver disease.
  - Permanent diffuse liver damage can produce similar peripheral signs regardless of aetiology.
  - No individual sign is specific for chronic liver disease.

- Nails
  - Leuconychia
    - Chronic liver or other disease causing hypoalbuminaemia can cause nail-bed opacification.
    - Abnormality is in the nail bed, not the nail.
    - Often leaves only a rim of pink nail bed at the top of the nail.
    - Terry's nails.
    - Thumb and index nails are most often involved.
    - Possible mechanism:
      - Compression of capillary flow by extracellular fluid.
  - Muehrcke's lines
    - Transverse white lines.
    - Can occur in hypoalbuminaemic states, including cirrhosis.
  - Blue lunulae
    - May occur in Wilson's disease (hepatolenticular degeneration).

- Clubbing
  - Up to one-third of patients with cirrhosis may have finger clubbing.
  - May be related to arteriovenous shunting in the lungs.
    - Results in arterial oxygen desaturation.
  - Cyanosis may occur with severe long-standing chronic liver disease.
  - Cause of pulmonary arteriovenous shunting is unknown.
  - Long-standing nutritional depletion can also cause clubbing, including:
    - Inflammatory bowel disease.
    - Coeliac disease.

- Palms
  - Palmar erythema ('liver palms')
    - Reddening of the palms.
    - Affects:
      - Thenar eminence.
      - Hypothenar eminence.
    - Soles of the feet may also be affected.
    - Can be a feature of chronic liver disease.
    - Has been attributed to raised oestrogen levels.
    - Not shown to be related to plasma oestradiol levels.
    - Aetiology remains uncertain.
    - Can also occur with:
      - Thyrotoxicosis.
      - Rheumatoid arthritis.
      - Polycythaemia.
      - Chronic febrile diseases.
      - Chronic leukaemia.
    - May be a normal finding, especially in women.
    - Like spider naevi, may occur in pregnancy.
  - Anaemia
    - Inspect palmar creases for pallor.
    - Possible causes include:
      - Gastrointestinal blood loss.
      - Malabsorption:
        - Folate deficiency.
        - Vitamin B12 deficiency.
      - Haemolysis, e.g. hypersplenism.
      - Chronic disease.
  - Dupuytren's contracture
    - Visible and palpable thickening and contraction of the palmar fascia.
    - Causes permanent flexion.
    - Most often affects the ring finger.
    - Often bilateral.
    - Occasionally affects the feet.
    - Associated with:
      - Alcoholism.
      - Manual work.
      - Familial occurrence.
    - Associated with alcoholism, not liver disease.
  - Hepatic flap (asterixis)
    - Ask the patient to:
      - Stretch out the arms in front.
      - Separate the fingers.
      - Extend the wrists for 15 seconds.
    - Push on the patient's fingers to keep them extended.
    - Positive finding
      - Jerky, irregular flexion-extension movements at:
        - Wrist joints.
        - Metacarpophalangeal joints.
      - Often accompanied by lateral movements of the fingers.
      - Constitutes the flapping of hepatic encephalopathy (asterixis).
    - Mechanism
      - Thought to result from interference with inflow of joint-position-sense information to the reticular formation in the brainstem.
      - Results in rhythmic lapses of postural muscle tone.
    - Other possible sites
      - Arms.
      - Neck.
      - Tongue.
      - Jaws.
      - Eyelids.
    - Can sometimes be demonstrated by asking the patient to:
      - Close the eyes forcefully.
      - Protrude the tongue.
    - Characteristics
      - Bilateral.
      - Usually absent at rest.
      - Brought on by sustained posture.
      - Movements are not synchronous on each side.
      - Absent when coma supervenes.
    - Although characteristic and an early sign of liver failure, asterixis is not diagnostic.
    - Can also occur in:
      - Cardiac failure.
      - Respiratory failure.
      - Renal failure.
      - Hypoglycaemia.
      - Hypokalaemia.
      - Hypomagnesaemia.
      - Barbiturate intoxication.
- Arms
  - Bruising
    - Large bruises (ecchymoses)
      - May be due to clotting abnormalities.
      - Hepatocellular damage can impair protein synthesis and production of clotting factors.
      - Exception → factor VIII, which is produced elsewhere in the reticuloendothelial system.
    - Obstructive jaundice
      - Reduced bile acids in the intestine.
      - May reduce absorption of vitamin K.
      - Vitamin K is essential for production of:
        - Factor II (prothrombin).
        - Factor VII.
        - Factor IX.
        - Factor X.
    - Petechiae
      - Pinhead-sized bruises.
      - May occur with:
        - Alcohol-related bone marrow depression → thrombocytopenia.
        - Portal hypertension → splenomegaly → hypersplenism → increased platelet destruction.
        - Severe liver disease, especially acute hepatic necrosis → disseminated intravascular coagulation.
  - Muscle wasting
    - Often a late manifestation of malnutrition in alcoholic patients.
    - Alcohol can also cause proximal myopathy.
  - Scratch marks
    - Due to severe pruritus.
    - Often prominent in obstructive or cholestatic jaundice.
    - Common presenting feature of primary biliary cirrhosis before other signs appear.
    - Pruritus is thought to result from retention of an unknown substance normally excreted in bile.
    - Previously attributed to bile salt deposition in the skin.
  - Spider naevi
    - Central arteriole with numerous radiating small vessels resembling spider legs.
    - Size → barely visible to approximately 0.5 cm in diameter.
    - Usually distributed in the area drained by the superior vena cava:
      - Arms.
      - Neck.
      - Chest wall.
    - May occasionally bleed profusely.
    - Pressure over the central arteriole
      - Causes blanching of the whole lesion.
      - Release of pressure → rapid refilling from the centre towards the peripheral vessels.
    - More than 2 spider naevi anywhere on the body is likely to be abnormal.
    - Causes and associations:
      - Cirrhosis, most frequently alcohol-related.
      - Viral hepatitis → may occur transiently.
      - Pregnancy → frequently appear during months 2–5 and disappear within days of delivery.
    - In cirrhosis
      - Number may increase or decrease with the patient's condition.
      - Prominence may change similarly to palmar erythema.
    - Traditionally attributed to oestrogen excess.
    - Chronic liver disease impairs hepatic inactivation of oestrogens.
    - Changes in plasma oestradiol levels do not correlate with appearance or disappearance of spider naevi.
    - Differential diagnosis:
      - Campbell de Morgan spots.
      - Venous stars.
      - Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia.
    - Campbell de Morgan spots
      - Elevated red circular lesions.
      - Occur on the abdomen or front of the chest.
      - Do not blanch on pressure.
      - Very common and benign.
    - Venous stars
      - 2–3 cm lesions.
      - May occur on:
        - Dorsum of feet.
        - Legs.
        - Back.
        - Lower chest.
      - Due to elevated venous pressure.
      - Found over the main tributary to a large vein.
      - Not obliterated by pressure.
      - Blood flow → periphery to centre.
      - Opposite direction to flow in spider naevi.
    - Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia
      - Lesions may occasionally resemble spider naevi.
  - Axillae
    - Palpate for lymphadenopathy.
    - Look for acanthosis nigricans.

- Physical examination for malnutrition in chronic liver disease
  - Subcutaneous fat
    - Look for:
      - Looseness of skin over subcutaneous tissues.
      - Loss of fullness.
    - Changes may be most obvious:
      - Over triceps muscles.
      - On chest at the costal margin in the midaxillary line.
      - In the hands.
  - Muscle wasting
    - Examine:
      - Deltoids.
      - Quadriceps femoris.
    - Loss of deltoid mass → squared-off appearance of shoulders.
    - Muscle wasting can also occur in neurological disease.
  - Movement of fluid from intravascular to extravascular space
    - Associated with inability of the liver to manufacture normal serum proteins.
    - Look for:
      - Ascites.
      - Peripheral oedema.
  - Micronutrient deficiencies
    - Bruising → vitamin K, vitamin C deficiency.
    - Glossitis → folate, vitamin B12, niacin deficiency.
    - Dermatitis → vitamin A deficiency.

- Face

  - Eyes
    - Sclerae
      - Look for jaundice.
      - Look for anaemia → pale conjunctiva.
    - Bitot's spots
      - Yellow, keratinised areas on the sclera.
      - Result from severe vitamin A deficiency.
      - May be due to:
        - Malabsorption.
        - Malnutrition.
      - Retinal damage and blindness may occur later.
    - Kayser–Fleischer rings
      - Brownish-green rings at the periphery of the cornea.
      - Upper pole affected more than lower pole.
      - Due to excess copper deposits in Descemet's membrane of the cornea.
      - Slit-lamp examination is often necessary.
      - Typically found in Wilson's disease.
      - Wilson's disease
        - Copper storage disease.
        - Causes cirrhosis and neurological disturbances.
      - Usually present by the time neurological signs appear.
      - Can also occur in other cholestatic liver diseases.
    - Iritis
      - May occur in inflammatory bowel disease.
    - Xanthelasmata
      - Yellowish plaques in subcutaneous tissues around the eyes.
      - Due to lipid deposits.
      - May indicate prolonged elevation of serum cholesterol.
      - In cholestasis:
        - Lipoprotein X is present in plasma.
        - Associated with elevated serum cholesterol.
      - Common in primary biliary cirrhosis.
    - Periorbital purpura
      - Following proctosigmoidoscopy, 'black eye syndrome'.
      - Characteristic but exceedingly rare sign of amyloidosis.
      - May be related to factor X deficiency.

  - Salivary glands
    - Inspect and palpate cheeks over the parotid area for parotid enlargement.
    - Ask the patient to clench the teeth.
      - Masseter muscle becomes palpable.
      - Normal parotid gland → impalpable.
      - Enlarged parotid gland → best felt behind the masseter muscle and in front of the ear.
    - Bimanual palpation
      - Allows the parotid duct to be felt.
    - Parotid calculus
      - May be present at the parotid duct orifice.
      - Located opposite the upper second molar.
    - Bilateral parotidomegaly
      - Associated with alcoholism rather than liver disease itself.
      - Due to fatty infiltration.
      - May be secondary to alcohol toxicity with or without malnutrition.
    - Tender, warm, swollen parotid
      - Suggests parotiditis following an acute illness or surgery.
    - Mixed parotid tumour (pleomorphic adenoma)
      - Most common cause of a parotid lump.
    - Parotid carcinoma
      - May cause facial nerve palsy.
    - Mumps
      - Causes acute parotid enlargement.
      - Usually bilateral.
    - Submandibular gland enlargement
      - Most often due to a calculus.
      - May be palpable bimanually.
      - Place gloved index finger on the floor of the patient's mouth beside the tongue.
      - Feel between the finger and fingers placed behind the body of the mandible.
      - May also be enlarged in chronic liver disease.

- Mouth

  - Teeth and breath
    - Mouth is accessible to inspection without elaborate equipment.
    - Inspect teeth.
    - Note whether teeth are real or false.
    - False teeth
      - May need to be removed for complete examination of the mouth.
    - Inspect for:
      - Gum hypertrophy.
      - Pigmentation.
    - Loose-fitting false teeth
      - May cause ulcers.
    - Decayed teeth
      - May cause fetor (bad breath).

  - Pigmented lesions of the mouth
    - Heavy metals
      - Lead or bismuth → blue-black line on gingival margin.
      - Iron in haemochromatosis → blue-grey pigmentation of hard palate.
    - Drugs
      - Antimalarials.
      - Oral contraceptive pill.
      - Brown or black areas of pigmentation anywhere in the mouth.
    - Addison's disease
      - Dark brown pigmentation anywhere in the mouth.
    - Peutz–Jeghers syndrome
      - Lips.
      - Buccal mucosa.
      - Palate.
    - Malignant melanoma
      - Raised, painless black lesions anywhere in the mouth.

  - Fetor (bad breath)
    - Causes:
      - Faulty oral hygiene.
      - Fetor hepaticus → sweet smell.
      - Ketosis
        - Diabetic ketoacidosis → ketones in exhaled air.
        - Sickly sweet smell.
      - Uraemia → fish breath due to ammoniacal odour.
      - Alcohol.
      - Paraldehyde.
      - Putrid smell
        - Anaerobic chest infections with large amounts of sputum.
      - Cigarettes.
    - Fetor hepaticus
      - Rather sweet smell of the breath.
      - Indicates severe hepatocellular disease.
      - May be due to methylmercaptans.
      - Methylmercaptans are exhaled in the breath.
      - May be derived from methionine when it is not demethylated by a diseased liver.
      - Severe fetor hepaticus filling the patient's room
        - Bad sign.
        - Indicates a precomatose condition in many cases.
      - Fetor hepaticus in a patient with coma of unknown cause
        - May provide a clue to the diagnosis.
    - If smell is not obvious
      - Ask the patient to exhale through the mouth.
      - Sniff a small amount of exhaled air.

  - Tongue
    - Coating
      - Thickened epithelium with bacterial debris and food particles.
      - Common, especially in smokers.
      - Rarely a sign of disease.
      - More marked posteriorly due to:
        - Less mobility.
        - Slower desquamation of papillae.
      - Common in respiratory tract infections.
      - Not related to constipation or serious abdominal disease.
    - Lingua nigra (black tongue)
      - Elongation of papillae over the posterior tongue.
      - Appears dark brown due to keratin accumulation.
      - Symptomless apart from aesthetic problems.
      - Bismuth compounds may also cause black discoloration.
    - Geographical tongue
      - Slowly changing red rings and lines on the tongue.
      - Not painful.
      - Tends to come and go.
      - Usually not significant.
      - May indicate riboflavin (vitamin B2) deficiency.
    - Leucoplakia
      - White-coloured thickening of the mucosa of the tongue and mouth.
      - Premalignant.
      - Common causes begin with 'S':
        - Sore teeth (poor dental hygiene).
        - Smoking.
        - Spirits.
        - Sepsis.
        - Syphilis.
      - Cause may not be apparent.
      - May also occur on:
        - Larynx.
        - Anus.
        - Vulva.
    - Glossitis
      - Smooth appearance of the tongue.
      - May also be erythematous.
      - Due to atrophy of papillae.
      - Later stages may show shallow ulceration.
      - Often results from nutritional deficiencies.
      - Tongue is susceptible because of rapid turnover of mucosal cells.
      - Common deficiencies:
        - Iron.
        - Folate.
        - Vitamin B group, especially vitamin B12.
      - Common in alcoholics.
      - Can occur in carcinoid syndrome.
      - Many cases, especially in elderly people, have no identifiable cause.
    - Macroglossia
      - Enlargement of the tongue.
      - Congenital conditions:
        - Down syndrome.
      - Endocrine disease:
        - Acromegaly.
      - Tumour infiltration:
        - Haemangioma.
        - Lymphangioma.
      - Amyloid infiltration in amyloidosis.

  - Mouth ulcers
    - Important because systemic diseases may present with mouth ulcers.
    - Aphthous ulceration
      - Most common type.
      - Begins as a small painful vesicle on:
        - Tongue.
        - Mucosal surface of the mouth.
      - May break down into a painful, shallow ulcer.
      - Heals without scarring.
      - Cause completely unknown.
      - Usually does not indicate serious systemic disease.
      - May occur in:
        - Crohn's disease.
        - Coeliac disease.
    - HIV infection
      - May be associated with multiple mouth lesions.
    - Angular stomatitis
      - Cracks at the corners of the mouth.
      - Causes include deficiencies of:
        - Vitamin B6.
        - Vitamin B12.
        - Folate.
        - Iron.
    - Causes of mouth ulcers
      - Common:
        - Aphthous.
        - Trauma.
        - Drugs, e.g. steroids.
      - Uncommon:
        - Gastrointestinal disease:
          - Crohn's disease.
          - Ulcerative colitis.
          - Coeliac disease.
        - Rheumatological disease:
          - Behçet's syndrome.
          - Reiter's syndrome.
        - Erythema multiforme.
        - Infection:
          - Viral:
            - Herpes zoster.
            - Herpes simplex.
          - Bacterial:
            - Syphilis:
              - Primary chancre.
              - Secondary snail-track ulcers.
              - Mucous patches.
            - Tuberculosis.
        - Self-inflicted.

  - Candidiasis (moniliasis)
    - Fungal infection with Candida albicans.
    - Causes creamy white, curd-like patches in the mouth.
    - Patches are removed only with difficulty.
    - Removal leaves a bleeding surface.
    - May spread to the oesophagus.
      - Can cause:
        - Dysphagia.
        - Odynophagia.
    - Associated with immunosuppression:
      - Steroids.
      - Tumour chemotherapy.
      - Alcoholism.
      - HIV infection.
      - Haematological malignancy.
      - Other immunological abnormalities.
    - Broad-spectrum antibiotics
      - Inhibit normal oral flora.
      - Permit fungal overgrowth.
    - Other causes/associations:
      - Faulty oral hygiene.
      - Iron deficiency.
      - Diabetes mellitus.
    - Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis
      - Distinct syndrome comprising:
        - Recurrent or persistent oral thrush.
        - Fingernail or toenail-bed infection.
        - Skin involvement.
      - Usually results from T-cell immunodeficiency.
      - About half of patients have associated endocrine diseases:
        - Hypoparathyroidism.
        - Hypothyroidism.
        - Addison's disease.
- Neck and chest
  - Cervical lymph nodes
    - Palpate the cervical lymph nodes.
    - May be enlarged due to:
      - Infection in the mouth or pharynx.
      - Oropharyngeal malignancy.
    - Can be palpated:
      - From in front of the patient.
      - From behind the patient.
  - Supraclavicular lymph nodes
    - Palpate especially on the left side.
    - Best examined from in front after asking the patient to shrug the shoulders.
    - Common cause of palpable nodes in developing countries:
      - Tuberculosis.
    - May also be enlarged due to:
      - Advanced gastric malignancy.
      - Other gastrointestinal malignancy.
      - Lung cancer.
    - Virchow's node
      - Large left supraclavicular lymph node.
      - In combination with carcinoma of the stomach → Troisier's sign.
  - Spider naevi
    - Look for spider naevi.
  - Gynaecomastia
    - Enlargement of the male breasts.
    - May be a sign of chronic liver disease.
    - May be:
      - Unilateral.
      - Bilateral.
    - Breasts may be tender.
    - May occur with:
      - Cirrhosis, particularly alcoholic cirrhosis.
      - Chronic autoimmune hepatitis.
    - In chronic liver disease
      - Changes in the oestradiol-to-testosterone ratio may be responsible.
    - Spironolactone
      - Common cause in cirrhotic patients.
      - Used to treat ascites.
    - Alcohol
      - Gynaecomastia may occur in alcoholics without liver disease.
      - May result from alcohol-related damage to Leydig cells of the testis.
    - Other drugs may rarely cause gynaecomastia:
      - Digoxin.
      - Cimetidine.
- Abdomen
  - Inspection
    - Patient position
      - Lie flat.
      - One pillow under the head.
      - Abdomen exposed from nipples to pubic symphysis.
      - May expose this area in stages to preserve dignity.
    - General appearance
      - Assess whether the patient appears unwell.
      - Acute abdomen
        - Patient may lie very still.
        - Breathing may be shallow.
    - Abdominal scars
      - May indicate previous surgery or trauma.
      - Look around the umbilicus for laparoscopic surgical scars.
      - Older scars → white.
      - Recent scars → pink due to persistent vascularity.
      - Common scars:
        - Upper midline.
        - Right subcostal (Kocher's).
        - Right paramedian.
        - Lower midline.
        - Nephrectomy.
        - Appendicectomy (gridiron).
        - Transplanted kidney.
        - Suprapubic (Pfannenstiel).
        - Left inguinal.
        - Umbilical port → laparoscopic surgery.
      - Laparoscopic scars
        - Common.
        - Most procedures include a port approximately 2 cm long just above the umbilicus.
    - Stomata and fistulae
      - Look for:
        - End-colostomy.
        - Loop colostomy.
        - Ileostomy.
        - Ileal conduit.
        - Fistulae.
    - Striae
      - Visible abdominal striae may follow weight loss.
    - Generalised abdominal distension
      - Causes can begin with the sound "F":
        - Fat → gross obesity.
        - Fluid → ascites.
        - Fetus.
        - Flatus → gaseous distension due to bowel obstruction.
        - Faeces.
        - "Filthy" big tumour → e.g. ovarian tumour or hydatid cyst.
        - "Phantom" pregnancy.
      - Umbilicus
        - Shape may provide a clue to the underlying cause.
        - Buried in fat → suggests excessive food intake.
        - Large-volume ascites
          - Flanks and abdominal wall appear tense.
          - Umbilicus becomes shallow or everted.
          - Umbilicus points downwards.
        - Pregnancy
          - Umbilicus pushed upwards by the enlarging uterus.
        - Huge ovarian cyst
          - May also push the umbilicus upwards.
    - Local swellings
      - May indicate enlargement of an abdominal or pelvic organ.
    - Hernia
      - Protrusion of an intra-abdominal structure through an abnormal opening.
      - May result from:
        - Weakening of abdominal wall due to previous surgery → incisional hernia.
        - Congenital abdominal wall defect.
        - Chronically increased intra-abdominal pressure.
    - Prominent abdominal veins
      - If present, determine the direction of venous flow.
      - Technique
        - Use one finger to occlude the vein.
        - Use a second finger to empty blood from the vein below the occluding finger.
        - Remove the second finger.
        - If the vein refills → flow is towards the occluding finger.
      - Test veins separately:
        - Above the umbilicus.
        - Below the umbilicus.
      - Portal hypertension
        - Portal-to-systemic flow occurs through umbilical veins.
        - Umbilical veins may become engorged and distended.
        - Direction of flow → away from the umbilicus.
        - Caput Medusae
          - Engorged abdominal veins due to portal hypertension.
          - Very rare.
          - Usually only one or two veins, often epigastric, are visible.
      - Inferior vena caval obstruction
        - May be due to:
          - Tumour.
          - Thrombosis.
          - Tense ascites.
        - Abdominal veins enlarge to provide collateral flow from the legs, bypassing the blocked inferior vena cava.
        - Direction of flow → upwards towards the heart.
      - Distinguishing caput Medusae from inferior vena caval obstruction
        - Determine direction of flow below the umbilicus.
        - Portal hypertension → flow towards the legs.
        - Inferior vena caval obstruction → flow towards the head.
      - Prominent superficial veins
        - May occasionally be congenital.

- Percussion
  - Uses
    - Defines the size and nature of organs and masses.
    - Most useful for:
      - Detecting fluid in the peritoneal cavity.
      - Eliciting tenderness in patients with peritonitis.

- Liver
  - Percuss the liver borders routinely to determine the liver span.
  - If the liver edge is not palpable and there is no ascites:
    - Percuss the right side of the abdomen in the midclavicular line.
    - Percuss upwards to the right costal margin.
    - Continue until dullness is encountered.
    - Defines the lower border of the liver even when it is not palpable.
  - Upper border
    - Must always be defined by percussing down the midclavicular line.
  - Loss of normal liver dullness
    - May occur in:
      - Massive hepatic necrosis.
      - Free gas in the peritoneal cavity, e.g. perforated bowel.

- Spleen
  - Percussion over the left costal margin is more sensitive than palpation for detecting splenic enlargement.
  - Technique
    - Percuss over the lowest intercostal space in the left anterior axillary line.
    - Perform during:
      - Inspiration.
      - Expiration.
    - Patient supine.
  - Splenomegaly should be suspected if:
    - Percussion note is dull.
    - Percussion note becomes dull on complete inspiration.
  - If percussion note is dull:
    - Repeat palpation.

- Kidneys
  - Percussion over a right or left subcostal mass can help distinguish hepatic or splenic masses from renal masses.
  - Renal mass
    - Usually has a resonant area due to overlying bowel.
    - A very large renal mass may displace the overlying bowel.

- Bladder
  - Suprapubic dullness
    - Indicates the upper border of:
      - An enlarged bladder.
      - Pelvic mass.

- Ascites
  - Normal abdomen
    - Percussion note over most of the abdomen is resonant due to air in the intestines.
    - Resonance extends to the flanks.
  - Development of ascites
    - Peritoneal fluid accumulates first in the flanks in a supine patient due to gravity.
    - Relatively early sign:
      - Dull percussion note in the flanks.
      - Usually when at least 2 L of fluid has accumulated.
  - Gross ascites
    - Abdomen distends.
    - Flanks bulge.
    - Umbilical eversion occurs.
    - Dullness extends closer to the midline.
    - Central resonance persists.
  - Routine percussion
    - Start in the midline.
    - Finger points towards the feet.
    - Percuss towards each flank.
  - Shifting dullness
    - Perform if, and only if, flank dullness is detected.
    - Technique
      - Stand on the right side of the bed.
      - Percuss towards the left flank until dullness is reached.
      - Mark the point.
      - Roll the patient towards you.
      - Wait 30 seconds to 1 minute for fluid to move within the abdominal cavity.
      - Repeat percussion over the marked point.
    - Positive shifting dullness
      - Previously dull area becomes resonant.
      - Due to peritoneal fluid moving under gravity to the right side when it becomes the lowermost point.
    - Possible confounders
      - Fluid and air in dilated small bowel in small-intestinal obstruction.
      - Massive ovarian cyst filling the abdomen.
    - Shifting dullness
      - Has good sensitivity.
      - Has good specificity.
  - Fluid thrill
    - Ask an assistant or the patient to place the medial edges of both palms firmly on the centre of the abdomen.
    - Fingers point towards each other.
    - Flick the side of the abdominal wall.
    - A pulsation (thrill) is felt by the hand on the opposite abdominal wall.
    - More valuable when there is massive ascites.
    - May also occur with:
      - Massive ovarian cyst.
      - Pregnancy with hydramnios.
  - Other clinical signs
    - Bulging flanks
      - Good sensitivity and specificity for detecting ascites.
    - Ankle oedema
      - Increases the likelihood of ascites.
  - Ascites and abdominal masses
    - Significant ascites may make abdominal masses difficult to feel by direct palpation.
    - Dipping
      - Place the hand flat on the abdomen.
      - Rapidly flex the fingers at the metacarpophalangeal joints.
      - Displaces the underlying fluid.
      - Allows the fingers to reach a mass covered in ascitic fluid.
      - Particularly useful for palpating:
        - Enlarged liver.
        - Enlarged spleen.
    - Liver and spleen may become ballottable when gross ascites is present.

- Classification of ascites by serum–ascites albumin concentration gradient
  - High gradient >11 g/L
    - Cirrhosis.
    - Alcoholic hepatitis.
    - Budd–Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis).
    - Veno-occlusive disease.
    - Fulminant hepatic failure.
    - Congestive heart failure.
    - Constrictive pericarditis (cardiac ascites).
  - Low gradient <11 g/L
    - Peritoneal carcinomatosis.
    - Tuberculosis.
    - Pancreatic ascites.
    - Nephrotic syndrome.
  - High serum-to-ascites albumin gradient
    - Most often associated with portal hypertension.
    - 97% accuracy.

- Auscultation
  - Bowel sounds
    - Place the diaphragm of the stethoscope just below the umbilicus.
    - Can be heard over all parts of the abdomen in healthy people.
    - Poorly localised.
    - Little value in listening in more than one place.
    - Sources
      - Most originate in the stomach.
      - Some originate in the large bowel.
      - Remaining sounds originate in the small bowel.
    - Character
      - Soft, gurgling.
      - Occur intermittently.
    - Description
      - Describe as:
        - Present.
        - Absent.
      - "Decreased" or "increased" are meaningless because sounds vary depending on when a meal was last eaten.
    - Complete absence
      - Complete absence of bowel sounds over 4 minutes indicates paralytic ileus.
      - Paralytic ileus → complete absence of peristalsis in a paralysed bowel.
      - As only liquid is present in the gut, heart sounds may be audible over the abdomen due to transmission through dilated bowel.
    - Obstructed bowel sounds
      - Obstructed bowel produces louder, higher-pitched sounds.
      - Have a tinkling quality.
      - Due to presence of:
        - Air.
        - Liquid.
      - Presence of normal bowel sounds makes obstruction unlikely.
    - Borborygmi
      - Occur with intestinal hurry or rush in diarrhoeal states.
      - Loud gurgling sounds.
      - May be audible without a stethoscope.

- Friction rubs
  - Indicate abnormality of the parietal and visceral peritoneum due to inflammation.
  - Very rare and non-specific.
  - May be audible over:
    - Liver.
    - Spleen.
  - Character
    - Rough, creaking or grating noise.
    - Heard as the patient breathes.
  - Hepatic causes
    - Hepatocellular cancer.
    - Liver metastases.
    - Liver abscess.
    - Recent liver biopsy.
    - Liver infarct.
    - Gonococcal or chlamydial perihepatitis.
      - Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome.
  - Splenic rub
    - Indicates splenic infarct.

- Venous hums
  - Continuous, low-pitched, soft murmur.
  - May:
    - Become louder with inspiration.
    - Diminish when more pressure is applied to the stethoscope.
  - Typically heard between:
    - Xiphisternum.
    - Umbilicus.
  - Usually associated with portal hypertension.
  - Rare.
  - May radiate:
    - To the chest.
    - Over the liver.
  - Cause
    - Large volumes of blood flowing in the umbilical or paraumbilical veins in the falciform ligament.
    - These veins channel blood from the left portal vein to:
      - Epigastric veins.
      - Internal mammary veins.
  - May occasionally be heard over:
    - Inferior mesenteric vein.
    - After portacaval shunting.
  - Thrill
    - May occasionally be detectable over the site of maximum intensity.
  - Cruveilhier–Baumgarten syndrome
    - Association of:
      - Venous hum at the umbilicus.
      - Dilated abdominal wall veins.
    - Almost always due to cirrhosis of the liver.
    - Occurs when a patent umbilical vein allows portal-to-systemic shunting.
  - Venous hum or prominent central abdominal veins
    - Suggest portal obstruction is intrahepatic rather than in the portal vein itself.

- Bruits
  - Hepatic bruit
    - Arterial systolic bruit may occasionally be heard over the liver.
    - Higher pitched than a venous hum.
    - Not continuous.
    - Well localised.
    - Usually due to:
      - Hepatocellular cancer.
    - May also occur with:
      - Acute alcoholic hepatitis.
      - Arteriovenous malformation.
      - Transiently after liver biopsy.
  - Renal bruits
    - Auscultate on either side of the midline above the umbilicus.
    - Indicated when renal artery stenosis is suspected.
  - Epigastric bruit
    - May occur with chronic intestinal ischaemia due to mesenteric arterial stenosis.
    - May also occur without pathology.
  - Splenic bruit
    - May occasionally be audible over the spleen with:
      - Tumour of the body of the pancreas.
      - Splenic arteriovenous fistula.

- Scratch test
  - Helps identify the inferior liver border when the abdomen is:
    - Very tender.
    - Tense.
    - Distended.
  - Technique
    - Place the diaphragm of the stethoscope below the xiphoid.
    - Lightly but briskly stroke the skin.
    - Stroke at right angles to the expected liver edge.
    - Start at the right lower quadrant.
    - Slowly move upwards to the right costal margin along the midclavicular line.
    - When the liver edge is reached:
      - Scratch sound is transmitted to the stethoscope.
    - Point of initial sound transmission
      - Most helpful for identifying the liver edge.
      - Initial rather than maximal sound transmission.
  - Accuracy
    - Test has been controversial.
    - Reliable with moderate accuracy compared with abdominal ultrasound.

- Rectal examination
  - Indications
    - Abdominal examination is not complete without rectal examination.
    - Consider in all patients with bowel symptoms admitted to hospital who are aged ≥40 years.
    - Contraindications / exceptions
      - No fingers.
      - No anus.
      - Acute illness such as myocardial infarction causing temporary contraindication.
  - Preparation
    - Obtain patient's permission.
    - Introduce a chaperone if indicated.
    - Ensure privacy throughout.
    - Explain:
      - What will happen.
      - Why the examination is necessary.
    - Position
      - Left lateral position:
        - Patient lies on left side.
        - Knees drawn up.
        - Back towards examiner.
      - Standing, bent-over position
        - May provide good information about the prostate.
        - Makes assessment of rectal function more difficult.
  - Inspection
    - Don gloves.
    - Separate the buttocks.
    - Inspect anus and perianal area.
    - Thrombosed external haemorrhoids
      - Small (<1 cm), tense, bluish swellings.
      - Usually seen on one side of the anal margin.
      - Painful.
      - Due to rupture of a vein in the external haemorrhoidal plexus.
      - Also called perianal haematomas.
    - Skin tags
      - Appear as tags elsewhere on the body.
      - May be:
        - Incidental.
        - Associated with haemorrhoids.
        - Associated with Crohn's disease.
    - Rectal prolapse
      - Circumferential folds of red mucosa protruding from the anus.
      - May become apparent only when the patient strains.
      - Mucosal prolapse
        - Radial folds.
      - Complete prolapse
        - Concentric folds.
      - Prolapsed mass:
        - Continuous with perianal skin.
        - Usually painless.
      - Mucosal rectal prolapse
        - Prolapsed mucosa can be felt between examiner's thumb and forefinger.
      - Gaping anus
        - Suggests loss of internal and external sphincter tone.
        - May coexist with prolapse.
    - Anal fissure
      - Crack in the anal wall.
      - May be sufficiently painful to prevent digital rectal examination.
      - Usually occurs:
        - Directly posteriorly.
        - In the midline.
      - Sentinel pile
        - Skin tag at the base of the fissure.
        - Indicates chronic fissure.
      - May need the patient to bear down for the fissure to become visible.
      - Multiple or broad-based fissures
        - May occur with:
          - Inflammatory bowel disease.
          - Malignancy.
          - Sexually transmitted disease.
    - Fistula-in-ano
      - External opening may be visible.
      - Usually within 4 cm of the anus.
      - Opening has a red, pouting appearance due to granulation tissue.
      - May occur with:
        - Crohn's disease.
        - Perianal abscess.
    - Condylomata acuminata
      - Anal warts.
      - May be confused with skin tags.
      - Actually pedunculated papillomas.
      - White surface with red base.
      - May surround the anus.
    - Anal carcinoma
      - May be visible as a fungating mass at the anal verge.
    - Pruritus ani
      - Appearance varies from:
        - Weeping red dermatitis.
        - Thickened white skin.
      - Usually caused by faecal soiling.
  - Straining
    - Ask the patient to strain.
    - Observe the perineum for:
      - Incontinence.
      - Leakage of faeces.
      - Leakage of mucus.
      - Abnormal descent of the perineum (>4 cm).
      - Patulous anus.
    - Gaping anus
      - Often correlates with lower resting pressures on anorectal manometry.
    - Internal haemorrhoids
      - Can prolapse in:
        - Right anterior position.
        - Right posterior position.
        - Left lateral position.
  - Anal wink
    - Stroke a cotton pad in all four quadrants around the anus.
    - Normal response
      - Brisk anal contraction.
      - Indicates intact sacral nerve pathways.
    - Weak response
      - Can occur in healthy people.
    - Complete absence
      - Particularly with faecal incontinence.
      - Suggests a spinal cord problem.
      - Requires:
        - More detailed neurological examination.
        - Consideration of further investigations.
  - Digital rectal examination
    - Lubricate the tip of the gloved right index finger.
    - Place it over the anus.
    - Ask the patient to breathe quietly through the mouth.
      - Helps relaxation.
      - Provides distraction.
    - Severe pain at the start
      - Strongly suggests anal fissure.
      - Abandon the remainder of the rectal examination.
      - Fissure can often be seen on inspection.
      - Lignocaine jelly may allow completion of the examination.
      - Usually preferable to perform anoscopy under appropriate sedation.
    - Other causes of significant anal pain
      - Recently thrombosed external haemorrhoids.
      - Ischiorectal abscess.
      - Active proctitis.
      - Anal ulceration from another cause.
    - Finger insertion
      - Unless painful, slowly increase pressure with the pulp of the finger.
      - Allow the sphincter to relax slightly.
      - Slowly advance the finger into the rectum.
    - Sphincter tone
      - Assess as:
        - Normal.
        - Reduced.
      - Resting tone
        - Predominantly attributable to the internal anal sphincter.
        - Internal anal sphincter contributes approximately 70–80%.
      - Reduced sphincter tone
        - May indicate a sphincter tear.
      - High anal resting tone
        - May contribute to difficulties with evacuation.
  - Palpation
    - Anterior rectal wall
      - Male → palpate prostate.
      - Female → palpate cervix.
    - Prostate
      - Normal:
        - Firm.
        - Rubbery.
        - Bilobed.
        - Central furrow.
      - Becomes firmer with age.
      - Prostatic enlargement:
        - Central sulcus becomes obliterated.
        - Gland often asymmetrical.
      - Prostatic carcinoma:
        - Very hard nodule.
      - Prostatitis:
        - Boggy.
        - Tender.
    - Mass above prostate or cervix
      - May indicate metastatic deposit on Blumer's shelf.
    - Rectal walls
      - Rotate finger clockwise to palpate sequentially:
        - Left lateral wall.
        - Posterior wall.
        - Right lateral wall.
      - Advance finger as high as possible.
      - Slowly withdraw along the rectal wall.
    - Palpable masses
      - Rectal carcinoma.
      - Rectal polyp.
      - Hypertrophied anal papilla.
      - Diverticular phlegmon:
        - Recent.
        - Old.
      - Sigmoid colon carcinoma prolapsing into the pouch of Douglas.
      - Metastatic pelvic deposits → Blumer's shelf.
      - Uterine malignancy.
      - Ovarian malignancy.
      - Prostatic malignancy → direct extension.
      - Cervical malignancy → direct extension.
      - Endometriosis.
      - Pelvic abscess.
      - Sarcoma.
      - Amoebic granuloma.
      - Foreign body.
      - Faeces
        - Can be palpable.
        - Also indent.

- Pelvic floor — special tests for pelvic floor dysfunction
  - Test 1: Straining
    - Ask the patient to strain and try to push out the examining finger.
    - Normal response
      - Anal sphincter relaxes.
      - Puborectalis relaxes.
      - Perineum descends by 1–3.5 cm.
    - Pelvic floor dyssynergia (anismus)
      - Paradoxical contraction of:
        - External anal sphincter.
        - Puborectalis.
      - Particularly suggestive when there is no perineal descent.
      - These contractions block normal defecation.
    - Rectocele
      - Ask the patient to strain again with the finger rotated anteriorly.
      - A rectocele may be palpable.
      - Rectocele → defect in the anterior wall of the rectum.

  - Test 2: Posterior rectal wall tenderness
    - Press on the posterior rectal wall.
    - Ask whether this causes pain.
    - Pain suggests puborectalis muscle tenderness.
    - Puborectalis tenderness can occur in pelvic floor dyssynergia.

  - Test 3: Pelvic floor contraction
    - Ask the patient to contract or squeeze the pelvic floor muscles.
    - Assess contraction of:
      - Anal sphincter.
      - Puborectalis.
    - Puborectalis contraction
      - Perceived as a "lift".
      - Muscle lifts the examining finger towards the umbilicus.
    - Faecal incontinence
      - Many patients cannot augment anal pressure when asked to squeeze.

  - Test 4: Abdominal wall contraction during straining
    - Place the other hand on the anterior abdominal wall.
    - Ask the patient to strain again.
    - Assess for excessive abdominal wall contraction.
    - May indicate:
      - Inappropriate Valsalva manoeuvre.
      - Excessive pelvic floor muscle contraction.
    - These may impede evacuation.
    - Exact value of this test is unclear.

  - Clinical significance
    - Constipation due to pelvic floor dysfunction
      - Responds to biofeedback in about 70% of cases.
      - May result in a laxative-free existence for patients with troubling outlet constipation.
    - Pelvic floor dysfunction
      - Diagnosis should be considered in all patients with chronic constipation.
      - Good rectal examination can help determine whether anorectal manometry is warranted.

- Ending the rectal examination
  - Withdraw the examining finger.
  - Inspect the glove for:
    - Bright blood.
    - Melaena.
    - Mucus.
    - Pus.
  - Note the colour of the faeces.
  - Haemorrhoids
    - Not palpable unless thrombosed.
  - Persistent gaping of the anal canal after withdrawal
    - May indicate external anal sphincter denervation.

- Testing stools for blood
  - May be considered when assessing:
    - Anaemia.
    - Iron deficiency.
    - Gastrointestinal bleeding.
    - Symptoms suggesting colonic cancer.
    - Colon cancer screening in asymptomatic people.
  - Guaiac test
    - Stool placed on guaiac-impregnated paper.
    - Blood causes phenolytic oxidation.
    - Positive result → blue colour.
  - Limitations
    - Both false-positive and false-negative results occur.
  - False-positive results
    - Peroxidase and catalase in:
      - Fresh fruit.
      - Uncooked vegetables.
    - Haem in red meat.
    - Aspirin.
    - Anticoagulants.
    - Oral iron.
  - False-negative results
    - Vitamin C can reduce the sensitivity of guaiac testing.
    - Vitamin C should not be taken before testing.
    - Colorectal neoplasms may bleed intermittently.
  - Clinical implication
    - Testing faecal occult blood from the glove after rectal examination is of little value.
    - More sensitive and specific testing, e.g. colonoscopy, is required depending on the clinical setting.

- Other examination
  - Legs
    - Examine for:
      - Bruising.
      - Oedema.
    - May result from liver disease.
  - Neurological examination
    - Look for neurological signs of alcoholism:
      - Coarse tremor.
    - Look for evidence of thiamine deficiency:
      - Peripheral neuropathy.
      - Memory loss.
  - Cardiovascular examination
    - May be helpful in patients with hepatomegaly.
    - Cardiac failure is a common cause of liver enlargement.
    - Cardiac failure can even cause cirrhosis.
  - Temperature
    - Important to measure, especially:
      - In acute abdominal cases.
      - When infection is suspected.
  - Lymph nodes, breasts and chest
    - Examine all lymph node groups, breasts and chest particularly carefully when there is evidence of malignant disease.
    - Example:
      - Firm, irregular hepatomegaly.

- Examination of the gastrointestinal contents
  - Faeces
    - Never miss an opportunity to inspect the patient's faeces.
    - Considerable information about the gastrointestinal tract can be obtained.

  - Melaena
    - Stools:
      - Poorly formed.
      - Black.
      - Tarry appearance.
      - Characteristic offensive smell.
    - Cause
      - Blood digested by:
        - Gastric acid.
        - Colonic bacteria.
    - Usually indicates bleeding from:
      - Oesophagus.
      - Stomach.
      - Duodenum.
    - Most common cause:
      - Acute or chronic peptic ulceration.
    - Less common causes:
      - Right-sided colonic bleeding.
      - Small-bowel bleeding → rare.
    - Differential diagnosis of dark stools:
      - Iron tablets.
      - Bismuth.
      - Liquorice.
      - Charcoal.
    - Dark stools due to these substances:
      - Small.
      - Well formed.
      - Non-tarry.
      - Offensive smell absent.

  - Bright-red blood (haematochezia)
    - Usually results from haemorrhage from:
      - Rectum.
      - Left colon.
    - Beetroot ingestion can cause confusion.
    - Causes of blood loss:
      - Carcinoma.
      - Polyp.
      - Arteriovenous malformation.
      - Inflammatory bowel disease.
      - Diverticular disease.
    - Can occasionally occur with massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding.
    - Blood mixed with bowel motion
      - Usually when bleeding originates above the anorectum.
    - Blood on surface of stool or only on toilet paper
      - Suggests, but does not guarantee, a local rectal cause.
      - Examples:
        - Internal haemorrhoids.
        - Anal fissure.
    - Dark-red jelly-like stools
      - May occur with ischaemic bowel.

  - Steatorrhoea
    - Stools:
      - Very pale.
      - Offensive.
      - Smelly.
      - Bulky.
      - Float.
      - Difficult to flush away.
    - Most common cause of floating stools
      - Gas and water rather than fat.
    - Cause
      - Malabsorption of fat.
    - Severe pancreatic disease
      - Oil (triglycerides) may be passed per rectum.
      - Virtually pathognomonic of pancreatic steatorrhoea.
      - Due to lipase deficiency.
    - Orlistat
      - Can cause fat malabsorption.
      - Oil may be seen in the stool.

  - "Toothpaste" stools
    - Faeces expressed like toothpaste from a tube.
    - Usually due to:
      - Severe constipation with overflow diarrhoea.
    - May also occur with:
      - Irritable bowel syndrome.
      - Stricture.
      - Hirschsprung's disease.

  - Rice-water stools
    - Cholera
      - Causes massive excretion of fluid and electrolytes from the bowel.
      - Produces severe secretory diarrhoea.
      - Stools:
        - Pale.
        - Watery.
        - Enormous volume.
        - Contain mucous debris.

- Vomitus
  - If vomitus is available for inspection, perform a detailed examination.
  - "Coffee grounds"
    - Old blood clot in vomitus.
    - Appearance resembles the dregs of espresso coffee.
    - Dark vomitus is often described as having this appearance.
    - Personal inspection is important.
    - Other substances can produce a similar appearance:
      - Iron tablets.
      - Red wine.
      - Coffee.
  - Bright-red blood (haematemesis)
    - Look for fresh clot.
    - Usually indicates fresh bleeding from the upper gastrointestinal tract.
  - Yellow-green vomitus
    - Results from vomiting:
      - Bile.
      - Upper small-bowel contents.
    - Often occurs with obstruction.
  - Faeculent vomiting
    - Brown, offensive material from the small bowel is vomited.
    - Late sign of small-intestinal obstruction.
    - Recently ingested tea can have a similar appearance but lacks the smell.
  - Brownish-black fluid
    - Large volumes may be vomited in acute gastric dilatation.
    - Succussion splash usually present.
    - Acute gastric dilatation may occur with:
      - Diabetic ketoacidosis.
      - Following abdominal surgery.
    - Medical emergency
      - Risk of aspiration.
      - Urgent placement of a nasogastric tube required.
  - Projectile vomiting
    - Describes the act of vomiting itself.
    - May indicate:
      - Pyloric stenosis.
      - Raised intracranial pressure.

- Urinalysis
  - Urine testing can be helpful in diagnosing liver disease.
  - Strip colour tests can detect:
    - Bilirubin.
    - Urobilinogen.
  - False-positive or false-negative results may occur with:
    - Vitamin C.
    - Exposure to sunlight.

- Bilirubin and urobilinogen metabolism
  - Red blood cells
    - Broken down by the reticuloendothelial system.
    - Haem is released.
    - Haem → biliverdin → unconjugated bilirubin.
  - Unconjugated bilirubin
    - Water-insoluble.
    - Therefore does not appear in urine.
    - Haemolytic anaemia
      - Increased unconjugated bilirubin.
      - No urinary bilirubin.
      - Termed acholuric jaundice.
    - Transported in blood:
      - Mainly bound to albumin.
      - Also bound to other plasma proteins.
  - Liver
    - Unconjugated bilirubin is taken up by hepatocytes.
    - Transported to the endoplasmic reticulum.
    - Glucuronyl transferases conjugate bilirubin with glucuronide.
    - Forms conjugated bilirubin.
  - Conjugated bilirubin
    - Water-soluble.
    - Concentrated and excreted by hepatocytes into the canaliculus.
    - Virtually all enters the small bowel.
  - Intestine
    - Conjugated bilirubin reaches the small bowel.
    - Converted in the terminal ileum and colon to:
      - Urobilinogen.
      - Stercobilin.
    - Stercobilin
      - Responsible for the normal colour of stools together with other non-bilirubinoid dietary pigments.
    - Urobilinogen
      - Up to 20% is reabsorbed by the bowel.
      - Small amounts are excreted in urine.
      - Urinary urobilinogen can normally be detected by reagent strips.

- Urinary findings in biliary obstruction
  - Total biliary obstruction
    - No conjugated bilirubin reaches the bowel.
    - No urobilinogen is formed.
    - Urinary urobilinogen → absent.
    - Stercobilin absent.
    - Stools → pale.
    - Conjugated bilirubin cannot be excreted through the biliary system.
    - Conjugated bilirubin leaks from hepatocytes into the blood.
    - Excreted by the kidneys into urine.
    - Urinary bilirubin → increased.
    - Urine → dark.
    - Normally, bilirubin is not detected in urine.

- Acute liver damage
  - Example:
    - Viral hepatitis.
  - May initially cause:
    - Increased urinary urobilinogen.
  - Mechanism
    - Liver is unable to re-excrete urobilinogen that has been reabsorbed from the bowel.
