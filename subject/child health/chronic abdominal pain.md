## Key Points
- In most children, no organic cause is found  
- Non-specific or functional abdominal pain is a distinct diagnosis and does not require exclusion of all organic causes  
- Many children do not need investigations. Investigations are guided by red flags or likely diagnosis. Imaging is rarely required  
- Significant functional impairment should prompt consideration of referral to a specialist  
## Background
- Chronic abdominal pain is intermittent or constant abdominal pain that has been present for at least two months  
- Non-specific abdominal pain that self-resolves over time without specific treatment is very common in primary school aged children  
- The aetiology may be **organic** or **functional**. These are not mutually exclusive; they can exist alongside each other and interact  
	- Organic: primary gastrointestinal disorder or non-gastrointestinal disease  
	- functional: without any readily identifiable organic cause  
- Comorbid mental health issues (including [[anxiety disorders]] and [[depression]]) are very common in children with chronic abdominal pain. These may both result from, and contribute to, the experience of chronic pain  
## Assessment
### History
- Pain characteristics (location, time course, triggers, association with meals, pain waking the child from sleep)  
- Associated symptoms:  
  - unintentional weight loss/stunting of height  
  - unexplained fever  
  - changes in bowel habit, chronic diarrhoea, blood in stools  
  - nocturnal stooling  
  - appetite changes  
  - dysphagia, odynophagia  
  - persistent vomiting (esp. bilious)  
  - urinary symptoms  
  - extraintestinal manifestations of IBD (eyes, skin, joints, perianal)  
- History of reflux symptoms  
- Dietary history  
- Menstrual/sexual history if relevant  
- Family history of IBD or coeliac disease  
- HEADSSS screen  
- Functional impact (school, social, physical activity)  
### Examination
- Signs of dehydration  
- Weight and height, preferably over time  
- Pubertal status  
- Abdominal examination for palpable faeces or mass  
- Extraintestinal manifestations of IBD  
## Differential Diagnosis
### Organic Causes
More likely if there are red flags  

| Condition | Key features |
|-----------|--------------|
| **[[constipation]]** | ≤2 stools/week, painful or hard bowel movements |
| **GORD** | Regurgitation, dysphagia, waterbrash, pain relieved by eating |
| **IBD** | Diarrhoea (± blood/mucus), fever |
| **Coeliac disease** | Altered bowel habit, poor weight gain, anaemia, bloating, family history |
| **Parasitic infection (Giardia)** | Loose, pale, greasy foul-smelling stools, bloating, nausea, anorexia |
| **DKA / Type 1 [[DM]] presentation** | Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss |
| **Gynaecological** | Pain associated with menstrual cycle |

### Functional Causes

| Condition                       | Key features                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Irritable bowel syndrome**    | Pain ≥4 days/month, associated with bowel habit change, pain with defecation                      |
| **Abdominal [[migraine]]**      | Paroxysmal periumbilical/diffuse pain, few/no GI complaints between attacks, [[migraine]] history |
| **Functional dyspepsia**        | Postprandial fullness, early satiety, epigastric pain/burning not stool-related                   |
| **Non-specific abdominal pain** | No organic cause, episodic/continuous, no stool frequency/consistency change                      |
| **Somatic symptom disorder**    | High focus on physical symptoms, distress/functional impairment, incongruent exam/investigation   |

## Management

### Investigations
- Many children do not need investigations  
- Investigations should be targeted to likely diagnoses  

**Possible investigations (guided by presentation):**
- **Urinalysis** ± culture, pregnancy test, STI screen if indicated  
- **Stool**: M/C/S, ova, cysts, parasites  
- **Faecal calprotectin** (children >4y with IBD features; avoid under 4 unless discussed with specialist)  
- **Bloods:** UEC, LFTs, CMP, lipase, TSH, blood sugar, coeliac serology + total IgA, [[ESR]], CRP, FBE, ferritin  
- **Imaging:**  
  - Not routinely required  
  - Abdominal ultrasound: low yield; may help exclude intra-[[abdominal mass]]  
  - Avoid abdominal X-rays (rarely useful for [[constipation]])  
  - CT/MRI/endoscopy: rarely required, seek specialist advice  
- **H. pylori testing:** Only if red flags for peptic ulcer disease (eg haematemesis, family history). Do not use serology

## Treatment

Treatment is targeted to the underlying cause

### [[constipation]]
- if older children and adolescents with reflux symptoms ==> a 4-week trial of proton pump inhibitor (PPI) ==> if not responding ==> referral may be helpful.  
### If IBD is suspected
- refer to paediatric gastroenterology.  
### Non-specific or functional abdominal pain
- This is a distinct diagnosis and does not require exclusion of all organic causes.  
- Red flags should be considered, but children may not need investigations to make this diagnosis.  
- Often multi-disciplinary input is required.  
#### For non-specific, suspected functional abdominal pain:
- Education and reassurance for the child and parents (there is no physical damage to treat, and most cases resolve with time and support).  
- Further investigations and medications are usually not required.  
- Consider and address psychosocial stressors.  
- Consider comorbid [[anxiety disorders]] or [[depression]]. If present, discuss with the child/young person and family and consider referral to mental health team or psychologist.  
- Encourage focus on return to function: continuing or graded return to usual activities (school, social, extra-curricular, physical activity).  
- Provide follow-up.  
#### For specific functional abdominal pain disorders (IBS, abdominal [[migraine]], functional dyspepsia):
Referral to a specialist for consideration of evidence-based treatments, which may include:  
- Psychology / Cognitive Behavioural Therapy / counselling  
- Specialist dietician input to discuss evidence-based dietary strategies  
  - Changes to diet should not be undertaken without professional advice  
- Gut-directed hypnotherapy  
- Use of medications such as antidepressants (SSRI/low dose TCA) and antispasmodics  
If there is significant functional impairment (eg poor school attendance, concurrent [[depression]] or [[anxiety disorders]], sleep disturbance), consider referral to:  
- Paediatrician  
- Adolescent medicine  
- Paediatric chronic pain management service  
### Consider consultation with local paediatric team when
- Child requiring admission  
- Red flags or abnormal investigations  
- Abdominal pain causing significant interruption to usual activities (schooling, social interactions, eating) → consider referral to general paediatrics, adolescent medicine or paediatric chronic pain management service  
### Consider transfer when
- Child requires care beyond the comfort level of the local hospital  
_For emergency advice and paediatric or neonatal ICU transfers, see Retrieval Services._  
### Consider discharge when
- There are no concerning clinical features and red flags have been considered **AND**  
- A clear follow-up plan has been arranged, often with either general paediatrics, adolescent medicine or paediatric chronic pain management service
