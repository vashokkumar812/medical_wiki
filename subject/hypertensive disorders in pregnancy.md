- Blood pressure changes in pregnancy

	- Normal physiological changes
		- BP decreases in 1st trimester
		- Further decreases in 2nd trimester
		- Returns to prepregnancy levels by term

- Hypertension in pregnancy

	- Definition
		- SBP ≥140 mmHg
		- DBP ≥90 mmHg
			- Korotkoff phase 5

	- Additional significance
		- Rise >30 mmHg systolic
		- Rise >15 mmHg diastolic

		- Especially important if associated with
			- Hyperuricaemia
			- Proteinuria
			- SGA fetus

- Severe hypertension (obstetric emergency)

	- Definition
		- SBP ≥160 mmHg
		- ± DBP ≥110 mmHg

	- Complications
		- Cerebral haemorrhage
		- Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)
		- Hypertensive encephalopathy

- Blood pressure recording

	- Confirmation
		- Repeat readings after several hours

	- Correct patient position
		- Seated
		- Legs supported
		- Arm at heart level

	- During labour
		- Lateral recumbent acceptable

	- Avoid
		- Supine position
			- Risk of supine hypotension

	- Inter-arm difference
		- Usually <10 mmHg

	- Korotkoff sounds
		- Systolic
			- K1 (first sound)
		- Diastolic
			- K5 (disappearance)

		- If K5 absent
			- Use K4 (muffling)

	- Cuff size
		- Standard cuff
			- Bladder covers 80% arm

		- Arm circumference 33–44 cm
			- Large cuff

		- >44 cm
			- Thigh cuff

	- Deflation rate
		- ≤2 mmHg/sec

- BP measurement devices

	- Mercury sphygmomanometer
		- Gold standard
		- Limited use
			- Mercury exposure concerns

	- Automated BP monitors
		- Useful for home monitoring
		- Limitation
			- Unreliable in preeclampsia

	- Validated devices in pregnancy
		- Microlife WatchBP Home
		- Microlife 3BTO-A
		- Omron T9P

	- Devices not recommended in preeclampsia
		- Microlife 3BTO-A
		- Omron M7

	- Alternatives
		- Nonmercury auditory devices
			- Acceptable if trained user

		- Aneroid devices
			- Prone to error

	- Recommendation
		- Each unit should maintain one mercury device for calibration

- Ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM)

	- Role
		- Diagnose white coat hypertension

	- Timing
		- Useful in early pregnancy (<20 weeks)

	- Note
		- Normal ranges vary with gestation

- Classification of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy

	- Preeclampsia
		- Multisystem disorder unique to pregnancy
		- Characterised by
			- Hypertension
			- Maternal organ involvement and/or fetal involvement

	- Eclampsia
		- Generalised tonic-clonic seizures and/or coma
		- Occurring in woman with preeclampsia
		- No alternative cause

	- Gestational hypertension
		- New hypertension after 20 weeks
		- No preeclampsia features
		- BP normalises within 3 months postpartum

	- Chronic hypertension
		- Present before pregnancy
		- OR before 20 weeks gestation

		- Includes
			- Essential hypertension
			- Secondary hypertension

	- White coat hypertension
		- Elevated clinic BP
		- Normal ABPM/home BP

		- Risk
			- Lower than essential hypertension
			- Higher than normotensive women

	- Preeclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension
		- Chronic hypertension + new preeclampsia features after 20 weeks

- Preeclampsia

	- Definition
		- Multisystem disorder unique to pregnancy
		- Characterised by
			- Hypertension
			- Maternal organ involvement and/or fetal involvement

	- Important points
		- Raised BP usually first sign
		- Proteinuria common but NOT mandatory

- Diagnostic criteria

	- Diagnosis
		- Clinical diagnosis

	- First sign
		- Increased BP

- Maternal organ involvement

	- Renal involvement
		- Proteinuria
			- Spot PCR ≥30 mg/mmol

		- Serum creatinine >90 µmol/L

		- Oliguria
			- <80 mL urine over 4 hours

		- Uric acid
			- NOT diagnostic

	- Haematological involvement
		- Thrombocytopenia
			- Platelets <100,000/µL

		- Haemolysis
			- Schistocytes/fragmented RBCs
			- Increased bilirubin
			- LDH >600 mIU/L
			- Reduced haptoglobin

		- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

	- Liver involvement
		- Raised transaminases
		- Epigastric pain
		- Right upper quadrant pain

	- Neurological involvement
		- Eclampsia
		- Hyperreflexia with sustained clonus
		- Persistent headache
		- Visual disturbance
			- Photopsia
			- Scotomata
			- Cortical blindness
			- Retinal vasospasm

		- PRES
		- Stroke

	- Pulmonary involvement
		- Pulmonary oedema

	- Fetal involvement
		- Fetal growth restriction (FGR)

- Severity of preeclampsia

	- Features suggesting severe disease
		- Difficult-to-control hypertension
		- HELLP syndrome
		- Impending eclampsia
		- Worsening thrombocytopenia
		- Worsening fetal growth restriction

	- Important note
		- Increasing proteinuria alone does NOT indicate severity

- Conditions mimicking preeclampsia
	- Acute fatty liver of pregnancy
	- Haemolytic uraemic syndrome
	- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
	- SLE flare
	- Cholecystitis

- Early-onset preeclampsia (<20 weeks)

	- Rare

	- Associated with
		- Hydatidiform mole
		- Multiple pregnancy
		- Fetal triploidy
		- Severe renal disease
		- Antiphospholipid syndrome

- Proteinuria assessment

	- Dipstick
		- 2+ or 3+ significant
		- Repeated 1+ results
			- Assume significant until confirmed

	- 24-hour urine protein
		- Historical gold standard

	- Spot urine PCR
		- Recommended test
		- Diagnostic cut-off
			- ≥30 mg/mmol
			- Equivalent to ≥300 mg/day protein

- Renal impairment in pregnancy

	- Creatinine normally decreases in pregnancy

	- Therefore
		- Creatinine 70–100 µmol/L may indicate impairment

- Oliguria

	- General definition
		- <500 mL/24 hours

	- Guideline definition
		- <80 mL over 4 hours

	- Note
		- May be physiological in labour/early postpartum

- Hyperuricaemia

	- Common in preeclampsia
	- NOT diagnostic

	- Upper uric acid limits
		- 24 weeks
			- 0.28 mmol/L
		- 32 weeks
			- 0.32 mmol/L
		- 36 weeks
			- 0.34 mmol/L
		- 38 weeks
			- 0.38 mmol/L

- Platelets in pregnancy

	- Mild reduction may be physiological

	- Platelets <100,000/µL
		- 4.5% proteinuric preeclampsia
		- 9.9% nonproteinuric preeclampsia
		- 1% normal pregnancy

- HELLP syndrome

	- Severe preeclampsia subtype

	- Features
		- Haemolysis
		- Elevated liver enzymes
		- Low platelets

	- May present with incomplete triad

- Fetal growth restriction (FGR)

	- Definition
		- Failure to achieve genetic growth potential

	- Associated with
		- Small for gestational age (SGA)
		- Abnormal umbilical artery Doppler
		- Oligohydramnios

	- Definition
		- New hypertension after 20 weeks

		- Before pregnancy

- White coat hypertension

		- Chronic hypertension + new preeclampsia features after 20 weeks

- Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

	- Investigation frequency

		| Condition | Investigation | Frequency |
		|------------|-------------|-----------|
		| Chronic hypertension | Assess for proteinuria | Each visit |
		| Chronic hypertension | Preeclampsia blood tests | If sudden BP rise or new proteinuria |
		| Gestational hypertension | Assess for proteinuria | 1–2 times/week |
		| Gestational hypertension | Preeclampsia blood tests | Weekly |
		| Preeclampsia | Assess for proteinuria | At diagnosis; if initially non-proteinuric repeat daily |
		| Preeclampsia | Preeclampsia blood tests | Twice weekly or more often if unstable |

	- Monitoring

		- Proteinuria monitoring

			- Initial test
				- Dipstick urinalysis

			- If ≥1+ protein
				- Confirm with spot urine protein:creatinine ratio (PCR)

			- Important
				- Once significant proteinuria is confirmed
					- Serial measurement is not required

				- Reason
					- Degree of proteinuria does not guide management

		- Preeclampsia blood tests

			- Include
				- Full blood count
				- Electrolytes
				- Creatinine
				- Liver function tests

			- Additional test if indicated
				- Coagulation studies

	- Management of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia

		- Fundamental principles

			- Preeclampsia is progressive
			- Usually worsens if pregnancy continues
			- No therapy corrects the underlying placental pathology

			- Definitive treatment
				- Delivery

			- Postpartum
				- Symptoms usually resolve within days
				- May take longer in some women

		- Specialist care

			- Severe preeclampsia
				- Requires specialist management

			- <32 weeks gestation
				- Consult tertiary centre

			- Aim
				- Transfer before delivery whenever possible

			- Reason
				- Anticipated neonatal intensive care requirements

		- Timing of delivery

			- Depends on
				- Maternal disease severity
				- Gestational age

			- Definitions

				- Immediate management
					- Delivery within 48 hours
					- After BP stabilisation
					- After antenatal corticosteroids if appropriate

				- Expectant management
					- Pregnancy prolonged >48 hours
					- Requires close maternal and fetal monitoring

			- Management according to gestation

				| Gestation | Management |
				|------------|------------|
				| <23 weeks | Tertiary consultation; termination often considered |
				| 24–31 weeks | Transfer to tertiary centre; attempt prolongation |
				| 32–36 weeks | Prolong if feasible; deliver in equipped centre |
				| ≥37 weeks | Plan delivery |

		- Rationale for expectant management

			- Improves fetal outcome
				- Benefit depends on gestational age

			- Does not improve maternal outcome

			- Average prolongation
				- <12 days
				- Often shorter at earlier gestations

		- Indications for delivery

			- Maternal indications

				- ≥37 weeks gestation
				- Uncontrolled hypertension
				- Falling platelet count
				- Haemolysis
				- Worsening liver function tests
				- Worsening renal function
				- Neurological symptoms
				- Persistent epigastric pain
				- Persistent vomiting
				- Pulmonary oedema

			- Fetal indications

				- Placental abruption
				- Severe fetal growth restriction (FGR)
				- Non-reassuring fetal status

		- Special situations

			- Previable pregnancy (<24 weeks)
				- Termination often advised

			- <32–34 weeks
				- Manage only in specialised centres
				- Define clear endpoints for delivery

			- HELLP syndrome

				- Expectant management is harmful

				- Maternal mortality
					- Approximately 6%

				- Increased risk
					- Placental abruption

				- Management
					- Prompt delivery

			- Late preterm disease (34–36 weeks)

				- Risks
					- Small for gestational age infant
					- Caesarean section
					- Respiratory distress syndrome
					- Longer NICU admission

				- Management
					- Give antenatal corticosteroids

			- Term disease (≥37 weeks)

				- Stabilise mother
				- Deliver

				- Induction
					- Reduces severe hypertension
					- Does not increase caesarean section rate

	- Antihypertensive therapy

		- Treatment thresholds

			- BP ≥160/110 mmHg
				- Mandatory treatment

			- BP 140–160 / 90–100 mmHg
				- Treatment may be considered

			- Benefits
				- Reduces progression of disease

		- Goals

			- Prevent intracranial haemorrhage
			- Prevent eclampsia

		- Medications

			- First-line

				- Methyldopa
				- Labetalol
				- Oxprenolol

			- Second-line

				- Hydralazine
				- Nifedipine
				- Prazosin

			- Avoid during pregnancy

				- ACE inhibitors

					- Examples
						- Enalapril
						- Captopril

					- Risks
						- Fetal death
						- Fetal renal failure

		- Compatible with breastfeeding

			- Methyldopa
			- Labetalol
			- Nifedipine
			- Hydralazine

		- Postpartum

			- ACE inhibitors acceptable

	- Severe hypertension

		- Definition

			- SBP ≥170 mmHg
			- OR DBP ≥110 mmHg

		- Requires urgent treatment

		- Goal

			- Gradual BP reduction

			- Avoid rapid reduction
				- May reduce placental perfusion

		- Acute antihypertensive drugs

			| Drug | Dose | Route | Onset | Main adverse effect |
			|--------|--------|--------|--------|--------|
			| Labetalol | 20–80 mg | IV | ~5 min | Bradycardia |
			| Nifedipine | 10–20 mg | Oral | 30–45 min | Headache |
			| Hydralazine | 5–10 mg | IV | ~20 min | Tachycardia |
			| Diazoxide | 15–45 mg | IV | 3–5 min | Hypotension |

		- Prevention of maternal hypoperfusion

			- Give 250 mL saline bolus

		- Monitoring

			- Consider continuous CTG

		- Refractory hypertension

			- Repeated doses
			- Continuous infusion

		- Rarely used agents

			- Nitroprusside
			- Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN)

			- Concern
				- Fetal toxicity

	- Fluid management

		- Avoid excessive fluid administration

			- Risk
				- Pulmonary oedema

		- Indications for fluid bolus

			- Before hydralazine administration
			- Before anaesthesia

				- 250 mL bolus

			- Oliguria with suspected hypovolaemia

				- 300 mL challenge

		- Avoid

			- Large-volume fluid administration
			- Routine diuretics

		- Exception

			- Pulmonary oedema

	- Thromboprophylaxis

		- Preeclampsia increases VTE risk

		- Assess all women

		- Measures

			- Compression stockings
				- Hospitalised patients

			- Pharmacological prophylaxis
				- If ≥2 major risk factors
				- Or 1 major + 2 minor risk factors
				- Follow local protocol

	- Haematological manifestations

		- Most common abnormality

			- Thrombocytopenia
				- Platelets <100 ×10⁹/L

		- Monitoring

			- Daily
			- Twice daily if severe

		- Indication for delivery

			- Progressive platelet fall

		- Bleeding risk

			- Significant when platelets <50 ×10⁹/L

		- Transfusion indications

			- Caesarean section
			- Postpartum haemorrhage
			- Active bleeding

		- Coagulopathy management

			- Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
			- Cryoprecipitate
				- Especially if fibrinogen low

	- Hepatic manifestations

		- Symptoms

			- Epigastric pain
			- Right upper quadrant pain

		- Laboratory findings

			- Elevated AST
			- Elevated ALT

		- Clinical note

			- Pain may improve with BP control

		- Imaging

			- Only if alternative diagnosis suspected

		- HELLP syndrome

			- Haemolysis
			- Elevated liver enzymes
			- Low platelets

			- May present as ELLP syndrome
				- Without overt haemolysis

			- Treat as severe preeclampsia

	- Role of corticosteroids

		- Indicated for fetal lung maturation

		- Not indicated for maternal disease

			- No proven benefit for
				- Platelet count
				- Liver dysfunction

	- Postpartum care

		- Resolution

			- Laboratory abnormalities usually improve over days

		- Hypertension

			- May persist for weeks to months

			- Management
				- Gradual withdrawal of medication

		- Avoid

			- NSAIDs

		- Postpartum oliguria

			- Common

			- Investigate if
				- Persists >24 hours
				- Creatinine rising

			- If no improvement after 72 hours
				- Consider alternative diagnoses

					- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
					- Antiphospholipid syndrome

- Eclampsia

	- Epidemiology
		- Rare complication of pregnancy

	- Clinical features

		- Classical warning symptoms
			- Traditionally considered signs of imminent eclampsia
				- Headache
				- Visual disturbance
				- Altered level of consciousness

		- Important points
			- No reliable clinical marker predicts eclampsia
			- Neurological symptoms correlate poorly with seizure occurrence
			- Hypertension may be absent
			- Proteinuria may be absent
			- Warning symptoms may not occur
			- If seizures occur remote from delivery
				- Consider alternative diagnoses

		- Timing of seizures
			- Antepartum
			- Intrapartum
			- Postpartum
				- Most commonly within 24 hours after delivery
				- May occur later in the puerperium

		- Differential diagnosis of seizures in pregnancy
			- Epilepsy
			- Cerebral venous thrombosis
			- Other neurological disorders
			- Metabolic disorders
			- Eclampsia is not the most common cause of seizures during pregnancy

	- Management

		- Four key components
			- Resuscitation
			- Prevention of further seizures
			- Control of hypertension
			- Delivery

		- Protocols
			- Eclampsia management protocols should be available in all obstetric units

		- Resuscitation

			- Seizures are usually self-limiting

			- Initial management
				- Maintain airway
				- Administer oxygen via face mask
				- Establish IV access

			- If seizure is prolonged
				- Diazepam
					- 2 mg/min IV
					- Maximum 10 mg
				- OR
				- Clonazepam
					- 1–2 mg IV over 2–5 minutes
				- Used while magnesium sulfate is being prepared

		- Prevention of further seizures

			- Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄)
				- Anticonvulsant of choice
				- Prevents recurrent seizures
				- Reduces progression from preeclampsia to eclampsia by approximately 58%

			- Standard regimen

				| Phase | Dose |
				|-------|------|
				| Loading dose | 4 g IV diluted in normal saline over 15–20 minutes |
				| Maintenance infusion | 1–2 g/hour IV |
				| Prediluted preparation | 4 g MgSO₄ in 100 mL normal saline |

			- Recurrent seizure
				- Additional 2–4 g IV
				- Administer over 10 minutes

			- Duration
				- Continue for 24 hours after the last seizure

		- Monitoring during magnesium therapy

			- Monitor
				- Blood pressure
				- Respiratory rate
				- Urine output
				- Oxygen saturation
				- Deep tendon reflexes

			- Serum magnesium levels
				- Not routinely required
				- Required if renal impairment is present

			- Caution
				- Magnesium is renally excreted
				- Use extreme caution in
					- Oliguria
					- Renal impairment

			- Failure of magnesium therapy
				- Seizure recurrence occurs in approximately 10–15%

		- Control of hypertension

			- Target BP
				- <160/100 mmHg

			- Goals
				- Reduce recurrent seizures
				- Prevent cerebral haemorrhage

			- Pathophysiology
				- Vasogenic cerebral oedema lowers the seizure threshold following eclampsia

		- Delivery

			- Once maternal condition is stabilised
				- Arrange delivery

			- Before delivery
				- Continue close fetal monitoring

			- Principle
				- Pregnancy should not continue after eclampsia
				- Applies even if the patient appears clinically stable

- Prevention of eclampsia

	- Drug of choice
		- Magnesium sulfate

	- Standard prophylactic regimen
		- Loading dose
			- 4 g IV
		- Maintenance infusion
			- 1 g/hour IV

	- Prophylaxis in mild preeclampsia
		- Controversial
		- May be considered if
			- Persistent headache
			- Hyperreflexia with clonus
			- Severe hypertension
			- Liver involvement

	- Limitation
		- Clinical predictors have poor predictive value

	- Follow local institutional protocols

	- Additional indications for magnesium sulfate

		- Eclampsia

		- Severe preeclampsia
			- BP ≥170/110 mmHg with ≥3+ proteinuria
			- OR
			- BP ≥150/100 mmHg on two occasions
				- With ≥2+ proteinuria
				- Plus ≥2 features suggesting imminent eclampsia

		- Preeclampsia with ≥1 CNS irritability feature

		- Prior to transfer to higher-level obstetric care

	- Important points

		- Do not use prophylactically without senior obstetric consultation

		- Indicated when CNS dysfunction is present
			- Antepartum
			- Intrapartum
			- Continue for 24 hours postpartum

		- Clinical signs do not reliably predict onset of eclampsia

		- Follow institutional MgSO₄ protocol
			- Include preparation and dilution instructions
			- Include maternal and fetal monitoring
			- Recognise MgSO₄ toxicity

- Fetal surveillance in hypertensive pregnancy

	- Risks
		- Increased adverse perinatal outcomes
		- Fetal growth restriction (FGR)

	- Highest-risk pregnancies
		- Early-onset disease
		- Severe hypertension
		- Chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia

	- Main challenge
		- Balance risks of fetal growth restriction against prematurity

	- Principles
		- Accurate pregnancy dating
			- Essential in chronic hypertension and other high-risk pregnancies

		- Ultrasound assessment
			- Fetal growth
			- Amniotic fluid volume (AFV)
			- Umbilical artery Doppler
			- Serial scans if pregnancy continues

		- Symphysis-fundal height
			- Poor screening test for small-for-gestational-age fetus

	- Umbilical artery Doppler

		- Most useful surveillance tool

		- Benefits
			- Predicts adverse perinatal outcomes
			- Improves neonatal outcomes
			- Reduces unnecessary intervention

		- Abnormal findings
			- Absent end-diastolic flow
			- Reversed end-diastolic flow

		- Notes
			- Doppler abnormalities may develop 7–10 days after a previously normal study
			- Limited usefulness after 36 weeks gestation

	- Other surveillance methods
		- CTG
		- Biophysical profile

		- Limitation
			- No clear improvement in clinical outcomes

	- Limitations of fetal surveillance
		- Cannot reliably predict
			- Placental abruption
			- Cord accidents

	- Surveillance protocol

		| Condition | Surveillance | Frequency |
		|-----------|-------------|-----------|
		| Chronic hypertension | Early dating ultrasound | First trimester |
		| Chronic hypertension | Growth scan + AFV + Umbilical artery Doppler | Third trimester as indicated |
		| Gestational hypertension | Growth scan + AFV + Umbilical artery Doppler | At diagnosis, then every 3–4 weeks |
		| Preeclampsia | Growth scan + AFV + Umbilical artery Doppler | At diagnosis, then every 2–3 weeks |
		| Preeclampsia with FGR | CTG | ≥2 times/week |
		| Preeclampsia with FGR | AFV + Umbilical artery Doppler | Weekly or more frequently |
		| Preeclampsia with FGR | Growth scan | Every 2 weeks |

- Antenatal corticosteroids

	- Types
		- Betamethasone
		- Dexamethasone

	- After 34 weeks
		- Limited evidence
		- Use on an individual basis

	- Urgent delivery
		- Do not delay solely to administer corticosteroids

	- Repeat courses
		- Controversial
		- No clear long-term benefit demonstrated

	- Indication
		- Imminent preterm delivery before 30 weeks gestation

	- Benefit
		- Reduces cerebral palsy
			- Relative risk approximately 0.69

- Postpartum course

	- Resolution pattern
		- Platelets may initially decrease
		- Liver enzymes may initially rise
		- Gradual recovery thereafter

	- Hypertension
		- May persist for days to weeks
		- May persist up to 3 months
		- Requires monitoring
		- Gradual withdrawal of antihypertensive medication

	- Avoid
		- NSAIDs
			- May worsen
				- Hypertension
				- Renal function
				- Platelet dysfunction

	- Psychological support
		- Anxiety is common
		- Emotional distress is common
		- Offer counselling
		- Encourage family support

	- Future pregnancy
		- Increased recurrence risk
		- Recommend pre-pregnancy specialist consultation

- Risk factors for preeclampsia

	- Major risk factors
		- Previous preeclampsia
		- Antiphospholipid syndrome
		- Diabetes mellitus
		- Multiple pregnancy

	- Moderate risk factors
		- Nulliparity
		- BMI ≥25 kg/m²
		- Maternal age ≥40 years
		- Family history of preeclampsia
		- Elevated BP in early pregnancy

	- Other risk factors
		- Chronic kidney disease
		- Autoimmune disease
		- Long interpregnancy interval

- Prevention strategies

	- Aspirin

		- Dose
			- 50–150 mg daily

		- Timing
			- Best started before 16 weeks gestation
			- Minimal benefit if commenced after 20 weeks

		- Recommendation
			- Moderate-risk women
			- High-risk women

		- Usually discontinued
			- At 37 weeks

	- Calcium

		- Dose
			- Approximately 1.5 g/day

		- Benefits
			- Reduces preeclampsia
			- Reduces preterm birth

		- Greatest benefit
			- Women with low dietary calcium intake

	- Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)

		- Not routinely recommended

		- Consider only for
			- Antiphospholipid syndrome

	- Vitamin C and Vitamin E
		- Not recommended
		- No proven benefit
		- Possible harm

	- Emerging therapies
		- Pravastatin
			- Experimental
		- Multivitamins
			- Possible benefit under investigation

- Long-term consequences

	- Maternal cardiovascular risk

		| Condition | Relative Risk |
		|-----------|--------------:|
		| Chronic hypertension | 3.7 |
		| Ischaemic heart disease | 2.16 |
		| Cerebrovascular disease | 1.81 |
		| End-stage renal disease | 4.3 |
		| Type 2 diabetes mellitus | 1.86 |

	- Cancer
		- No increased risk demonstrated

	- Lifestyle advice
		- Smoking cessation
		- Maintain healthy weight
		- Regular exercise
		- Healthy diet
		- May reduce future cardiovascular risk by approximately 4–13%

	- Long-term follow-up
		- Annual blood pressure measurement
		- Cardiovascular risk assessment every 5 years
			- Lipid profile
			- Blood glucose

	- Cognitive and offspring effects

		- Maternal
			- Memory impairment may occur 3–8 months postpartum
			- Longer-term cognitive effects reported after eclampsia

		- Offspring
			- Higher blood pressure
			- Higher BMI
			- Possible neurodevelopmental effects
				- Evidence remains inconsistent
