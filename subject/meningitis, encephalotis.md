- Definition
	- Meningitis
		- Inflammation of the meninges surrounding the brain and spinal cord
	- Encephalitis
		- Inflammation of the brain parenchyma
	- Clinical distinction may be difficult
		- If uncertain, empiric treatment should cover both

- Etiology
	- Meningitis
		- Viral
			- Enterovirus
			- Parechovirus
		- Bacterial
			- Neisseria meningitidis
			- Streptococcus pneumoniae
			- Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib)
			- Group B Streptococcus (GBS)
			- Listeria monocytogenes
			- Gram-negative organisms
	- Encephalitis
		- Herpes simplex virus (HSV) (most important treatable cause)
		- Enterovirus
		- Parechovirus
		- Other viruses
	- Opportunistic organisms
		- Cryptococcus
		- Mycobacteria

- Pathogenesis
	- Meningitis
		- Infection causes inflammation of the meninges
		- May progress to cerebral oedema, raised ICP and septic shock
	- Encephalitis
		- Infection causes inflammation of brain parenchyma
		- Leads to neuronal injury, cerebral oedema and seizures

- Risk factors
	- Age <5 years
	- Neonates (<3 months)
			- Maternal GBS colonisation
	- Unimmunised
	- Immunocompromised
	- VP shunt
	- Cochlear implant
	- Previous neurosurgery
	- Penetrating head injury

- Most common
	- Viral meningitis more common than bacterial meningitis
	- Most important bacterial causes
		- Neisseria meningitidis
		- Streptococcus pneumoniae
		- Haemophilus influenzae type b (rare due to vaccination)
	- Most important treatable cause of encephalitis
		- HSV

- Clinical features
	- Fever
	- Headache
	- Vomiting
	- Neck stiffness
	- Photophobia
	- Irritability
	- Lethargy
	- Poor feeding (infants)
	- Bulging fontanelle
	- Altered mental state
	- Seizures
	- Focal neurological deficits
	- Rash (meningococcal disease)
	- Fulminant presentation may occur within hours
	- Recent antibiotic therapy may mask classical features

- Features suggesting encephalitis
	- Altered conscious state
	- Encephalopathy
	- Focal neurological signs
	- Seizures
	- Obtunded child
	- Suspected HSV infection

- Examination
	- Full neurological examination
	- Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)
	- Neck stiffness
	- Kernig sign
			- Pain/resistance on passive knee extension with hip flexed to 90°
	- Brudzinski sign
			- Reflex hip and knee flexion with passive neck flexion
	- Focal neurological deficits
	- Cranial nerve abnormalities
	- Signs of raised intracranial pressure
	- Petechial or purpuric rash
	- Signs of septic shock

- Diagnosis
	- Clinical diagnosis supported by investigations
	- High index of suspicion in any unwell child with:
		- Altered mental state
		- No clear focus of infection
	- Do not delay treatment awaiting investigations
	- If lumbar puncture contraindicated or delayed >30 minutes
		- Immediately commence empiric antimicrobials
		- Add aciclovir if encephalitis suspected
		- Consider dexamethasone

- Investigations
	- Initial
		- Blood cultures
		- FBE
			- May be normal
		- Blood glucose
		- Blood gas
			- Lactate
		- UEC
			- Assess hyponatraemia
		- Lumbar puncture (if safe)
			- CSF microscopy
			- Cell count
			- Protein
			- Glucose
			- Simultaneous blood glucose
	- Most appropriate
		- Lumbar puncture with CSF analysis
	- Additional
		- CSF multiplex PCR (BioFire®)
		- HSV PCR
		- Enterovirus PCR
		- Parechovirus PCR
		- Neisseria meningitidis PCR
		- Streptococcus pneumoniae PCR
		- Serum bacterial PCR
		- Serum viral PCR
		- Coagulation profile (shock/coagulopathy)
		- LFTs
		- Metabolic studies
		- Toxicology (if non-infectious encephalopathy suspected)
		- EEG
			- Helpful in encephalitis
		- MRI brain
			- Preferred imaging
		- CT brain
			- Focal deficits
			- Raised ICP
			- Encephalopathy
			- Diagnostic uncertainty
			- Suspected abscess
			- Suspected thrombosis
	- Lumbar puncture contraindications
		- Focal neurological signs
		- Ongoing seizures
		- Markedly reduced GCS
		- Cardiovascular compromise
		- Coagulopathy
	- Important notes
		- Tachycardia or tachypnoea alone are NOT contraindications to LP
		- Normal CT does NOT exclude raised ICP

- Treatment
	- Initial
		- ABC resuscitation
		- IV antibiotics within 30 minutes of decision to treat
		- IV aciclovir if:
			- Suspected encephalitis
			- Encephalopathy
			- Focal neurological signs
			- Seizures
			- HSV risk factors
			- Neonates
		- IV dexamethasone
			- Immediately before or with first antibiotic dose
			- Ideally within 4 hours (maximum 12 hours)
			- Not recommended in neonates
	- Supportive care
		- Oxygen
		- Treat seizures immediately
		- Shock
			- 10 mL/kg 0.9% sodium chloride boluses
		- IV fluids
			- Maximum 2/3 maintenance
			- Restrict further if:
				- Hyponatraemia
				- Raised ICP
		- Maintain:
			- Normal blood pressure
			- Adequate circulating volume
		- Droplet isolation for first 24 hours
		- Chemoprophylaxis for close contacts where indicated

- Directed antimicrobial therapy
	- Neisseria meningitidis
		- Benzylpenicillin
		- 7 days
	- Streptococcus pneumoniae (penicillin-sensitive)
		- Benzylpenicillin
		- 10 days
	- Streptococcus pneumoniae (penicillin-resistant)
		- Ceftriaxone/Cefotaxime
		- Add Vancomycin if cephalosporin-resistant
		- 10 days
	- Haemophilus influenzae type b
		- Ceftriaxone/Cefotaxime
		- 10 days
	- Gram-negative organisms
		- Ceftriaxone/Cefotaxime
		- 21 days
	- GBS/Listeria
		- Benzylpenicillin
		- 14–21 days
	- HSV
		- Aciclovir
		- Minimum 21 days
	- Organism not isolated
		- Ceftriaxone/Cefotaxime
		- Minimum 7 days

- Monitoring
	- Weight
	- Head circumference (<2 years)
	- HR
	- BP
	- Neurological status
	- Electrolytes
	- Urea
	- Creatinine
	- Blood glucose
	- Hydration
	- Sodium
	- Acid-base status every 6–12 hours during first 48 hours

- Complications
	- Septic shock
	- Multi-organ failure
	- Hydrocephalus
	- Venous sinus thrombosis
	- Cerebral abscess
	- Subdural empyema
	- Subdural effusion
	- Persistent fever after 4–6 days
			- Nosocomial infection
			- Inadequate treatment
			- Abscess
			- Empyema
	- Seizures
	- Epilepsy
	- Hearing impairment
	- Neurodevelopmental impairment
	- Permanent focal neurological deficit

- Hearing loss
	- Highest risk
			- Streptococcus pneumoniae meningitis (~22%)
	- May occur within 48 hours
	- Early cochlear ossification may occur
	- Baseline inpatient audiology if possible
	- Early ENT referral

- Follow-up
	- Formal audiology assessment
			- 6–8 weeks after discharge
	- Monitor neurodevelopment
	- Consider complement deficiency testing after recurrent meningococcal disease

- Notification
	- Immediate notification
		- Neisseria meningitidis
		- Haemophilus influenzae type b
	- Streptococcus pneumoniae
		- Notify within 5 days
