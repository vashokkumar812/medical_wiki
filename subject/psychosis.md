# Schizophrenia

  - Definition
    - Chronic psychotic disorder characterized by:
      - Delusions
      - Hallucinations
      - Disorganized speech
      - Grossly disorganized or catatonic behaviour
      - Negative symptoms
    - Symptoms persist for ≥6 months, with ≥1 month of active-phase symptoms.
    - Causes significant impairment in social, occupational, or self-care functioning.

  - Epidemiology
    - Lifetime prevalence
      - ~1%
    - Usual age of onset
      - Males
        - Late teens to early 20s
      - Females
        - Late 20s to early 30s
    - Earlier onset is associated with poorer prognosis.

  - Risk Factors
    - Family history
    - Advanced paternal age
    - Obstetric complications
    - Prenatal infections
    - Cannabis use (especially high potency)
    - Childhood adversity
    - Urban living
    - Migration

  - Pathophysiology
    - Dopamine hypothesis
      - ↑ Dopamine activity in mesolimbic pathway
        - Positive symptoms
      - ↓ Dopamine activity in mesocortical pathway
        - Negative symptoms
    - Glutamate (NMDA receptor hypofunction)
    - Genetic predisposition
    - Neurodevelopmental abnormalities

  - Clinical Features

    - Positive Symptoms
      - Delusions
        - Persecutory
        - Grandiose
        - Referential
        - Control
      - Hallucinations
        - Auditory (most common)
        - Visual (less common)
      - Disorganized speech
      - Disorganized behaviour
      - Catatonia

    - Negative Symptoms
      - Flat affect
      - Alogia
      - Avolition
      - Anhedonia
      - Asociality

    - Cognitive Symptoms
      - Poor attention
      - Impaired working memory
      - Executive dysfunction
      - Reduced processing speed

  - Schneider First-Rank Symptoms (High-Yield)
    - Audible thoughts
    - Voices arguing
    - Voices commenting
    - Thought insertion
    - Thought withdrawal
    - Thought broadcasting
    - Delusional perception
    - Passivity phenomena
      - Made acts
      - Made impulses
      - Made feelings

  - DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria
    - ≥2 of the following for ≥1 month
      - Delusions
      - Hallucinations
      - Disorganized speech
      - Grossly disorganized or catatonic behaviour
      - Negative symptoms
    - At least one must be:
      - Delusions
      - Hallucinations
      - Disorganized speech
    - Continuous disturbance for ≥6 months.
    - Functional decline.
    - Not explained by:
      - Substance use
      - Medical illness
      - Mood disorder

  - Investigations
    - Diagnosis is clinical.
    - Exclude organic causes.
    - Blood tests
      - FBC
      - UEC
      - LFT
      - TFT
      - B12
      - Folate
      - Glucose
      - Calcium
    - Toxicology screen
    - CT/MRI brain
      - If atypical presentation
      - Neurological signs
      - Older onset
    - ECG
      - Before antipsychotics
    - Baseline metabolic assessment
      - Weight
      - BMI
      - Waist circumference
      - Blood pressure
      - HbA1c
      - Lipids

  - Management

    - Acute Psychosis
      - Ensure safety
      - Assess suicide risk
      - Assess risk to others
      - Antipsychotic medication
      - Hospital admission if required

    - First-Line Antipsychotics (Second-generation)
      - Risperidone
      - Olanzapine
      - Quetiapine
      - Aripiprazole
      - Ziprasidone
      - Lurasidone
      - Paliperidone

    - First-Generation Antipsychotics
      - Haloperidol
      - Chlorpromazine

    - Treatment-Resistant Schizophrenia
      - Failure of ≥2 adequate antipsychotic trials
      - Clozapine is the treatment of choice.

    - Psychosocial Management
      - Psychoeducation
      - CBT for psychosis
      - Family therapy
      - Social rehabilitation
      - Vocational support
      - Community mental health follow-up

  - Antipsychotic Adverse Effects

    - Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)
      - Acute dystonia
      - Akathisia
      - Parkinsonism
      - Tardive dyskinesia

    - Metabolic Syndrome
      - Weight gain
      - Diabetes
      - Dyslipidaemia
      - Most common with:
        - Clozapine
        - Olanzapine

    - Hyperprolactinaemia
      - Risperidone
      - Paliperidone

    - QT prolongation
    - Sedation
    - Anticholinergic effects
    - Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (rare)

  - Clozapine

    - Indications
      - Treatment-resistant schizophrenia
      - Persistent suicidality

    - Important Adverse Effects
      - Agranulocytosis
      - Myocarditis
      - Cardiomyopathy
      - Seizures
      - Severe constipation/ileus
      - Weight gain
      - Diabetes
      - Sialorrhoea

    - Monitoring
      - Regular FBC (mandatory)
      - Weight
      - Lipids
      - HbA1c
      - ECG (when indicated)

  - Differential Diagnosis
    - Schizophreniform disorder
    - Brief psychotic disorder
    - Schizoaffective disorder
    - Bipolar disorder with psychotic features
    - Major depressive disorder with psychotic features
    - Substance-induced psychosis
    - Delirium
    - Dementia

  - Comparison of Psychotic Disorders

    | Disorder | Duration | Mood Symptoms |
    |----------|----------|---------------|
    | Brief psychotic disorder | <1 month | Minimal |
    | Schizophreniform disorder | 1–6 months | Minimal |
    | Schizophrenia | >6 months | Not predominant |
    | Schizoaffective disorder | Psychosis with significant mood episodes + ≥2 weeks psychosis without mood symptoms | Prominent |
    | Bipolar disorder with psychosis | Psychosis only during manic/depressive episodes | Always present |

  - Prognostic Factors

    - Good Prognosis
      - Acute onset
      - Later onset
      - Female sex
      - Good premorbid functioning
      - Good social support
      - Predominantly positive symptoms

    - Poor Prognosis
      - Early onset
      - Male sex
      - Gradual onset
      - Predominantly negative symptoms
      - Poor premorbid functioning
      - Family history
      - Substance misuse

  - High-Yield AMC Pearls
    - Auditory hallucinations are the most common hallucinations.
    - Visual hallucinations suggest an organic cause until proven otherwise.
    - Negative symptoms respond poorly to treatment.
    - Second-generation antipsychotics are first-line.
    - Clozapine is indicated after failure of two adequate antipsychotic trials.
    - Clozapine requires regular FBC monitoring because of agranulocytosis risk.
    - Olanzapine and clozapine have the highest risk of metabolic syndrome.
    - Risperidone commonly causes hyperprolactinaemia.
    - Acute dystonia is treated with benztropine or procyclidine.
    - Neuroleptic malignant syndrome presents with:
      - Fever
      - Rigidity
      - Autonomic instability
      - Elevated CK
      - Requires immediate cessation of antipsychotic.

  - AMC Exam Tips
    - Young adult + auditory hallucinations + persecutory delusions + social withdrawal for >6 months
      - → Schizophrenia
    - Failure of two antipsychotics
      - → Clozapine
    - New fever + rigidity after antipsychotic
      - → Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
    - First episode psychosis
      - → Exclude substance use and organic causes before confirming schizophrenia.





- Variants (Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders)

  - Schizophrenia
    - Duration
      - ≥6 months
    - Active psychotic symptoms
      - ≥1 month
    - Functional decline
      - Present

  - Schizophreniform Disorder
    - Duration
      - 1–6 months
    - Symptoms identical to schizophrenia
    - Functional decline
      - May or may not be present
    - Some patients later develop schizophrenia.

  - Brief Psychotic Disorder
    - Duration
      - <1 month
    - Sudden onset
    - Full return to premorbid functioning
    - Often triggered by:
      - Severe stress
      - Trauma
      - Postpartum period

  - Schizoaffective Disorder
    - Features of:
      - Schizophrenia
      - Mood disorder (major depression or bipolar disorder)
    - Diagnostic hallmark
      - ≥2 weeks of psychotic symptoms **without mood symptoms**
    - Mood symptoms present for the majority of the illness.

  - Delusional Disorder
    - One or more delusions
      - ≥1 month
    - No prominent hallucinations
    - Functioning relatively preserved
    - Types
      - Persecutory
      - Grandiose
      - Jealous
      - Somatic
      - Erotomanic
      - Mixed

  - Shared Psychotic Disorder (Folie à deux)
    - Rare
    - Delusions shared between closely associated individuals
    - Usually resolves after separation.

  - Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder
    - Psychosis directly related to:
      - Amphetamines
      - Cocaine
      - Cannabis
      - Hallucinogens
      - Corticosteroids
    - Improves after removal of the offending agent.

  - Psychotic Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition
    - Secondary to:
      - Brain tumour
      - Temporal lobe epilepsy
      - Autoimmune encephalitis
      - Delirium
      - Dementia
      - CNS infection

- Historic Schizophrenia Subtypes (No longer DSM-5 diagnoses, but still tested occasionally)

  - Paranoid Schizophrenia
    - Predominantly:
      - Delusions
      - Auditory hallucinations
    - Less disorganized speech/behaviour
    - Better functional prognosis

  - Disorganized (Hebephrenic) Schizophrenia
    - Disorganized speech
    - Disorganized behaviour
    - Flat or inappropriate affect
    - Early onset
    - Poor prognosis

  - Catatonic Schizophrenia
    - Catatonia
      - Stupor
      - Mutism
      - Waxy flexibility
      - Posturing
      - Negativism
      - Echolalia
      - Echopraxia
      - Agitation
    - First-line treatment
      - Benzodiazepines (Lorazepam)
    - If refractory
      - Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)

  - Undifferentiated Schizophrenia
    - Mixed symptoms
    - Does not fit another subtype

  - Residual Schizophrenia
    - Minimal positive symptoms
    - Persistent negative symptoms
    - Functional impairment remains

- AMC High-Yield Duration Comparison

  | Disorder | Duration | Functional Decline |
  |----------|----------|-------------------|
  | Brief Psychotic Disorder | <1 month | Usually absent |
  | Schizophreniform Disorder | 1–6 months | May or may not occur |
  | Schizophrenia | >6 months | Present |
  | Schizoaffective Disorder | Variable | Usually present |
  | Delusional Disorder | ≥1 month | Usually preserved |

- AMC Pearls
  - **<1 month** → Brief psychotic disorder.
  - **1–6 months** → Schizophreniform disorder.
  - **>6 months** → Schizophrenia.
  - **Psychosis only during mood episodes** → Mood disorder with psychotic features.
  - **Psychosis persists for ≥2 weeks without mood symptoms** → Schizoaffective disorder.
  - **Isolated fixed delusions with preserved functioning** → Delusional disorder.
  - **Catatonia is treated with lorazepam first, not antipsychotics.**




- Acute psychosis is the presence of a mental state where appreciation of reality is impaired, as evidenced by the presence of psychotic symptoms such as:
	- Delusions
	- Hallucinations
	- Mood disturbance
	- Bizarre behaviour

- In the acute presentation, it is usually more important to:
	- Establish the presence of psychosis
	- Establish the symptom characteristics of the patient’s illness
	- Carry out a risk assessment
	- Rather than make a definitive diagnosis such as:
		- Schizophrenia
		- Bipolar mood disorder

- The diagnostic boundaries of the various disorders are often unclear.
	- There is considerable overlap between the different entities.

- Diagnoses may change over time.
	- A patient with early psychosis presenting as schizophrenia may later reveal:
		- Bipolar mood disorder
		- Schizoaffective disorder

- The diagnosis of psychosis is entirely clinical.
	- There are no diagnostic tests.

- A number of major psychiatric disorders may present with psychosis.

- Psychosis often occurs only briefly during the course of a disorder.
	- Patients suffering from these disorders may not manifest psychosis, especially if treated.

- The onset of acute psychosis or the occurrence of relapse is often a medical emergency.

- While most general practitioners will be able to enlist the assistance of specialist services:
	- Some rural GPs may need to provide the entire treatment.

- Parents often bring in the first-episode patient.
	- The patient may be coming reluctantly.

- Acutely psychotic patients may be:
	- Irritable
	- Agitated
	- Unrealistically suspicious about the motives of those around them
	- Perplexed
	- Hostile

- Frequently there is impaired insight.
	- The patient may not be aware that:
		- He/she is ill
		- Treatment is required

- The first step is to establish:
	- A therapeutic alliance
	- Rapport

- Helpful approaches include:
	- Interest
	- Trust
	- A sincere offer of assistance

- The GP should avoid:
	- Colluding with psychotic symptoms

- It is not usually necessary to:
	- Confront unusual thoughts and experiences
	- Challenge unusual thoughts and experiences

- The GP should be:
	- Calm
	- Friendly
	- Unhurried

- Questions should be asked:
	- Clearly

- It is always helpful to talk to the patient’s family.
	- Especially in early psychosis

- Little may emerge during the interview.
	- The presence of illness may need to be inferred from the family’s account.

- If not volunteered by the patient:
	- Symptoms of psychosis can be elicited through specific questions.

- It is best to ask probing questions that:
	- May reflect normal experience
	- Do not immediately suggest pathology

- On mental state examination, it is important to note whether the patient is:
	- Agitated
		- Suggests acuity
	- Depressed
	- Suspicious
	- Perplexed

- Thought process may be:
	- Accelerated in mania
	- Retarded in depression
	- Illogical with loosening of associations
	- Incoherent with loosening of associations

- Delusions may be present.

- Hallucinations may be present.

- Insight may be impaired.

- Some patients retain insight while presenting with a tormenting symptom such as:
	- Accusatory voices

- Patients with early onset psychosis may not have full-blown psychotic symptoms.

- Prodromal symptoms of psychosis may be present.

- Detection and treatment of early psychosis without delay is vital because:
	- Delay in treatment worsens prognosis

- There are a number of physical causes of acute psychosis.

- Frequently physical examination is not possible.
	- It may need to be deferred until psychosis has been controlled.

- A number of investigations are also needed.

- Many patients with acute psychosis also abuse:
	- Cannabis
	- Alcohol
	- Stimulants

- Comorbid substance abuse will need to be addressed in long-term management.

- Clinical judgment must determine whether the patient constitutes a risk to:
	- Himself/herself
	- Others

- If there is:
	- Danger of suicide
	- Danger of violence
	- Inpatient management will usually be needed

- Sometimes it will be necessary to invoke legal procedures to facilitate:
	- Involuntary hospitalisation

- Many patients with acute psychosis can be treated in the community.

- The widespread provision of assertive outreach teams has enabled:
	- High-standard care to be provided to patients in their home

- General practitioners often provide critical input to assertive outreach teams through:
	- Collaborative treatment

- AMC MCQ high-yield points:
	- Acute psychosis is a clinical diagnosis.
		- There is no diagnostic test.
	- Priorities:
		- Establish psychosis.
		- Characterise symptoms.
		- Perform risk assessment.
		- Exclude organic causes.
		- Initiate treatment.
	- Do not:
		- Argue with delusions.
		- Reinforce delusions.
		- Collude with psychotic beliefs.
	- Obtain collateral history from family.
	- Assess:
		- Suicide risk.
		- Violence risk.
		- Insight.
		- Ability to care for self.
	- Consider:
		- Substance-induced psychosis.
		- Medical causes of psychosis.
	- Early treatment improves prognosis.
	- Hospitalisation is indicated for:
		- Suicide risk.
		- Violence risk.
		- Severe psychosis.
		- Inability to care for self.
	- Involuntary treatment may be required when significant risk is present.

- Build on the therapeutic alliance established during assessment.
	- Patients who are frightened, suspicious, and lack insight require:
		- Support
		- Reassurance
	- A trusted GP has a significant therapeutic advantage.
	- Families and carers often require:
		- Support
		- Psychoeducation

- Acutely psychotic patients may be:
	- Anxious
	- Agitated
	- Overactive
	- Hostile
	- Distressed
	- Sleep deprived

- For acute settling:
	- Use sedative/anxiolytic medications.
	- Benzodiazepines are most frequently recommended.
	- Sedating antipsychotics may also be useful:
		- Chlorpromazine (Largactil)
		- Quetiapine (Seroquel)
	- Other sedative/hypnotics may be used.

- Definitive treatment:
	- Antipsychotic medication

- Psychosis + mania:
	- Add a mood stabiliser:
		- Lithium
		- Valproate
		- Carbamazepine

- Psychosis + depression:
	- Antipsychotic + antidepressant
	- Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is also useful.

- Antipsychotics are classified into:
	- Conventional
	- Novel (atypical)

- Conventional antipsychotics:
	- Examples:
		- Chlorpromazine
		- Haloperidol
	- Common adverse effects:
		- Extrapyramidal side effects (EPS)
			- Dystonia
			- Parkinsonism
			- Akathisia
			- Tardive dyskinesia

- Atypical antipsychotics:
	- Usually preferred because:
		- Most patients can be treated without EPS
	- Cause other adverse effects that may affect long-term physical health.
	- Require periodic GP physical review.

- Antipsychotics act by reducing/eliminating:
	- Delusions
	- Hallucinations
	- Thought disorder
	- Abnormal mood states

- Antipsychotic effect:
	- May take weeks to become evident.

- Relapse often occurs due to poor medication compliance.
	- Assess reasons for non-compliance:
		- Unacceptable side effects
		- Poor symptom control
	- Changing medication may help.

- Some patients benefit from depot antipsychotics:
	- Long-acting injectable risperidone

- Important adjunctive treatments:
	- Supportive psychotherapy
	- Psychoeducation
	- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
	- Family intervention
	- Social work assistance

- Specialist involvement:
	- Usually seek consultant psychiatrist input.
	- May occur through:
		- Psychiatric hospitalisation
		- Community psychiatric services
		- Assertive outreach teams

- Important point:
	- Advice from community psychiatric workers does not equal a consultant psychiatrist opinion.

- AMC high-yield:
	- Benzodiazepines → acute agitation/anxiety.
	- Antipsychotics → definitive treatment.
	- Mania + psychosis → antipsychotic + mood stabiliser.
	- Depression + psychosis → antipsychotic + antidepressant ± ECT.
	- Conventional antipsychotics → higher EPS risk.
	- Atypical antipsychotics → lower EPS risk but require physical health monitoring.
	- Antipsychotic response takes weeks.
	- Poor adherence is a major cause of relapse.
	- Depot antipsychotics improve adherence.

	- Schizophreniform disorder

		- Physical conditions

- Questions for eliciting psychotic symptoms:
	- Anxiety:
		- Feeling especially nervous or fearful?
		- Feeling tense and shaky?
		- Palpitations?
	- Depressed mood:
		- Feeling sad or down?
		- Reduced enjoyment of activities?
	- Elevated mood:
		- Feeling unusually good, cheerful, or full of life?
	- Auditory hallucinations:
		- Hearing voices when no one is present?
	- Thought insertion:
		- Thoughts being put into your mind?
		- Telepathy?
	- Thought withdrawal:
		- Thoughts being taken out of your mind?
	- Thought broadcasting:
		- Other people aware of your thoughts?
	- Thought echo:
		- Voices echoing your thoughts?
	- Delusion of control:
		- Under the control or influence of an outside force?
	- Delusions of reference:
		- TV or radio programs having special meaning?
	- Delusions of persecution:
		- Being singled out?
		- Conspiracy against you?
	- Delusions of grandeur:
		- Special abilities or power?
	- Delusions of guilt:
		- Sinned or deserving punishment?

	- Physical examination:
		- General examination

	- Chlorpromazine:

		- Sedation

		- Sedation

- Management of acute psychosis:
	- Assess:
		- Danger to self
		- Danger to others
		- Need for hospitalisation
	- Assess:
		- Physical state
		- Possibility of substance abuse
	- Consider specialist treatment:
		- Psychiatrist
		- Mobile community outreach psychiatric services
	- Antipsychotic medication
	- If response is inadequate after several weeks:
		- Reassess treatment
		- Increase treatment intensity if appropriate
		- Monitor for extrapyramidal side effects
	- Treat:
		- Anxiety
		- Agitation
		- Insomnia
		- Benzodiazepines may be used short term
		- Quetiapine or chlorpromazine may also be useful
	- If mania is present:
		- Add mood stabiliser
			- Lithium
			- Valproate
			- Carbamazepine
	- If depression persists:
		- Consider adjunctive antidepressants
	- If adherence is poor:
		- Consider long-acting injectable risperidone
	- Engage patient in:
		- Supportive psychotherapy
		- Case management
		- Family therapy
		- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
	- Consider social interventions:
		- Housing
		- Resources
		- Social supports
	- Evaluate:
		- Functional status
		- Vocational rehabilitation options
