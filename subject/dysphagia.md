- Dysphagia
	- Definition
		- Complex anatomical and/or functional disruption of the aerodigestive tract resulting in impaired swallowing
	- Major categories
		- By anatomical location
			- Oropharyngeal dysphagia
			- Oesophageal dysphagia
		- By pathophysiology
			- Mechanical obstruction
			- Motility disorder

- Epidemiology
	- Significant disease burden
	- Associated with high morbidity and mortality
	- Population prevalence
		- Approximately 2–20%
	- Australia
		- ~20% of people >50 years affected
		- Up to 50% in long-term care facilities
	- Aspiration pneumonitis
		- Leading cause of death among people with disability in NSW residential care facilities (2016)
	- Risk factors in elderly
		- Previous stroke
		- Dementia
		- Motor neurone disease
	- More common in women than men

- Classification

	- Oropharyngeal dysphagia

		- Mechanical obstruction
			- Oropharyngeal malignancy
				- Squamous cell carcinoma (most common)
				- Lymphoma
				- Salivary gland tumours
			- Pharyngitis
			- Tonsillitis
			- Laryngitis
			- Parapharyngeal abscess
			- Deep neck space infection
			- Zenker diverticulum
				- Most common pharyngeal diverticulum
				- Due to spasm of upper oesophageal sphincter
				- High-pressure pharynx
			- Radiation injury
				- Mucositis
				- Xerostomia
				- Fibrosis
				- Strictures
			- Cervical osteophytes
			- Thyroid goitre
			- Thyroid cancer

		- Motility disorders
			- Stroke (most common cause)
			- Parkinson disease
			- Alzheimer disease
			- Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)
			- Myasthenia gravis
			- Multiple sclerosis
			- Functional dysphagia
				- Diagnosis of exclusion
			- Laryngopharyngeal reflux

	- Oesophageal dysphagia

		- Mechanical obstruction
			- Oesophageal adenocarcinoma
				- Most common oesophageal malignancy
				- Dysphagia usually indicates advanced disease
			- Peptic stricture
				- Secondary to chronic reflux
			- Schatzki ring
			- Foreign body

		- Motility disorders
			- Eosinophilic oesophagitis
			- Achalasia
				- Most common oesophageal motility disorder
			- Distal oesophageal spasm
			- Scleroderma
			- Diabetic autonomic neuropathy

- Oropharyngeal dysphagia

	- Definition
		- Dysmotility or obstruction at or above the upper oesophageal sphincter (UES)

	- Neurological causes
		- Central
			- Stroke
			- Parkinson disease
			- Alzheimer disease
		- Peripheral
			- Diabetic neuropathy
			- Myasthenia gravis

	- Stroke
		- Most common cause
		- Causes
			- Delayed swallow
			- Poor airway protection
			- Aspiration risk
		- Up to one-third develop aspiration pneumonia within first month

	- Mechanical causes
		- Head and neck cancers
		- Strictures
		- Radiation fibrosis
		- Goitre
		- Cervical osteophytes

	- Head and neck cancer
		- >90% are squamous cell carcinoma
		- Types
			- HPV-negative SCC
				- Smoking
				- Heavy alcohol use
			- HPV-positive SCC
				- Increasing incidence

- Oesophageal dysphagia

	- Definition
		- Dysmotility or obstruction below the upper oesophageal sphincter

	- Most common obstruction
		- Peptic stricture due to GORD

	- GORD complications
		- Oesophagitis
		- Peptic stricture
		- Barrett oesophagus
			- Premalignant lesion
			- Precursor to adenocarcinoma

	- Motility disorders

		- Primary
			- Achalasia
				- Failure of LES relaxation
				- Aperistalsis
			- Diffuse oesophageal spasm
			- Nutcracker oesophagus

		- Secondary
			- Scleroderma
			- Diabetic autonomic neuropathy

	- Stroke = most common cause of oropharyngeal dysphagia
	- Aspiration pneumonia is a major complication of dysphagia
	- Oropharyngeal dysphagia
		- Coughing
		- Choking
		- Nasal regurgitation
		- Difficulty initiating swallow
	- Oesophageal dysphagia
		- Food sticking after swallowing
		- Chest/throat catching sensation
	- Solids + liquids from onset = motility disorder
	- Solids first then liquids = mechanical obstruction
	- Achalasia = most common oesophageal motility disorder
	- Peptic stricture from GORD = most common oesophageal obstruction
	- Barrett oesophagus predisposes to adenocarcinoma
	- Head and neck SCC = most common oropharyngeal malignancy
	- Upper GI endoscopy is mandatory for suspected oesophageal dysphagia
	- ENT assessment with nasoendoscopy for suspected oropharyngeal obstruction
	- Barium swallow is a useful first-line investigation for motility disorders



# megaesophagus
- Megaoesophagus

	- Definition
		- End-stage manifestation of achalasia cardia
		- Results from severe oesophageal dilatation due to impaired oesophageal emptying

	- Epidemiology
		- Achalasia incidence
			- Approximately 1 per 100,000 population
		- Equal male and female distribution
		- Can occur at any age
		- Onset before adolescence is uncommon

	- Pathophysiology
		- Disordered oesophageal peristalsis
		- Failure of lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation
		- Progressive decompensation of oesophageal muscle
		- Leads to:
			- Oesophageal dilatation
			- Food retention
			- Megaoesophagus

	- Other causes of megaoesophagus
		- Oesophageal obstruction
			- Polyp
			- Leiomyoma
		- Secondary oesophagitis
		- Tight gastric band

	- Clinical presentation
		- Chronic cough
			- Especially nocturnal cough
		- Dysphagia
		- Regurgitation of undigested food
		- Reflux symptoms
		- Aspiration episodes
		- Recurrent chest infections

	- Important complications
		- Aspiration pneumonia
		- Severe oesophageal dilatation
		- Rare sudden death reported

	- Diagnostic clues

		- History
			- Chronic cough
			- Dysphagia
			- Reflux
			- Oesophagitis history

		- Examination
			- May be unremarkable
			- Signs of aspiration if present

		- Chest X-ray
			- Key initial investigation
			- Dilated mediastinum
			- Air-fluid level within oesophagus
			- Suggests megaoesophagus/achalasia

	- Case findings

		- Initial chest X-ray
			- Dilated mediastinum
			- Air-fluid level at level of sternal notch

		- Nasogastric tube
			- Inserted into oesophagus
			- Aspirated:
				- Thick fluid
				- Old partially masticated food
			- Subsequently repositioned into stomach

		- Upper GI endoscopy
			- Confirmed megaoesophagus
			- Large pool of thick fluid and food debris
			- Oesophageal wall
				- Oedematous
				- Friable
			- Tight lower oesophageal sphincter identified

	- Management

		- Initial
			- Nasogastric decompression
			- Removal of retained food and fluid

		- Endoscopic management
			- Oesophageal washout
			- Balloon dilatation
			- Botulinum toxin (Botox) injection into LES

		- Follow-up
			- Repeat chest X-ray
				- Resolution of air-fluid level
				- Residual gaseous distension may persist
				- Usually clinically insignificant

	- Treatment options

		- Medical/endoscopic
			- Botulinum toxin injection
				- Effective symptom relief
				- Can be repeated after 6–12 months
				- Subsequent responses may be reduced
				- Possible cause:
					- Neutralising antibodies against toxin

			- Pneumatic dilatation
				- Success rate:
					- Approximately 70–90%
				- High relapse rate

		- Surgical
			- Open surgery
			- Laparoscopic surgery
				- e.g. Heller myotomy

	- AMC MCQ Pearls

		- Achalasia → failure of LES relaxation + absent peristalsis
		- Megaoesophagus is the end-stage consequence of achalasia
		- Chronic nocturnal cough + air-fluid level on chest X-ray = think achalasia/megadoesophagus
		- Aspiration pneumonia is a major complication
		- Chest X-ray may show:
			- Dilated mediastinum
			- Air-fluid level
		- Upper GI endoscopy confirms diagnosis and excludes malignancy
		- Botox injection and pneumatic dilatation are common non-surgical treatments
		- Pneumatic dilatation success rate ≈ 70–90%
		- Surgical myotomy provides definitive treatment in many patients

# achalsia cardia

- Achalasia cardia – AMC MCQ

	- Definition
		- Primary oesophageal motility disorder
		- Failure of **lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation**
		- Loss of oesophageal peristalsis
		- Degeneration of myenteric (Auerbach) plexus neurons

---

- Clinical features
	- Progressive dysphagia ⭐
		- Solids **and liquids from onset**
	- Regurgitation of undigested food ⭐
		- Especially at night
	- Nocturnal cough
	- Aspiration
	- Chest pain
	- Weight loss
	- Halitosis
	- Food sticking in chest
	- Long history of symptoms

---

- Examination
	- Usually normal
	- Weight loss (late disease)
	- Signs of aspiration if complicated

---

- Initial investigations
	- FBC
	- U&E
	- Chest X-ray
		- May show dilated oesophagus
		- Air-fluid level
		- Absent gastric air bubble

---

- Investigation of choice (IOC)
	- Upper GI endoscopy ⭐
		- Exclude pseudoachalasia (malignancy)
		- Exclude oesophageal cancer
		- May show retained food and dilated oesophagus

---

- Definitive investigation (Gold standard)
	- Oesophageal manometry ⭐⭐⭐
		- Increased LES resting pressure
		- Incomplete LES relaxation
		- Absent peristalsis

---

- Supportive investigation
	- Barium swallow
		- Bird-beak appearance ⭐
		- Smooth tapering distal oesophagus
		- Dilated oesophagus
		- Delayed contrast emptying

---

- Differential diagnosis
	- Oesophageal carcinoma
		- Progressive dysphagia
		- Solids → liquids
		- Significant weight loss
	- Peptic stricture
	- Diffuse oesophageal spasm
	- Scleroderma
		- Low LES pressure
		- Severe reflux

---

- Management

	- Initial
		- IV fluids if dehydrated
		- Aspiration precautions
		- Nutritional support
		- Treat aspiration pneumonia if present

	- Medical (poor surgical candidates)
		- Nifedipine
		- Glyceryl trinitrate
		- Temporary symptom relief only

	- Endoscopic
		- Pneumatic balloon dilatation ⭐
		- Botulinum toxin injection
			- Elderly
			- Frail
			- High operative risk

	- Definitive treatment ⭐
		- Laparoscopic Heller myotomy
			- Usually combined with partial fundoplication
		- POEM (Peroral endoscopic myotomy)
			- Increasingly used
			- Similar efficacy

---

- Complications
	- Aspiration pneumonia ⭐
	- Megaoesophagus
	- Oesophageal candidiasis
	- Malnutrition
	- Increased risk of squamous cell carcinoma

---

- AMC Pearls ⭐
	- Dysphagia to **solids and liquids from the beginning** → Achalasia
	- Dysphagia **solids then liquids** → Oesophageal carcinoma
	- Bird-beak appearance → Barium swallow
	- Gold standard diagnosis → Oesophageal manometry
	- Always perform endoscopy first to exclude malignancy
	- Definitive treatment → Heller myotomy or POEM

# gerd

- Suggestive features
	- Retrosternal burning
	- Regurgitation
	- Open hand movement along oesophagus
- Associated with:
	- Asthma
- Response to acid suppression
	- Supports diagnosis
	- Not specific
- Alarm features requiring endoscopy/barium swallow
	- Dysphagia
	- Odynophagia
	- GI bleeding
	- Weight loss
	- Aspiration symptoms
	- Recurrent respiratory infection
- Special investigations
	- pH monitoring/manometry
		- Usually not required
		- Used before surgery
		- Exclude achalasia
- Volume reflux
	- Regurgitation without burning
	- Suggests maximal acid suppression
	- Consider fundoplication
- PPIs
	- Preferred over H2 blockers
	- More potent
	- Well tolerated
- PPI complications
	- Reduced iron absorption
	- Increased enteric infection risk
		- Clostridium difficile
- Gastroscopy indications
	- Alarm symptoms
	- Age >55 years
	- Earlier threshold in:
		- High gastric cancer risk populations
			- Asian background
	- Lower threshold with:
		- Aspirin use
		- NSAID use
	- Gastroscopy risks
		- Extreme age
		- Obesity
		- Advanced cardiac disease
		- Advanced respiratory disease
	- Barium meal
		- Less sensitive/specific
		- No tissue biopsy

- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
	- Common upper GI disorder
	- Defined as:
		- Symptoms or oesophagitis due to reflux of gastric contents into distal oesophagus

	- Symptoms should occur:
		- ≥1 day/week

	- Major mechanism
		- Lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation

	- Hiatus hernia
		- Commonly associated
		- Not the direct cause


- Diagnosis
	- Clinical diagnosis
	- Based on:
		- Symptom pattern

	- Endoscopy reserved for selected patients

- Typical symptoms
	- Heartburn
		- Burning sensation
		- Epigastrium → retrosternal → neck

		- Triggered by:
			- Meals
			- Postural change
			- Medications
			- Stress
	
		- Relieved by:
			- Antacids

	- Associated symptoms
		- Regurgitation
		- Waterbrash
		- Chest pain
		- Cough
		- Hoarseness
		- Sore throat
		- Bloating
		- Belching
		- Nausea

	- Associated disease
		- Asthma

	- Significant negative effect on quality of life

- Alarm symptoms
	- Dysphagia
	- Odynophagia
	- Haematemesis
	- Weight loss

- Disease course
	- Usually chronic + relapsing
	- Relapse common after stopping acid suppression

- Endoscopy indications
	- Alarm symptoms
	- Diagnostic uncertainty
	- Failure of initial treatment
	- Longstanding troublesome symptoms
	- Older patients
		- Especially Asian background

	- Preoperative assessment
	- Barrett’s oesophagus assessment
	- Persistent patient anxiety/reassurance

- Endoscopy findings
	- Symptoms poorly correlate with oesophagitis severity
	- <50% with typical symptoms have visible oesophagitis

- Barium studies
	- Poor for oesophagitis diagnosis
	- Useful for:
		- Motility disorders
		- Strictures
		- Large hiatus hernias
		- Pharyngeal pouches

- pH monitoring
	- Highly specialised
	- Indications
		- Uncertain diagnosis
		- Failed therapeutic trial

- Helicobacter pylori
	- Relationship with GORD controversial
	- Routine testing not recommended

	- Some experts recommend eradication if:
		- Long-term daily PPI needed

- Management goals
	- Relieve symptoms
	- Improve quality of life
	- Heal oesophagitis
	- Reduce complications

- Lifestyle/nonpharmacological management
	- Avoid provoking foods
	- Avoid bending/stooping
	- Avoid reflux-triggering activities
	- Small meals
	- Avoid late meals
	- Elevate head of bed

	- Possible provoking substances/drugs
		- Alcohol
		- Anticholinergics
		- Calcium channel blockers
		- Bisphosphonates
		- Theophylline
		- NSAIDs
		- Aspirin

	- Smoking cessation
		- General health benefit
		- GORD benefit uncertain

	- Weight loss
		- General health benefit
		- GORD benefit uncertain

- Pharmacological treatment
	- Depends on:
		- Severity
		- Chronicity
	- Not dependent on presence of oesophagitis

	- PPIs
		- Most effective treatment
		- Better than H2 antagonists

	- Antacids/H2 blockers
		- Suitable for mild intermittent symptoms

- Step-down therapy
	- Current preferred strategy

	- Initial treatment
		- Standard-dose PPI for 4–8 weeks

	- Examples
		- Omeprazole 20 mg
		- Pantoprazole 40 mg
		- Lansoprazole 30 mg
		- Rabeprazole 20 mg

	- If refractory
		- Twice-daily dosing

	- After symptom control
		- Reduce to maintenance dose
		- Trial withdrawal
		- Step down to:
			- H2 antagonists
			- Antacids

	- Failure of symptom resolution
		- Requires endoscopy

- Relapse management
	- ~70% relapse within 6 months after stopping PPI

	- Severe oesophagitis
		- Usually require ongoing therapy

	- Mild symptoms
		- Intermittent/on-demand treatment possible

- Esomeprazole
	- Superior in severe oesophagitis

- Prokinetics
	- Domperidone/metoclopramide
		- Unclear benefit

	- Cisapride
		- Withdrawn
		- Cardiotoxicity

- Surgical treatment
	- Usually unnecessary
	- Consider if:
		- Medical failure
		- Medication side effects
		- Patient unwilling for long-term medication

	- Procedure
		- Laparoscopic fundoplication

	- Outcomes
		- ~90% symptom remission at 10 years

	- Complications
		- Dysphagia
		- Early satiety/fullness
		- Flatulence

- Complications of GORD
	- Stricture
	- Ulcerative oesophagitis
	- Barrett’s oesophagus
	- Oesophageal adenocarcinoma

- Barrett’s oesophagus
	- Long-term GORD complication
	- Diagnosed only on endoscopy

	- Pathology
		- Squamous epithelium replaced by columnar epithelium

	- Cancer risk
		- Increased adenocarcinoma risk

	- Types
		- Long segment
			- >3 cm

		- Short segment
			- <3 cm
			- Lower cancer risk

	- Prevalence in endoscopy patients
		- Long segment
			- 3–5%

		- Short segment
			- 10–15%

	- Management
		- Endoscopic surveillance

	- High-grade dysplasia
		- May require:
			- Ablation
			- Local resection
			- Oesophagectomy

- Key points
	- GORD is primarily a clinical diagnosis
	- Endoscopy reserved for alarm features/treatment failure
	- PPIs are first-line
	- Step-down therapy preferred
