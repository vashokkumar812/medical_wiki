# General

* Bedside manner and establishing rapport  
  * Preparation before meeting the patient  
    * Follow hospital or clinic rules for dress, identification and permission to interview the patient.  
    * Ensure the patient is approached in a professional and respectful manner.  
    * Maintain empathy while remaining clinically objective.  
  * Introduction and consent  
    * Greet the patient politely.  
    * Introduce yourself by name and explain your role.  
    * Explain why you have come to see the patient.  
    * Confirm the patient's identity using appropriate identifiers.  
    * Obtain permission to proceed with the interview and examination.  
    * Address the patient using their preferred name and appropriate title.  
  * Privacy and comfort  
    * Invite the patient to sit comfortably.  
    * Close the door or draw the curtains to ensure privacy.  
    * Sit beside or near the patient at approximately eye level.  
    * Adopt a relaxed posture and avoid appearing rushed.  
    * Ensure the patient feels comfortable discussing sensitive information.  
  * Communication and rapport  
    * Maintain appropriate eye contact.  
    * Give the patient your attention rather than focusing continuously on the computer.  
    * Use a calm, respectful and non-judgemental tone.  
    * Show empathy and acknowledge the patient's concerns.  
    * Avoid patronising comments or insensitive questioning.  
    * Consider the patient's perspective: how would you wish to be treated in the same situation?  
    * Remain compassionate without becoming emotionally overwhelmed or losing clinical objectivity.  
  * Confidentiality  
    * Explain confidentiality and its relevant limits when appropriate.  
    * Protect the patient's personal information and medical records.  
    * Share information only for legitimate clinical purposes and in accordance with privacy requirements.  
    * When discussing a case with colleagues who are not directly involved in care, avoid identifying the patient.  
    * Remove identifying details from documents and investigations displayed during open clinical meetings.  
  * Obtaining the history  
    * Begin with an open-ended question.  
      * Could you tell me what has brought you here today?  
      * Could you tell me more about the problem you have been experiencing?  
    * Allow the patient to describe the problem in their own words.  
    * Listen actively without interrupting unnecessarily.  
    * Encourage the patient to explain the onset and progression of symptoms.  
    * Observe verbal and non-verbal communication.  
    * Summarise the patient's story to confirm your understanding.  
    * Ask focused, specific questions to clarify missing details.  
    * Follow a systematic history-taking sequence to avoid missing important symptoms.  
    * Adapt the depth of questioning to the complexity of the presenting illness.  
  * Note-taking during the interview  
    * Explain that you will take notes while continuing to listen.  
    * Record important symptoms, dates, events and relevant positive or negative findings.  
    * Maintain eye contact and engagement while writing.  
    * Avoid allowing the computer or note-taking to dominate the consultation.  
    * If electronic documentation interferes with rapport, consider completing detailed notes after the interview.  
  * Recording the history  
    * Document a clear, accurate and chronological account of the illness.  
    * Include the development, progression and course of the presenting problem.  
    * Record relevant past medical and psychiatric history.  
    * Distinguish current active problems from resolved or inactive problems.  
    * For follow-up consultations, focus on interval changes, current symptoms, treatment response and relevant updates to the history.  
    * Ensure the final record is complete, systematic and clinically relevant.  
  * Closing the consultation  
    * Summarise the key information obtained.  
    * Ask whether the patient has anything else they wish to mention.  
    * Clarify any uncertainties or important omitted details.  
    * Explain the next steps in assessment or management.  
    * Thank the patient for their time and cooperation.  
    * Aim to ensure the patient feels heard, respected and supported.  
* Introductory questions and opening the consultation  
  * Establish rapport  
    * Introduce yourself and explain your role.  
    * Confirm the patient's identity.  
    * Obtain permission to proceed.  
    * Begin with a calm, conversational and non-judgemental approach.  
  * Initial open-ended questions  
    * What has been troubling you recently?  
    * What problem have you been experiencing?  
    * When were you last feeling completely well?  
    * What made you come to the hospital or clinic today?  
    * Allow the patient to describe the main problem in their own words.  
  * Follow-up consultation  
    * How have things been going since I last saw you?  
    * What has been happening since your last visit?  
    * Have your symptoms improved, worsened or remained the same?  
    * Acknowledge the previous consultation to demonstrate continuity of care.  
  * Encourage further information  
    * Is there anything else you would like to tell me?  
    * What else have you noticed?  
    * Is there anything else that has been troubling you?  
    * If the patient stops speaking, briefly summarise what they have said and invite them to continue.  
  * Explore the presenting complaint  
    * Allow the patient to complete the initial narrative before asking detailed questions.  
    * Clarify the onset, duration, progression and severity of symptoms.  
    * Ask about associated symptoms and relevant positive and negative findings.  
    * Explore the effect of symptoms on daily activities and functioning.  
    * Avoid interrupting unnecessarily or making a premature diagnosis.  
  * Focused questions to test diagnostic possibilities  
    * Ask specific questions to identify associations the patient may not have noticed.  
    * Example: for chest discomfort, ask whether it occurs during exercise or exertion.  
    * If the patient struggles to describe a symptom, offer neutral descriptive options.  
    * Example: Is the pain sharp, dull, heavy or burning?  
    * Do not lead the patient towards a particular answer.  
  * Clarify the patient's description  
    * Can you show me exactly where you feel the pain?  
    * What do you mean when you describe it as sharp or dull?  
    * Can you describe what the sensation feels like in your own words?  
    * Clarify unfamiliar or pseudo-medical terms used by the patient rather than assuming their meaning.  
    * Recheck important details if the patient's descriptions differ during repeated questioning.  
  * Communication and listening  
    * Listen with an open mind and avoid premature diagnostic conclusions.  
    * Observe non-verbal cues, including facial expressions, gestures and behaviour.  
    * Use appropriate reassuring gestures without exaggeration.  
    * Summarise the information periodically and check that you have understood correctly.  
    * Keep a talkative patient focused on the presenting problem using polite, directed questions.  
    * Avoid introducing detailed personal or social questions too early if they are not relevant to the immediate concern.  
    * Explore broader personal and social issues when appropriate, once rapport has been established.  
  * Adapt the interview to the patient's needs  
    * Recognise hearing difficulties, speech problems or memory impairment.  
    * Adapt the pace, wording and structure of questions accordingly.  
    * Allow additional time when required.  
    * Check understanding and clarify answers without making assumptions.  
  * End of the initial history  
    * Summarise the presenting problem and its chronology.  
    * Ask whether the patient has anything else to add.  
    * Clarify inconsistencies or important missing information.  
    * Proceed to focused questions and a systematic review of relevant symptoms.

* Presenting (principal) symptom  
  * Identify the main presenting complaint  
    * What problem or symptom concerns you the most?  
    * Which symptom made you seek medical attention?  
    * Is there anything else troubling you?  
    * Record each presenting symptom in the patient's own words.  
    * Avoid converting the patient's description into medical terminology at this stage.  
    * Identify clinically significant symptoms even if the patient considers them minor.  
    * Prioritise potentially serious symptoms, even when they are not the patient's main concern.  
  * Analyse each major symptom  
    * Anatomical diagnosis: Where is the problem located?  
    * Pathological diagnosis: What is the nature of the symptom or likely disease process?  
    * Physiological and functional diagnosis: How does it affect normal functioning and daily activities?  
    * Aetiological diagnosis: What might have caused the symptom?  
* History of presenting illness (HOPI)  
  * Initial narrative  
    * Allow the patient to describe the problem in their own words.  
    * Begin with open-ended questions and avoid interrupting unnecessarily.  
    * Identify all presenting problems, including symptoms affecting different systems.  
    * Ask focused questions after the initial narrative to clarify the history.  
    * Arrange events chronologically when documenting the history.  
    * If multiple systems are involved, establish the chronology for each system.  
    * Determine whether the presenting problems are related or independent.  
  * Current symptom assessment: SOCRATES  
    * Site  
      * Where exactly is the symptom?  
      * Can you point to the affected area?  
      * Is it localised or diffuse?  
      * For non-localised symptoms, clarify the sensation and circumstances.  
    * Onset  
      * When did the symptom start?  
      * Did it begin suddenly, instantaneously or gradually?  
      * What were you doing when it started?  
      * Was the onset associated with exertion, rest, sleep or another activity?  
      * Has the symptom been continuous or intermittent?  
      * Is it getting better, worsening or remaining unchanged?  
      * When did the pattern change?  
    * Character  
      * Can you describe the symptom in your own words?  
      * If pain: is it sharp, dull, stabbing, boring, burning or cramping?  
      * If dizziness: does the room spin, or do you feel faint or about to lose consciousness?  
      * If indigestion: is it abdominal pain, heartburn, post-meal fullness, excessive wind or a change in bowel habit?  
      * Clarify exactly what the patient means by their symptom description.  
    * Radiation  
      * Does the pain spread anywhere else?  
      * Where does it start, and where does it travel?  
      * Does the distribution follow a particular anatomical pattern?  
    * Alleviating factors  
      * What makes the symptom better?  
      * Does rest or a change in position relieve it?  
      * Does anything you take relieve the symptom?  
      * Have you tried analgesics or other medications?  
      * What was the response to treatment?  
    * Timing  
      * When did you first notice the symptom?  
      * Can you recall the first unusual thing that happened?  
      * When were you last completely well?  
      * Is the symptom constant, recurrent or episodic?  
      * How long does each episode last?  
      * How frequently does it occur?  
      * Have you experienced a similar episode before?  
      * Why have you decided to seek medical attention now?  
    * Exacerbating factors  
      * What makes the symptom worse?  
      * Does it worsen with movement, exertion, eating, breathing or a particular position?  
      * Is it triggered by any specific activity or situation?  
    * Severity  
      * How severe is the symptom?  
      * Does it interfere with your normal daily activities?  
      * Does it disturb your sleep?  
      * Is it mild, moderate, severe or very severe?  
      * For pain, consider a 0–10 numerical rating scale if appropriate.  
      * Consider a faces pain scale or visual analogue scale when suitable.  
      * For breathlessness, establish the activity or distance that triggers symptoms.  
      * For chest pain, determine whether it occurs during exertion or at rest.  
      * Record functional limitations and changes in severity over time.  
      * Compare symptom severity before and after treatment when relevant.  
  * Associated symptoms  
    * What other symptoms have you noticed?  
    * Are there any symptoms occurring at the same time as the main complaint?  
    * Ask systematically about symptoms associated with the affected organ system.  
    * Explore relevant symptoms in other systems when indicated.  
    * Identify important associated symptoms and relevant negative findings.  
    * Consider the combination of symptoms rather than relying on one symptom alone.  
  * Chronology and synthesis  
    * Establish the sequence of symptom onset, progression and associated events.  
    * Clarify inconsistencies by asking the patient to describe or demonstrate the symptom again.  
    * Summarise the history and confirm that it accurately reflects the patient's experience.  
    * Ensure every significant presenting problem has been assessed.  
    * Use the history to develop likely anatomical, pathological, physiological and aetiological diagnoses.  
* Effect of the illness  
  * Effect on the patient  
    * How has this illness affected your daily life?  
    * Has it affected your ability to work, study or perform usual activities?  
    * Has it affected your physical functioning or independence?  
    * Has it affected your confidence or self-esteem?  
    * Has it caused anxiety about your health or future?  
    * Are you worried about your ability to support yourself or your family?  
  * Effect on the family  
    * How has your illness affected your family?  
    * Has anyone had to change their routine or responsibilities to care for you?  
    * Has the illness caused financial, emotional or relationship difficulties?  
    * What support have you received from family or friends?  
  * Coping and expectations  
    * How have you been coping with the illness so far?  
    * What has helped you manage the situation?  
    * What concerns you most about your condition?  
    * What are your hopes and expectations for the future?  
    * What do you understand about your illness and its likely course?  
    * What explanations have healthcare professionals given you?  
    * Have you obtained information from the internet or other sources?  
    * Is there anything about your illness or treatment that you would like clarified?  
  * Patient education  
    * Explain the likely course and prognosis realistically and sympathetically.  
    * Discuss the expected benefits and possible effects of treatment.  
    * Address the patient's concerns and misconceptions.  
    * Assess the patient's understanding and expectations.  
* Drug and treatment history  
  * Current medication history  
    * Are you currently taking any tablets, medicines, inhalers, injections or other treatments?  
    * Could you show me the medicines you are currently using?  
    * If the patient cannot recall the names, ask them to describe the medication or provide the packaging or prescription.  
    * Record the name of each medication, dose, route, frequency and duration of use.  
    * Ask why each medication was prescribed.  
    * Ask whether each medication is still being taken.  
    * Ask about effectiveness and any side effects.  
    * Check whether medications are being taken as prescribed.  
    * Ask whether any doses have been missed or medications stopped, and why.  
  * Specifically ask about medication used for  
    * Hypertension.  
    * Hyperlipidaemia.  
    * Diabetes mellitus.  
    * Arthritis and chronic pain.  
    * Anxiety or depression.  
    * Erectile dysfunction.  
    * Contraception, including oral contraceptive pills.  
    * Hormone replacement therapy.  
    * Epilepsy.  
    * Anticoagulation.  
    * Antibiotic treatment.  
    * Inhalers or puffers.  
  * Other medication preparations  
    * Over-the-counter medicines, including aspirin, antihistamines and paracetamol.  
    * NSAIDs and other analgesics.  
    * Vitamins, minerals and dietary supplements.  
    * Herbal, traditional and other natural remedies.  
    * Topical medications, creams and ointments.  
    * Transdermal patches.  
    * Subcutaneous implants, including contraceptive implants.  
    * Non-prescribed or borrowed medications.  
  * Analgesic and opioid history  
    * Do you regularly take painkillers?  
    * Which painkillers do you use, and how much do you take?  
    * How frequently and for how long have you been taking them?  
    * Do you use opioid-containing medications, such as codeine or morphine?  
    * Do you use opioid patches or other long-acting preparations?  
    * Have you experienced adverse effects or difficulty reducing or stopping these medications?  
  * Previous medication and treatment history  
    * Which medications or treatments have you used in the past?  
    * Why were they prescribed?  
    * How effective were they?  
    * Did you experience any adverse effects?  
    * Have you previously received corticosteroids?  
    * Have you received chemotherapy or other anticancer drugs?  
    * Have you undergone radiotherapy?  
    * Have you received physiotherapy or rehabilitation?  
    * Have you required dietary restrictions or therapeutic supplements?  
    * Have you undergone any other relevant non-drug treatment?  
  * Medication reconciliation  
    * Review the patient's current medication list, prescriptions or electronic records.  
    * Ask about each listed medication individually.  
    * Confirm whether the patient is still taking it and what it is used for.  
    * Identify medications that have been stopped, changed or taken differently from the record.  
    * Update the medication list when responsible for the patient's care.  
  * Drug allergies and adverse reactions  
    * Do you have any allergies to medications?  
    * Which medication caused the reaction?  
    * What exactly happened after you took it?  
    * Did you develop a rash, swelling, breathing difficulty or bronchospasm?  
    * How soon after taking the medication did the reaction occur?  
    * Did you require medical treatment?  
    * Have you taken the same medication or a related drug since then?  
    * Distinguish a suspected allergic reaction from an adverse effect or intolerance.  
    * Document the suspected medication, reaction and relevant details.  
  * Recreational and non-prescribed drug use  
    * Do you use any recreational or non-prescribed substances?  
    * Which substances have you used?  
    * When did you last use them?  
    * How much do you use, and how frequently?  
    * Ask about intravenous drug use when relevant.  
    * If injecting, ask whether needles or injecting equipment have ever been shared.  
    * In acute presentations, consider possible overdose, unknown substances or unexpected drug potency.  
    * Ask about relevant symptoms following substance use, including chest pain, dehydration, confusion or psychotic symptoms.  
    * Obtain collateral information when clinically necessary and appropriate.  
  * Non-drug treatment and lifestyle measures  
    * Have you undergone physiotherapy or rehabilitation?  
    * Have you received treatment following surgery, injury or severe illness?  
    * Are you following any prescribed dietary restrictions?  
    * Do you take therapeutic dietary supplements, such as pancreatic enzymes?  
    * Do you follow a gluten-free diet or another medically prescribed diet?  
    * Are there any other treatments or lifestyle measures you use to manage your condition?  
* Past history  
  * Introduction  
    * I would like to ask you some questions about your previous medical problems and general health, as these may affect your current investigations and treatment.  
  * Previous medical and surgical history  
    * Have you had any serious illnesses in the past?  
    * Do you have any chronic medical conditions?  
    * Have you ever undergone surgery?  
    * Have you ever been admitted to hospital?  
    * Have you had any obstetric or gynaecological problems, if relevant?  
    * Did you have any significant illnesses during childhood?  
    * Have you ever received a blood transfusion?  
      * When did you receive it?  
      * What was the reason?  
      * Were there any complications?  
    * For each relevant illness or operation, establish the diagnosis, symptoms, investigations, treatment and outcome.  
    * Clarify whether previous diagnoses were confirmed by investigations or were based on symptoms alone.  
  * Important previous conditions and procedures  
    * Have you undergone surgery for malignancy?  
    * Have you had bowel surgery?  
    * Have you undergone cardiac surgery, particularly heart valve surgery?  
    * Do you have any implanted prostheses or devices, such as joint replacements, cardiac valves or pacemakers?  
    * Are there any relevant complications or ongoing follow-up requirements?  
    * Document implanted devices because they may affect infection risk and the safety or suitability of certain investigations.  
  * Childhood illness and its impact  
    * Did you have any serious or chronic illnesses during childhood?  
    * Did these illnesses affect your schooling or educational progress?  
    * Did they prevent you from participating in sports or social activities?  
    * How did you feel about these experiences, and how did they affect you?  
  * Chronic disease management  
    * Which healthcare professionals or specialists manage your condition?  
    * Do you attend any specialist clinics?  
    * What treatment or supervision do you receive from doctors, nurses, educators or dietitians?  
    * Who do you contact if you have a problem with your treatment?  
    * Do you have an action plan for an urgent or potentially dangerous complication?  
    * How well do you understand your condition and its treatment?  
    * Do you monitor your condition at home?  
      * Diabetes: home blood glucose records and insulin adjustment plan.  
      * Heart failure: daily weight monitoring and advice for worsening symptoms.  
    * Do you adjust your medication doses yourself?  
    * Do you feel confident making these adjustments?  
    * Do you know when to seek medical advice?  
  * Immunisation history  
    * Are your routine vaccinations up to date?  
    * Have you received relevant vaccinations, including:  
      * Measles, mumps and rubella (MMR).  
      * Tetanus.  
      * Influenza.  
      * COVID-19.  
      * Hepatitis B.  
      * Human papillomavirus (HPV).  
      * Pneumococcal vaccine.  
      * Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), where indicated.  
  * Other healthcare providers  
    * Which other doctors or specialists do you currently see?  
    * Are you receiving treatment at any other hospital or clinic?  
    * Would you like a copy of my report sent to your other treating doctors?  
    * Respect the patient's choice regarding sharing information, subject to applicable legal requirements.  
* Additional history for female patients  
  * Menstrual history  
    * At what age did your periods begin?  
    * When was the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP)?  
    * Are your periods regular?  
    * How long is your usual menstrual cycle?  
    * How long does each period last?  
    * Have you reached menopause?  
    * Are your current symptoms related to any particular stage of your menstrual cycle?  
    * Are there any associated menstrual or pelvic symptoms?  
  * Pregnancy and obstetric history  
    * Is there any possibility that you could be pregnant?  
    * Have you had any previous pregnancies?  
    * Have you experienced any miscarriages?  
    * Have you had any pregnancy-related complications?  
    * Record gravida (total number of pregnancies) and para (number of births beyond the specified gestational threshold used by the clinical recording system).  
    * Consider pregnancy status before selecting investigations or medications that may pose risks during pregnancy.  
* Social history  
  * Upbringing and education  
    * Where were you born?  
    * Where have you lived?  
    * What level of education have you completed?  
    * Did you experience any difficulties with schooling?  
    * Did childhood illnesses affect your education?  
    * Consider whether the patient's educational background affects how information should be explained.  
    * Ask about relevant migration history and previous exposure to infectious diseases, such as tuberculosis.  
    * Consider relevant family ancestry when clinically indicated, such as the risk of thalassaemia or sickle cell disease.  
  * Marital status, family and social support  
    * What is your current marital or relationship status?  
    * Who lives with you at home?  
    * How is the health of your partner and children?  
    * Are there any other household members?  
    * Who provides your main support?  
    * If you need help with daily activities, who is your main caregiver?  
    * Do you have friends or relatives you can rely on?  
    * Has your illness affected your relationships or social activities?  
  * Living conditions and independence  
    * Where do you currently live?  
    * Is your accommodation suitable for your needs?  
    * Can you manage your daily activities independently?  
    * Do you need assistance with bathing, dressing, cooking or other activities?  
    * Are there stairs or other barriers that make access difficult?  
    * Is the toilet easily accessible?  
    * Do you have adequate support to remain safely at home?  
    * Do you receive home assistance, cleaning services or meal delivery services?  
    * Do you have pets at home, particularly if infection or allergy is suspected?  
  * Sexual health and spirituality  
    * Ask sensitively about sexual activity when relevant to the presenting problem.  
    * Are there any concerns about sexual function or sexual health?  
    * Do you consider yourself a spiritual or religious person?  
    * Are there spiritual or cultural beliefs that you would like us to consider in your care?  
    * Are there particular beliefs or preferences relevant to serious illness, end-of-life care or advance care planning?  
  * Mobility and transport  
    * How well are you able to walk and move around?  
    * Do you use any mobility aids?  
    * Are you still driving?  
    * How do you travel to shops, medical appointments and other activities?  
    * Does your illness limit your ability to travel independently?  
  * Diet and exercise  
    * What does your usual daily diet consist of?  
    * Do you feel you are eating adequately?  
    * Who usually prepares your meals?  
    * Do you have difficulty obtaining or preparing food?  
    * Do you receive meal delivery or other nutritional support?  
    * How physically active are you?  
    * What exercise or physical activities do you usually perform?  
    * Has your illness affected your diet, appetite or exercise tolerance?  
  * Occupational history: WHACS  
    * W — What do you do?  
      * What is your current occupation?  
      * What are your usual work duties?  
    * H — How do you do it?  
      * Describe your daily tasks and working conditions.  
      * Are you exposed to dust, chemicals, biological hazards or other occupational risks?  
    * A — Are you concerned about any exposures or experiences?  
      * Have you noticed any health problems related to your work?  
      * Have you had any relevant occupational exposures?  
    * C — Colleagues or others exposed?  
      * Have your colleagues experienced similar health problems?  
      * Are other people in your workplace exposed to the same hazards?  
    * S — Satisfied with your job?  
      * Are you satisfied with your current work?  
      * Does your work cause significant stress?  
      * Has your illness affected your ability to work?  
  * Hobbies and recreational activities  
    * What hobbies or recreational activities do you enjoy?  
    * Do you have any exposures associated with your hobbies?  
    * Do you keep birds or other animals?  
    * Do you use solvents, chemicals or other potentially hazardous materials?  
    * Could your hobbies be contributing to respiratory symptoms, allergies or other health problems?  
  * Impact of illness on social functioning  
    * Has your illness affected your relationships?  
    * Has it affected your employment or income?  
    * Has it limited your hobbies or leisure activities?  
    * Has it affected your independence or quality of life?  
    * What practical or social support would help you manage your condition?  
* Overseas travel history  
  * Have you travelled overseas recently?  
  * Which countries or regions did you visit?  
  * When did you travel, and how long did you stay?  
  * Where did you stay during the trip?  
  * Did you stay in local accommodation or international hotels?  
  * Did you eat local foods or drink untreated or unbottled water?  
  * Were you exposed to anyone with an infectious illness?  
  * Were you hospitalised or did you undergo any medical or surgical procedures overseas?  
  * Have you taken any travel-related prophylaxis, such as antimalarial medication?  
  * Are your travel-related vaccinations up to date?  
  * Consider travel-associated infections and antibiotic-resistant organisms, particularly following overseas hospitalisation.  
* Smoking history  
  * Have you ever smoked or used tobacco?  
  * Do you currently smoke?  
  * What do you smoke: cigarettes, cigars or a pipe?  
  * How many cigarettes do you smoke per day?  
  * For how many years have you smoked?  
  * Have you stopped smoking?  
  * If you stopped, when did you quit?  
  * Have you had periods when you stopped and restarted?  
  * Calculate cigarette pack-years:  
    * Pack-years \= (cigarettes smoked per day ÷ 20\) × years smoked.  
    * Example: 10 cigarettes/day for 20 years \= 10 pack-years.  
  * Ask about smoking-related risks, including cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, malignancy and peptic ulcer disease.  
  * Offer brief, non-judgemental smoking cessation advice when appropriate.  
* Alcohol history  
  * Do you drink alcohol?  
  * What type of alcohol do you drink: beer, wine or spirits?  
  * How many drinks do you usually have on a typical drinking day?  
  * How often do you drink?  
  * How much do you drink on a single occasion?  
  * Have you had episodes of heavy drinking or intoxication?  
  * Has alcohol affected your health, relationships, work or daily activities?  
  * Have you experienced withdrawal symptoms when you stop drinking?  
  * Consider asking a validated screening questionnaire.  
  * CAGE questions  
    * C — Cut down: Have you ever felt you should cut down on your drinking?  
    * A — Annoyed: Have people annoyed you by criticising your drinking?  
    * G — Guilty: Have you ever felt guilty about your drinking?  
    * E — Eye-opener: Have you ever had a drink first thing in the morning to steady your nerves or relieve a hangover?  
    * Two or more positive responses suggest possible alcohol dependence and warrant further assessment; a negative result does not exclude unhealthy drinking.  
  * AUDIT-C screening  
    * How often do you have a drink containing alcohol?  
    * How many drinks containing alcohol do you have on a typical day when drinking?  
    * How often do you have six or more drinks on one occasion?  
    * Score each question from 0 to 4 according to the standard AUDIT-C scoring system.  
    * Use the appropriate validated threshold and local guidelines to identify unhealthy alcohol use.  
  * Quantify alcohol intake accurately using the local definition of a standard drink.  
  * If the history is unclear and clinically important, consider collateral information with the patient's permission.  
* Analgesic and recreational drug history  
  * Do you regularly take painkillers or other over-the-counter medications?  
  * Which analgesics do you use, and at what doses?  
  * How frequently and for how long have you used them?  
  * Have you ever used cannabis or other recreational drugs?  
  * Have you ever injected recreational drugs?  
  * Have you used prescription medications for non-medical reasons?  
  * Ask: In the past year, how many times have you used an illegal drug or taken a prescription medication for a non-medical reason?  
  * If injecting drugs, ask about sharing needles or other injecting equipment.  
  * Establish the substance, quantity, frequency, last use and any associated complications.  
  * Consider toxicity, dependence, withdrawal and interactions with prescribed medications.  
  * Assess for possible overdose or exposure to unknown substances when clinically indicated.  
* Mood and depression screening  
  * How are things going at home and at work at the moment?  
  * Have you been feeling low, depressed or hopeless?  
  * Have you lost interest or pleasure in activities you previously enjoyed?  
  * Has your mood affected your ability to cope with your current illness?  
  * If depression is suspected, assess symptom duration, severity and functional impact.  
  * Consider medical contributors to depressive symptoms, such as hypothyroidism or Cushing's syndrome.  
  * If depression is suspected, ask directly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plans and immediate safety.  
  * Asking directly about suicide does not increase suicide risk.  
* Sexual history  
  * Establish whether a sexual history is relevant to the presenting complaint.  
  * Choose an appropriate time and ensure privacy before asking sensitive questions.  
  * Explain why the questions are relevant and seek permission to proceed.  
  * Are you currently sexually active?  
  * Do you have any concerns about your sexual health or sexual function?  
  * Ask further focused questions according to the clinical presentation, including relevant pregnancy risk, contraception, partners and sexually transmitted infection risk.  
  * Maintain a respectful, confidential and non-judgemental approach.  
* Family history  
  * Opening questions  
    * Does anyone in your family have a medical condition similar to yours?  
    * Does anyone in your family have any significant medical illnesses?  
    * Are there any illnesses that seem to run in your family?  
  * Family members and health status  
    * Are your parents alive?  
    * If deceased, at what age did they die, and what was the cause of death?  
    * Are your siblings alive and well?  
    * Do your siblings have any significant medical conditions?  
    * Do your children have any significant medical conditions, if applicable?  
    * Record relevant diagnoses, ages at onset and current health status of affected relatives.  
  * Specific familial diseases  
    * Ischaemic heart disease, particularly premature disease in parents or siblings.  
    * Hypertension and hyperlipidaemia, where relevant.  
    * Diabetes mellitus, where relevant.  
    * Malignancies, particularly breast cancer and colorectal cancer.  
    * Haemophilia and other known inherited disorders.  
    * Epilepsy.  
    * Psychiatric or other relevant neurological disorders.  
    * Any other disease relevant to the presenting complaint.  
  * Assess possible genetic risk  
    * Are several relatives affected by the same disorder?  
    * Did the disease occur at an unusually young age in any relative?  
    * Has the disease occurred in relatives of a sex in which it is less common?  
    * Has the disease occurred despite the absence of usual risk factors?  
    * Is there a relevant ancestral or population background associated with the disease?  
    * Are the patient's parents biologically related, such as being first cousins?  
  * Family history of malignancy  
    * Which relatives had cancer?  
    * What type of cancer did they have?  
    * At what age were they diagnosed?  
    * Were multiple relatives affected by the same or related cancers?  
    * Were cancers diagnosed at unusually young ages?  
    * Clarify details when the reported diagnosis or family history is uncertain.  
  * Family pedigree  
    * Construct a family tree if a hereditary condition is suspected.  
    * Include first-degree relatives and other relevant family members across generations.  
    * Record each relative's relationship to the patient.  
    * Document age, current health status or age and cause of death.  
    * Record the diagnosis and age at onset for affected relatives.  
    * Identify patterns suggesting inherited disease.  
  * Consanguinity  
    * Are your parents biologically related?  
    * Are they blood relatives, such as first cousins?  
    * Explore consanguinity when the pedigree or suspected disorder suggests an inherited condition, particularly an autosomal recessive disorder.  
  * Clinical interpretation  
    * Consider both genetic inheritance and shared environmental exposures.  
    * Recognise that family history may be incomplete or inaccurate.  
    * Ask sensitively about cancer, epilepsy and mental illness, as patients may be reluctant to disclose these.  
    * Identify patterns that may warrant further assessment or genetic counselling.
