Definition
	- Acoustic neuroma = vestibular schwannoma
	- Benign, slow-growing tumour arising from Schwann cells of the vestibular division of CN VIII
	- Most commonly arises from the superior vestibular nerve
	- Usually located in the internal auditory canal with extension into the cerebellopontine angle (CPA)

- Etiology
	- Usually sporadic
	- Associated with Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2)
		- Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are highly suggestive of NF2
	- Previous cranial irradiation may increase risk

- Pathogenesis
	- Schwann cell proliferation within the vestibular nerve
	- Progressive enlargement causes compression of:
		- Cochlear nerve → hearing loss
		- Vestibular nerve → imbalance
		- Facial nerve (CN VII) → facial weakness
		- Trigeminal nerve (CN V) → facial numbness
		- Cerebellum and brainstem → neurological deficits
		- CSF pathways → hydrocephalus (large tumours)

- Most common
	- Most common cerebellopontine angle (CPA) tumour
	- Most common symptom = unilateral sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL)
	- Most common presentation = progressive unilateral SNHL with unilateral tinnitus
	- Most common nerve of origin = superior vestibular nerve

- Epidemiology
	- Typically presents between 40–60 years
	- Slight female predominance
	- Usually unilateral
	- Bilateral disease is uncommon and suggests NF2

- Clinical features
	- Progressive unilateral SNHL
	- Unilateral tinnitus
	- Disequilibrium or imbalance
	- Aural fullness
	- Poor speech discrimination
	- Vertigo (less common)
	- Facial numbness or paraesthesia (CN V involvement)
	- Facial weakness (late feature)
	- Headache and symptoms of raised ICP in large tumours
	- Cerebellar symptoms in advanced disease

- Examination
	- Otoscopy usually normal
	- Findings of unilateral SNHL on bedside hearing tests
	- Reduced corneal reflex if CN V compressed
	- Facial nerve weakness in advanced disease
	- Cerebellar signs in large tumours

- Diagnosis
	- Suspect in any patient with:
		- Unilateral progressive SNHL
		- Unilateral tinnitus
		- Asymmetrical audiometry findings
	- Confirmed by MRI with gadolinium contrast

- Investigations
	- Initial
		- Pure-tone audiometry
			- Asymmetrical SNHL
		- Speech discrimination testing
			- Often disproportionately poor
	- Most appropriate (gold standard)
		- MRI with gadolinium contrast
			- Detects small tumours
			- Defines tumour size and extent
	- Additional
		- Auditory brainstem response (ABR)
		- CT scan for surgical planning

- Treatment
	- Depends on:
		- Tumour size
		- Symptoms
		- Hearing status
		- Patient age
		- Medical comorbidities
		- Available facilities and expertise
	- Management options:
		- Observation
		- Radiation
		- Surgery

	- Initial
		- Observation with serial MRI for:
			- Small tumours
			- Elderly patients
			- Significant medical comorbidities
			- Minimal symptoms
		- Hearing aids may be required if hearing loss affects communication
		- Advise avoidance of further noise exposure and other ototoxic influences

	- Most appropriate (definitive)
		- Microsurgical excision (gold standard)
			- Aim:
				- Total tumour removal
				- Minimise morbidity
			- Approaches:
				- Translabyrinthine approach
					- Large tumours
					- Poor pre-operative hearing
					- Hearing sacrificed
				- Middle cranial fossa approach
					- Hearing preservation
					- Small tumours with good hearing
				- Retrosigmoid (posterior cranial fossa) approach
					- Hearing preservation possible
		- Stereotactic radiation therapy
			- Tumours ≤ 3 cm
			- Particularly useful in elderly or medically unfit patients
			- Goal = halt tumour growth

- Prognosis
	- Generally excellent with treatment
	- Treatment aims:
		- Limit morbidity
		- Prevent mortality from tumour growth
		- Preserve hearing and facial nerve function when possible
	- Cure/control rates:
		- Approximately 90–99% with surgery
		- Approximately 90–99% with stereotactic radiation
	- Long-term MRI follow-up often required



- Vascular associations
	- Increased SSNHL risk with
		- Diabetes mellitus
		- Coronary artery disease
	- Better vascular risk factor control may help

- Sarcoidosis
	- Rare cause of SSNHL
	- May involve eighth cranial nerve

- Autoimmune associations
	- SLE
	- Antiphospholipid syndrome
	- Other autoimmune diseases

- Infective causes
	- Syphilis
		 - Established cause
		 - Early treatment may improve outcome
	- Viral illnesses may contribute

- Audiological rehabilitation
	- Important in permanent hearing loss
	- Often multidisciplinary
