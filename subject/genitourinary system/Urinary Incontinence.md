# Urinary Incontinence

* Definition
	- International Continence Society (ICS):
		- Urinary incontinence (UI) is the complaint of any involuntary leakage of urine.
	- Epidemiology
		- More common in women.
		- Prevalence increases with age.
		- Can significantly affect:
			- Physical health
			- Psychological wellbeing
			- Social activities
			- Quality of life
		- Frequently under-reported due to social stigma or embarrassment.
		- Some women with urinary incontinence also experience faecal incontinence.

* Normal continence physiology
	- Lower urinary tract has two functional phases:
		- Storage phase
			- Bladder relaxes and accommodates urine at low pressure.
			- Bladder outlet remains closed.
			- Urethral pressure remains higher than intravesical pressure.
			- Pelvic floor and external urethral sphincter maintain continence.
		- Voiding phase
			- Bladder detrusor contracts.
			- Bladder outlet and urethral sphincter relax.
			- Urine is expelled.
	- Structures maintaining continence
		- Bladder
		- Bladder neck
		- Urethral smooth muscle
		- External urethral sphincter (rhabdosphincter)
		- Pelvic floor muscles
		- Surrounding connective tissue
	- Neurological control
		- Sympathetic nervous system
			- Promotes urine storage.
			- Contracts bladder neck.
			- Inhibits detrusor contraction.
		- Parasympathetic nervous system (S2–S4)
			- Promotes voiding.
			- Contracts detrusor muscle.
		- Somatic nervous system (Onuf's nucleus, S2–S4)
			- Controls voluntary external urethral sphincter contraction.
		- Pontine micturition centre
			- Coordinates bladder contraction and sphincter relaxation.
		- Cerebral cortex
			- Provides voluntary control over micturition.

* Types of urinary incontinence

	- Stress urinary incontinence (SUI)
		- ICS definition
			- Involuntary leakage on effort or exertion, or on sneezing or coughing.
		- Mechanism
			- Increased intra-abdominal pressure causes intravesical pressure to exceed urethral pressure.
			- Due to:
				- Pelvic floor weakness
				- Urethral sphincter weakness
				- Bladder neck hypermobility
				- Intrinsic sphincter deficiency
		- Typical history
			- Leakage while coughing.
			- Leakage while sneezing.
			- Leakage while laughing.
			- Leakage during exercise or lifting.
		- Risk factors
			- Childbirth
			- Pelvic floor injury
			- Menopause
			- Pelvic surgery or trauma
			- Obesity

	- Urge urinary incontinence (UUI)
		- ICS definition
			- Involuntary leakage accompanied by or immediately preceded by urgency.
			- Urgency is a sudden compelling desire to pass urine that is difficult to defer.
		- Mechanism
			- Detrusor overactivity.
			- Reduced bladder compliance.
		- Detrusor overactivity
			- Involuntary detrusor contractions during the bladder filling phase.
			- May be:
				- Idiopathic
				- Neurogenic
		- Neurological causes
			- Stroke
			- Parkinson disease
			- Multiple sclerosis
			- Spinal cord pathology
		- Typical history
			- Sudden urgency.
			- Unable to reach the toilet in time.
			- Leakage following urgency.
			- Frequency and nocturia.

	- Mixed urinary incontinence (MUI)
		- ICS definition
			- Involuntary leakage associated with urgency and also with exertion, effort, sneezing or coughing.
		- Combination of:
			- Stress urinary incontinence
			- Urge urinary incontinence

	- Overflow incontinence
		- Mechanism
			- Incomplete bladder emptying with urinary retention.
			- Bladder becomes excessively distended.
			- Small amounts of urine leak continuously or intermittently.
		- Causes
			- BPH
			- Urethral stricture
			- Detrusor underactivity
			- Neurogenic bladder
			- Diabetic autonomic neuropathy
		- Typical history
			- Continuous or frequent small-volume dribbling.
			- Weak urinary stream.
			- Hesitancy.
			- Sensation of incomplete bladder emptying.

	- Quiet dribble incontinence
		- Variable mechanism.
		- May be due to overflow from incomplete bladder emptying.

	- Continuous urinary leakage
		- Vesicovaginal fistula.
		- Ectopic ureter.
		- Patulous urethra.

	- Reflex incontinence
		- Involuntary leakage without warning or awareness.
		- Associated with neuropathic bladder.

	- Functional incontinence
		- Normal or relatively preserved urinary function, but inability to reach or use the toilet.
		- Causes:
			- Restricted mobility
			- Cognitive impairment
			- Environmental barriers

* Causes of urinary incontinence

	- DIAPPERS mnemonic
		| Letter | Cause | Examples / Notes |
		|---|---|---|
		| D | Delirium / Dementia | Acute confusion affecting continence awareness |
		| I | Infection | UTI |
		| A | Atrophic urethritis | Postmenopausal urogenital atrophy |
		| P | Pharmacological | Diuretics, sedatives, anticholinergics |
		| P | Psychological | Acute distress, depression |
		| E | Endocrine | Hypercalcaemia, diabetes insipidus |
		| E | Environmental | Unfamiliar surroundings, inaccessible toilet |
		| R | Restricted mobility | Arthritis, stroke, physical disability |
		| S | Stool impaction | Faecal impaction causing bladder dysfunction |
		| S | Sphincter damage or weakness | Pelvic floor or urethral sphincter injury |

* Drugs causing or aggravating urinary incontinence

	| Drug group / Mechanism | Examples / Notes | Type of incontinence caused or aggravated |
	|---|---|---|
	| ACE inhibitors | Enalapril, lisinopril | May aggravate stress leakage through cough |
	| Alpha-adrenergic blockers | Phenoxybenzamine, prazosin, labetalol | May reduce outlet resistance and aggravate leakage |
	| Bladder relaxants (↓ detrusor contraction) | Anticholinergics, tricyclic antidepressants | Overflow incontinence |
	| Bladder stimulants (↑ detrusor activity) | Cholinergic agents, caffeine | Urge incontinence |
	| Sedatives / CNS depressants | Antidepressants, antihistamines, antipsychotics, hypnotics, tranquillisers | Urge / functional incontinence |
	| Others | Alcohol, loop diuretics (e.g. frusemide), other diuretics, lithium | Urge incontinence |

* History taking

	- Presenting symptoms
		- "Do you ever leak urine without intending to?"
		- "When did the leakage first start?"
		- "How frequently does it happen?"
		- "How much urine do you usually lose?"
		- "Does it happen during the day, at night or both?"
		- "Does it interfere with your daily activities?"
		- "Do you use any pads or other protection?"

	- Identify the type of incontinence
		- Leakage during coughing, sneezing, laughing or exercise
			- Stress urinary incontinence
		- Sudden compelling urge followed by leakage
			- Urge urinary incontinence
		- Leakage with both exertion and urgency
			- Mixed urinary incontinence
		- Continuous dribbling with incomplete emptying
			- Overflow incontinence
		- Continuous leakage without normal voiding
			- Fistula
			- Ectopic ureter
		- Leakage without awareness or warning
			- Reflex incontinence
			- Neuropathic bladder
		- Leakage because of inability to reach the toilet
			- Functional incontinence

	- Associated lower urinary tract symptoms
		- Storage symptoms
			- Frequency
			- Urgency
			- Nocturia
			- Urinary incontinence
		- Voiding symptoms
			- Hesitancy
			- Weak urinary stream
			- Intermittent stream
			- Straining
			- Terminal dribbling
			- Incomplete bladder emptying
		- Other symptoms
			- Dysuria
			- Haematuria
			- Recurrent UTI

	- Relevant history
		- Previous urinary tract infection.
		- Diabetes mellitus and glycaemic control.
		- Constipation.
		- Previous childbirth and obstetric complications.
		- Previous pelvic surgery or trauma.
		- Menopausal status.
		- Neurological disease.
		- Mobility and cognitive impairment.
		- Medication history, particularly diuretics and alpha blockers.
		- Fluid intake and caffeine consumption.
		- Bowel symptoms, including faecal incontinence.

* Examination

	- General examination
		- Mobility and ability to reach the toilet.
		- Cognitive status.
		- Body habitus and obesity.
		- Peripheral oedema.
		- Signs of systemic illness.

	- Abdominal examination
		- Palpate for a distended bladder.
		- Assess for pelvic or abdominal masses.
		- Assess for tenderness.

	- Neurological examination
		- Perform if neurological pathology is suspected.
		- Assess for:
			- Upper motor neuron lesions
				- Multiple sclerosis
				- Parkinson disease
				- Stroke
			- Lower motor neuron or sacral nerve lesions
				- Sacral nerve root pathology
				- Spinal cord or cauda equina pathology

	- Pelvic examination in women
		- Assess:
			- Oestrogen status and urogenital atrophy.
			- Pelvic organ prolapse.
			- Urethral meatal abnormalities.
			- Pelvic floor muscle tone.
			- Urinary leakage during coughing or Valsalva manoeuvre.
		- Pelvic floor muscle strength
			- Ask the patient to contract the muscles around the examining finger.
			- Assess the strength of contraction.

* Diagnosis and investigations

	- Urine microscopy and culture
		- Exclude UTI.
		- Assess for haematuria.
		- Check for glycosuria.

	- Post-void residual (PVR) urine volume
		- Measure using bladder ultrasound.
		- >100 mL is abnormal according to the supplied notes.
		- Helps identify incomplete bladder emptying and urinary retention.

	- Bladder diary / frequency-volume chart
		- Record:
			- Fluid intake.
			- Frequency of urination.
			- Volume of urine passed.
			- Episodes of leakage.
			- Time of day or night.
			- Circumstances of leakage.
			- Pad usage.
		- Useful for initial assessment and monitoring treatment response.

	- Urodynamic studies
		- Cystometry or other urodynamic assessment.
		- Consider when:
			- Suspected voiding difficulty.
			- Neuropathy.
			- Previous treatment failure.
			- Considering surgery.
			- Complex or unclear symptoms.

	- Additional assessment
		- Consider specialist neurological assessment if CNS pathology is suspected.
		- Haematuria or persistent irritative symptoms require assessment to exclude urinary tract malignancy.

	- Severity assessment
		- Question 1: "How often do you experience urine leakage?"
			- 0 = Never
			- 1 = Less than once a month
			- 2 = One or several times a month
			- 3 = One or several times a week
			- 4 = Every day and/or night
		- Question 2: "How much urine do you lose each time?"
			- 1 = Drops or little
			- 2 = More
		- Total score
			- Frequency score × Amount score
			- 0 = Dry
			- 1–2 = Slight
			- 3–4 = Moderate
			- 6–8 = Severe

* Management

	- General principles
		- Identify the type of incontinence.
		- Identify and treat reversible causes.
		- Improve symptoms and quality of life.
		- Start with conservative management where appropriate.
		- Consider pharmacological treatment or specialist referral if conservative treatment fails.

	- Lifestyle interventions
		- Weight reduction
			- Particularly in overweight or obese patients.
			- May improve stress and urge incontinence.
		- Fluid modification
			- Reduce excessive fluid intake if urinary frequency is problematic.
			- Avoid excessive fluid restriction.
			- Maintain adequate hydration.
		- Reduce bladder irritants
			- Caffeine
			- Alcohol
			- Carbonated beverages
		- Avoid constipation
			- Treat constipation.
			- Avoid excessive straining.
		- Exercise and physical activity
			- Encourage appropriate physical activity.

	- Stress urinary incontinence

		- First-line: Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT)
			- Supervised by continence nurse or physiotherapist.
			- Continue for at least 3 months before assessing response.
			- Best candidates:
				- Motivated patients.
				- Predominantly stress urinary incontinence.
			- Technique
				- Contract pelvic floor muscles as if stopping urine flow or controlling diarrhoea.
				- Hold contraction.
				- Relax completely.
				- Repeat multiple times.
				- Perform several sets daily.
			- Structured training from the supplied article
				- Three sets of 8–12 slow maximal contractions.
				- Sustain each contraction for 6–8 seconds.
				- Perform 3–4 times per week.
				- Continue for 3–4 months before assessing success.
			- Additional measures
				- Weight reduction if obese.
				- Vaginal oestrogen if postmenopausal with urogenital atrophy.

		- Management pathway

			```mermaid
			flowchart TD
			    A["Predominantly stress incontinence"] --> B["Pelvic floor muscle training for at least 3 months"]
			    B --> C["Supervision by continence nurse or physiotherapist"]
			    C --> D["Weight reduction if obese"]
			    C --> E["Vaginal oestrogen if postmenopausal with atrophy"]
			    C --> F{"Response to treatment?"}
			    F -->|Improved| G["Continue conservative management"]
			    F -->|Persistent symptoms| H["Consider specialist referral"]
			    A -->|Severe symptoms| H
			    H --> I["Consider surgical treatment"]
			```

		- Pharmacological options
			- Medical treatments are generally less effective for stress incontinence.
			- Duloxetine (SNRI)
				- May improve stress urinary incontinence.
				- Increases urethral outlet resistance.
				- Adverse effects:
					- Nausea
					- Fatigue
					- Dry mouth
					- Constipation
			- Imipramine (tricyclic antidepressant)
				- May reduce detrusor contractility.
				- Increases outlet resistance.
				- Evidence and use are limited.

		- Surgical treatment
			- Consider specialist referral if severe symptoms or conservative treatment failure.
			- Options:
				- Midurethral sling.
				- Colposuspension.
				- Pubovaginal sling using rectus fascia.
				- Periurethral bulking agents.

	- Urge urinary incontinence

		- First-line: Conservative management
			- Lifestyle modifications.
			- Bladder training.
			- Pelvic floor muscle training.
			- Urge suppression techniques.

		- Bladder training
			- Scheduled voiding programme.
				- Gradually increase the time between voids.
				- Initial interval in the supplied article: 1 hour during waking hours.
				- Increase by 15–30 minutes per week.
				- Aim for a 2–3 hour voiding interval.
			- Urge suppression techniques
				- Distraction.
				- Relaxation.
				- Pelvic floor muscle contraction.
				- Delay voiding when possible and allow the urge to pass.
			- Best performed under supervision of:
				- Continence nurse.
				- Physiotherapist.

		- Pharmacological treatment
			- Anticholinergic / antimuscarinic drugs
				- Reduce involuntary detrusor contractions mediated by acetylcholine.
				- Used for overactive bladder and urge urinary incontinence.
				- Drug options:
					- Oxybutynin
						- Oral: 2.5–5 mg BD or TDS.
						- Transdermal patch: 3.9 mg twice weekly.
					- Solifenacin
						- 5–10 mg orally daily.
					- Tolterodine
						- 2 mg orally BD.
					- Darifenacin
						- 7.5–15 mg orally daily.
				- Important adverse effects
					- Dry mouth.
					- Constipation.
					- Blurred vision.
					- Confusion and sedation, particularly in older patients.
					- May precipitate acute urinary retention.
				- Use caution in patients with:
					- Urinary retention.
					- Significant constipation.
					- Cognitive impairment.
					- High anticholinergic burden.

			- Hormonal therapy
				- Vaginal oestrogen may help postmenopausal women with urogenital atrophy.
				- Systemic oestrogen replacement is not established as treatment for urinary incontinence.

		- Refractory urge incontinence
			- Consider specialist referral.
			- Intravesical botulinum toxin A
				- Reduces detrusor overactivity.
			- Neuromodulation
				- Electrical stimulation of sacral nerves.
			- More invasive specialist options
				- Detrusor myomectomy.
				- Bladder augmentation.
				- Ileal conduit urinary diversion as a last resort.

		- Management pathway

			```mermaid
			flowchart TD
			    A["Predominantly urge incontinence"] --> B["Lifestyle modifications"]
			    A --> C["Bladder training and PFMT"]
			    B --> D["Reassess symptoms"]
			    C --> D
			    D -->|Persistent symptoms| E["Antimuscarinic medication"]
			    E --> F["Reassess response and adverse effects"]
			    F -->|Refractory symptoms| G["Specialist referral"]
			    G --> H["Intravesical botulinum toxin A"]
			    G --> I["Sacral neuromodulation"]
			    A --> J["Postmenopausal urogenital atrophy"]
			    J --> K["Consider vaginal oestrogen"]
			```

	- Overflow urinary incontinence
		- Identify and treat the underlying cause.
		- Assess post-void residual volume.
		- Evaluate for:
			- BPH.
			- Urethral stricture.
			- Detrusor underactivity.
			- Neurogenic bladder.
		- Manage urinary retention appropriately.
		- Review medications that impair detrusor contraction or bladder emptying.

	- Functional urinary incontinence
		- Treat reversible causes.
		- Improve access to toilets.
		- Provide mobility assistance.
		- Consider scheduled toileting.
		- Address cognitive impairment and environmental barriers.

* Important complications and clinical considerations

	- Psychological distress and social embarrassment.
	- Reduced quality of life.
	- Skin irritation and dermatitis.
	- Recurrent UTI, particularly with incomplete emptying.
	- Urinary retention.
	- Associated faecal incontinence.
	- Falls associated with urgency and nocturia.

* AMC Clinical Key Points

	- Leakage during coughing, sneezing or exercise
		- Stress urinary incontinence.
		- Pelvic floor muscle training is first-line.
	- Sudden urgency followed by leakage
		- Urge urinary incontinence.
		- Bladder training and lifestyle modification are first-line.
	- Both stress and urge symptoms
		- Mixed urinary incontinence.
	- Continuous dribbling with weak stream and incomplete emptying
		- Consider overflow incontinence.
		- Check post-void residual volume.
	- Continuous leakage following pelvic surgery or childbirth
		- Consider vesicovaginal fistula.
	- Urinary leakage with neurological disease
		- Consider neurogenic bladder.
	- New urinary symptoms with haematuria
		- Investigate for urinary tract pathology, including malignancy.
	- Older patient with new incontinence
		- Remember DIAPPERS and review medications, infection, constipation, mobility and cognition.
