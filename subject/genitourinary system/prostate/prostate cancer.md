## Risk Factors
- Increasing age (most important).
- Family history
	- Father or brother diagnosed before age 65 years.
- BRCA mutations
	- First-degree relative with familial breast cancer.
	- BRCA1.
	- BRCA2.
- Environmental factors
	- Possibly high dietary fat.

## Pathology
- Usually begins in the peripheral zone.



## diagnosis
- May remain asymptomatic even after metastasis
- Incidental diagnosis
	- Elevated PSA.
	- Histological diagnosis after TURP.
- Symptomatic disease
	- Lower urinary tract obstruction (rapidly progressive).
	- Acute urinary retention.
	- Haematuria.
	- Uraemia.
	- Back pain.
	- Bone pain.
		- Pelvis.
		- Vertebrae.
	- Weight loss.
	- Fatigue.
	- Perineal pain.
- Digital Rectal Examination (DRE)
	- May be completely normal.
	- Typical advanced findings
		- Hard gland.
		- Nodular gland.
		- Irregular gland.
		- Loss of median sulcus.
		- Poorly defined borders.
		- Large tumour may obliterate median sulcus.
	- Abnormal DRE signs
		- Hard lump.
		- Asymmetry.
		- Induration.
		- Loss of median sulcus.

- Investigations
	- PSA
		- Prostate-specific, NOT prostate cancer-specific.
		- Should be interpreted with DRE.
		- Can be elevated without cancer
			- BPH
			- Infection
			- Exercise
			- Recent ejaculation
			- Instrumentation
	- PSA Interpretation
		- <4 ng/mL
			- Usually normal.
			- 15–25% of cancers still occur.
		- 4–10 ng/mL
			- Intermediate suspicion.
		- >10 ng/mL
			- Strongly suggests cancer.
		- >20 ng/mL
			- Suggests metastatic disease.
	- Age-specific PSA
		- 40–49 years
			- 0.25–2.5 ng/mL
		- 50–59 years
			- 0.25–3.5 ng/ml
		- 60–69 years
			- 0.3–5.5 ng/mL
		- 70–79 years
			- 0.3–7.5 ng/mL.

	- Additional PSA Parameters
		- Free/Total PSA ratio
			- Lower (<8%) suggests prostate cancer.
			- Useful when PSA = 4–10 ng/mL.
		- PSA above age-related median
			- May require closer monitoring.
		- PSA kinetics
			- PSA velocity.
			- PSA doubling time.
			- Rapid rise → consider urology review.
		- Overall role remains controversial.

- Core Biopsy
	- Indications
		- Positive DRE.
		- Elevated PSA.
		- Depends on overall risk profile.
	- Technique
		- TRUS-guided biopsy.
		- Transperineal biopsy.
	- Only definitive diagnostic test.

- Grading
	- Gleason Score
		- Sum of two most common histological patterns.
		- Each graded 1–5.
		- Total score
			- Minimum = 2.
			- Maximum = 10.
	- Gleason Risk
		- 2–4
			- Low.
		- 5–6
			- Moderate.
		- 7
			- Intermediate.
		- 8–10
			- High
	- **Mnemonic**
		- **2-4 Low**
		- **5-6 Moderate**
		- **7 Intermediate**
		- **8-10 High**



	- Bone metastasis
		- Axial skeleton involved in ~85% of fatal cases.

	- Stage grouping
		- Stage I → Stage IV.
		- Determines prognosis and treatment.

## Treatment
- Depends on
	- Gleason grade.
	- TNM stage.
	- PSA.
	- Age.
	- Life expectancy.
	- Comorbidities.
	- Patient preference.
	- Psychosocial considerations.

- Localised Prostate Cancer
	- Radical Prostatectomy
		- Best candidates
			- Life expectancy >10 years.
			- Low surgical risk.
			- Low PSA.
			- Localised disease.
		- Approaches
			- Open.
			- Laparoscopic.
			- Robot-assisted.
		- Major complications
			- Urinary incontinence.
			- Erectile dysfunction.
	- Radiotherapy
		- External beam.
		- Brachytherapy.
		- Adverse effects
			- Urinary frequency.
			- Faecal urgency.
			- Diarrhoea.
			- Erectile dysfunction.
	- Active Surveillance
		- Suitable for
			- Low-risk disease.
			- Limited life expectancy.
			- Patients wishing to avoid treatment complications.
		- Monitoring
			- PSA.
			- Repeat biopsy.
			- Clinical review.
- Locally Advanced / Metastatic Disease
	- Main treatment
		- Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).
	- Options
		- Bilateral orchidectomy.
		- Anti-androgens
			- Cyproterone.
			- Flutamide.
			- Bicalutamide.
			- Abiraterone.
		- LHRH agonists
			- Goserelin.
			- Leuprorelin.
	- Important point
		- LHRH agonists initially increase testosterone.
		- Start anti-androgen first to prevent tumour flare.
	- Additional therapies
		- Radiotherapy for bone metastases.
		- Palliative treatment.
		- Combination hormonal therapy.

- Pathophysiology
	- usually adenocarcinoma
	- commonly arises from peripheral zone of prostate

- Risk Factors
	- age >60 years
	- family history
	- African American ethnicity

- Classification
	- Gleason score

- Spread

	- Local Spread
		- local lymph nodes
		- through prostatic capsule
			- seminal vesicles
			- rectum

	- Metastasis
		- local lymph nodes
		- bone
		- supra-diaphragmatic lymph nodes
		- lung

- Diagnosis

	- Clinical Features

		- Early disease
			- usually asymptomatic

		- Origin
			- peripheral zone

		- LUTS
			- hesitancy
			- poor stream
			- usually due to BPH

		- Bone pain
			- suggests advanced disease

	- Investigations

		- Digital Rectal Examination (DRE)

			- essential in older men

			- irregular prostate
				- ~50% malignant

			- 20–25% prostate cancers may have normal PSA

			- DRE may be only abnormal finding

			- clinical importance
				- normal PSA does not exclude malignancy
				- DRE remains essential

		- Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA)

			- glycoprotein produced predominantly by prostate epithelium

			- circulates as
				- free (unbound) PSA
				- bound (complexed) PSA

			- biological variation
				- may vary by ~20% over days

			- Factors Increasing PSA
				- prostate biopsy
				- TURP
				- prostatitis
				- urinary retention
				- ejaculation within 1 hour before testing

			- Factors Reducing PSA
				- finasteride
				- anti-androgen therapy
				- radiotherapy

			- Factors NOT Significantly Affecting PSA
				- DRE
				- alpha blockers
					- prazosin
					- tamsulosin

		- Free-to-Total PSA Ratio

			- useful in
				- younger patients
				- PSA 4–10 ng/mL

			- prostate cancer
				- predominantly bound PSA
				- ↓ free PSA ratio

			- BPH
				- ↑ free PSA ratio

			- interpretation
				- >22–25%
					- low risk of prostate cancer
				- <22–25%
					- further evaluation recommended
					- even if total PSA normal

		- PSA Velocity

			- rate of PSA change over time

			- concerning threshold
				- >0.75 ng/mL/year

			- PSA above age-expected value
				- does not confirm carcinoma
				- warrants urology referral

		- TRUS + Prostate Biopsy

			- indications
				- abnormal DRE
				- PSA greater than expected for age
				- progressive PSA rise
				- evidence of advanced disease without histological confirmation

			- settings
				- radiology suite
				- consulting suite
				- day surgery unit
				- operating theatre (most common)

			- preparation
				- preparatory enema
				- rectal Betadine swab

			- TRUS technique
				- ultrasound probe inserted into rectum
				- prostate visualised
				- biopsy needle passed through rectal wall into prostate

			- biopsy protocol
				- 12–14 core biopsies
				- systematic sampling of prostate zones

			- antibiotic prophylaxis
				- mandatory

				- example regimen
					- norfloxacin 400 mg orally BD
						- day before biopsy
						- day of biopsy
						- day after biopsy

			- complications
				- ~1% risk gram-negative sepsis
				- pain/discomfort
				- rectal bleeding

		- Bone Scan

			- radioisotope bone scan

			- most sensitive test for bony metastasis

			- metastasis commonly in axial skeleton

			- if PSA <10
				- no bone scan required

		- CT / MRI
			- no routine role initially

	- Indications for Lymph Node Sampling During Radical Retropubic Prostatectomy
		- PSA >10
		- TRUS biopsy Gleason score >7

- Population Screening

	- routine population screening
		- not currently recommended in most countries

	- reason
		- lack of definitive RCT evidence showing improved survival

	- epidemiological observations
		- many countries show reduced mortality
		- possible reasons
			- increased testing
			- earlier detection
			- earlier treatment

	- strongest evidence
		- Tyrol region, Austria
			- free PSA screening >10 years
			- 40–50% reduction in mortality

	- major trials
		- PLCO trial (USA)
			- Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian cancer study

		- ERSPC (Europe)
			- European Randomised Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer

	- current dilemma
		- absence of robust RCT evidence
		- epidemiological evidence suggests benefit
		- uncertainty about benefit vs harm

	- current approach
		- informed decision-making

		- discuss with patients
			- PSA limitations
			- false positives
			- need for TRUS biopsy
			- treatment side effects
			- possible mortality reduction
				- not guaranteed

	- asymptomatic men
		- testing should include
			- PSA
			- DRE

		- rationale
			- normal PSA does not exclude cancer

	- early treatment
		- may cause morbidity
		- may not improve survival in some men

	- Australian professional bodies
		- do not endorse population-wide screening

- Management

	- Hormone-Dependent Metastatic Prostate Cancer

		- androgen ablation
			- bilateral orchidectomy
			- GnRH analogues
				- with or without anti-androgens

	- GnRH Analogues

		- advantages
			- reversible
			- equivalent to orchidectomy

		- disadvantages
			- initial tumour flare

	- Alternatives
		- aminoglutethamide
		- ketoconazole

	- Hormone-Refractory Disease
		- some tumour cells become refractory to hormonal therapy

	- Symptomatic Relief
		- radiotherapy
		- chemotherapy

	- Maximum Androgen Blockade (MAB)

		- combination
			- orchidectomy or GnRH analogue
			- + anti-androgen

		- use
			- low-volume metastatic disease

# gleason score
* Definition  
  * histopathology of prostate ⇒ predicts tumor aggressiveness and prognosis.  
* Calculation  
  * Gleason Score \= Primary \+ Secondary  
* Gleason Patterns

| Pattern | Histology |
| ----- | ----- |
| 3 | Well-formed separate glands |
| 4 | Fused / poorly formed glands |
| 5 | Solid sheets, necrosis, no glands |

Interpretation

| Score | Meaning | ISUP Grade |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| 6 (3+3) | Low-grade, indolent | 1 |
| 7 (3+4) | Intermediate – favorable | 2 |
| 7 (4+3) | Intermediate – unfavorable | 3 |
| 8 (4+4 / 3+5 / 5+3) | High-grade | 4 |
| 9–10 (4+5 / 5+4 / 5+5) | Very aggressive | 5 |

# PSA screening
  - only after informed consent/shared decision-making
  - routine screening not recommended without discussion

- average risk
  - no family history
  - PSA every 2 years
    - age 50–69 years
  - investigate if PSA >3 ng/mL
    - repeat PSA in 1–3 months
    - if PSA 3–5.5 ng/mL
      - add free:total PSA ratio

- moderate risk
  - father or brother diagnosed <60 years
  - PSA every 2 years
    - age 45–69 years

- high risk
  - ≥3 first-degree relatives with prostate cancer
  - PSA every 2 years
    - age 40–69 years

- do not screen
  - life expectancy <7 years
  - asymptomatic men with DRE alone
    - DRE not recommended as routine screening adjunct

- age ≥70 years
  - discuss harms > benefits before initiating/continuing PSA screening

- AMC pearls
  - average risk → start 50 years
  - moderate risk → start 45 years
  - high risk → start 40 years
  - interval = every 2 years
  - PSA >3 ng/mL → repeat in 1–3 months
  - PSA 3–5.5 ng/mL → free:total PSA ratio
  - routine DRE not recommended for asymptomatic screening
