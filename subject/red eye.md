## info

### epidemiology
- more common in adults

## diagnosis

### criteria
1. if watery
	1. viral conjuctivities
2. if unilateral
	1. less likely to be conjuctivities
	2. corneal ulcer, keratitis, foreign body, trauma, uveitis or acute glaucoma
3. if uncomfortable 
	1. [[keratitis]]
4. Herpes simplex keratitis (dendritic ulcer)
	- painless
- gradual onset 
	- conjuctivities
	- uvetis
- small foreign body
	- rapid hyperemia
-  wearing of contact lenses is important as these are prone to cause infection or the ‘overwear syndrome’, which resembles an acute ultraviolet (UV) burn

## approach
### history
1. history of trauma (including wood/metalwork—foreign body)
2. vision
3. the degree and type of discomfort
4. presence of discharge
5. presence of photophobia

### Unilateral red eye
1. trauma
2. foreign body, including intra-ocular (IOFB)
3. corneal ulcer
4. iritis (uveitis)
5. viral conjunctivitis (commonest type)
6. acute glaucoma

### loss of vision in red eye
- iritis (uveitis)
- scleritis
- acute glaucoma (pain; nausea and vomiting)
- chemical burns

### investigations
- swab of discharge for micro and culture or for viral studies
- ESR/CRP
- imaging

#### RED EYE – EXAMINATION (MCQ NOTES)

- Perform thorough inspection
- Note nature of injection:
  - Localised → episcleritis
  - Diffuse → more serious causes
- Inspect iris for irregularity
- Observe cornea
- Search for foreign bodies
  - Especially under eyelids
- Look for penetrating injury
- Upper eyelid eversion is mandatory
- Examine both eyes
- Use fluorescein to detect corneal ulceration
- Instil local anaesthetic drops before examining painful lesions
- Local anaesthetic test:
  - Pain relieved → surface problem
  - Pain not relieved → deeper problem suspected
- Palpate pre-auricular lymph nodes
  - Enlargement → viral conjunctivitis

#### INJECTION PATTERNS

- Conjunctivitis
  - Vessels clearly delineated
  - Branch from corners towards cornea
  - Mainly involves tarsal plate
- Episcleral / scleral vessels
  - Larger than conjunctival vessels
  - Concentrated towards cornea
- Ciliary injection (ciliary flush)
  - Red ring around limbus
  - Individual vessels not clearly visible
  - Indicates deep inflammatory disease

#### TARSAL CONJUNCTIVA SIGNS

- Fine follicles → viral infection
- Cobblestone appearance → allergic conjunctivitis

#### NOTE

- Slit lamp examination is ideal

## practise tips
- Avoid long-term use of any medication, especially antibiotics (e.g. chloramphenicol: course for a maximum of 10 days)
- note: Beware of allergy or toxicity to topical medications, especially antibiotics, as a cause of persistent symptoms
- As a general rule, avoid using topical corticosteroids or combined corticosteroid/antibiotic preparations
- Never use corticosteroids in the presence of a dendritic ulcer
- To achieve effective results from eye ointment or drops, remove debris such as mucopurulent exudate with bacterial conjunctivitis or blepharitis by using a warm solution of saline (dissolve a teaspoon of kitchen salt in 500 mL of boiled water) to bathe away any discharge from conjunctiva, eyelashes and lids
- A gritty sensation is common in conjunctivitis but the presence of a foreign body must be excluded
- Beware of the contact lens ‘overwear syndrome’, which is treated in a similar way to flash burns.
