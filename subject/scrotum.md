## other causes
- - Pain may be referred to the testes from other sites, including
    - intraabdominal: appendicitis/renal colic/UTI
    - referred hip pain
- Skin infections of the scrotum
    - cellulitis/shingles
- Testicular pain and swelling can occur in [[HSP]] secondary to vasculitis in the scrotum

# managament
## first preference 
- surgical review > Investigations

## do not
- Blood tests, ultrasound and doppler ultrasound are not useful in the acute setting (excluding traumatic injuries)

##  steps
- if torsion and irreducible hernia excluded
- if diagnosis remain unclear
	- - Colour doppler flow ultrasound may assess blood flow, anatomy and may localise swelling and fluid collections


## if suspected epididymo-orchitis
- - Urine MCS (ideally first pass urine collection)
    - Bacterial infection more likely in child with structural urinary tract abnormalities, recent instrumentation of urinary tract, or STI
    - A normal urine does not exclude epididymo-orchitis
- Urine _Chlamydia_ and _Gonorrhoea_ PCR testing (if STI clinically suspected)
- Viral causes include enterovirus, adenovirus and rarely mumps (mumps orchitis occurs 4-6 days after parotitis) 
- If mumps is suspected:
	- RT-PCR and/or IgM

- Testicular Torsion
	- Definition
		- twisting of spermatic cord → testicular ischaemia
	- Epidemiology
		- neonates and adolescents
		- peak age
			- 12–18 years
	- site
		- usually bilateral abnormality
	- Clinical features
		- sudden severe unilateral scrotal pain
		- scrotal swelling
		- abdominal/groin pain may occur
		- high-riding testis
		- horizontal/transverse lie
		- absent cremasteric reflex
			- normal reflex does NOT exclude torsion
		- negative Prehn sign
			- pain worsens or no relief on elevation
		- nausea and vomiting
	- prognosis
		- Time-dependent salvage
			- 24 hours
			- surgery still indicated even after prolonged symptoms
	- Investigations
		- Doppler ultrasound
			- useful in equivocal cases
			- imaging must NOT delay surgery
		- Technetium-99m scan
			- torsion
				- avascular testis
			- epididymo-orchitis
				- hyperaemia
	- Management
		- immediate surgical exploration
		- detorsion
		- if viable
			- orchidopexy
		- if non-viable/gangrenous
			- orchidectomy
		- always fix contralateral testis
			- bilateral orchidopexy
		- Manual detorsion
			- only if surgery delayed/not immediately available
			- “open book” manoeuvre
				- rotate outward/laterally
			- surgery still required afterward


- Torsion of Testicular Appendage
	- Clinical features
		- similar to testicular torsion but less severe
		- gradual onset pain
		- localised tenderness at upper pole
		- nausea/vomiting
		- blue dot sign
			- dark blue nodule at upper pole of testis
			- may be masked by hydrocele
	- Diagnosis
		- often clinical
		- may require surgical exploration to exclude true torsion
	- Management
		- supportive treatment
			- NSAIDs
			- rest
			- ice
			- scrotal support
		- symptoms usually resolve in 2–10 days

# Epididymitis
- Definition
	- inflammation of epididymis usually due to infection
	- most common cause of scrotal pain in adults

- Common Pathogens

	- Age <35 years
		- Chlamydia trachomatis
		- Neisseria gonorrhoeae

	- Age >35 years
		- E. coli
		- other coliform bacteria

	- Children
		- enterovirus
		- adenovirus
		- mumps
		- E. coli

	- Chronic infection
		- Mycobacterium tuberculosis

- Risk Factors
	- sexual activity
	- heavy physical exertion
	- prolonged sitting
	- cycling
	- motorbike riding

- Clinical Features

	- Symptoms
		- gradual onset scrotal pain
		- fever
		- rigors
		- dysuria
		- frequency
		- urgency

	- Signs
		- tender epididymis
		- indurated upper pole of testis
		- scrotal swelling
		- hydrocele
		- erythema

	- Important Signs

		- Prehn sign
			- pain relieved with scrotal elevation

		- Cremasteric reflex
			- present

- Investigations

	- urine analysis
	- urethral swab for STI

	- ultrasound
		- if torsion unclear

	- recurrent cases
		- evaluate structural causes
			- renal ultrasound
			- uroflowmetry
			- cystoscopy
			- micturition cystourethrogram

- Treatment

	- Conservative Management
		- analgesia
		- NSAIDs
		- ice
		- scrotal support

	- empirical antibiotics

	- severe infection
		- hospital admission

- Torsion of Appendix Testis

- Epidemiology
	- most common cause of scrotal pain in children

- Clinical Features

	- Symptoms
		- gradual onset pain
		- moderate–severe pain
		- nausea
		- vomiting

	- Signs
		- localized tenderness at anterior testis
		- hydrocele

		- classic sign
			- blue dot sign
				- visible infarcted appendage

- Investigations

	- ultrasound
		- normal testicular blood flow
		- hypoechoic appendage

- Treatment

	- Conservative
		- NSAIDs
		- rest
		- ice
		- scrotal elevation
		- supportive underwear

	- pain may last weeks

	- surgery only if
		- severe pain
		- persistent symptoms
		- diagnostic uncertainty
