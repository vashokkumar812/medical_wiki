

# Psychiatric history

* Psychiatric history follows the same basic format as a standard medical history.  
  * History of presenting illness  
  * Past psychiatric history  
  * Past medical history  
  * Family history  
  * Social history  
* Psychiatric history uses a biopsychosocial approach to understand the patient's illness.  
  * Biological factors  
  * Psychological functioning  
  * Interpersonal relationships  
  * Social background  
  * Life circumstances  
* Particular attention is given to developmental, personal and social history.  
* Psychiatric and physical illnesses often coexist and influence each other.  
  * Always consider physical causes of psychiatric symptoms.  
  * Assess both physical and psychological concerns.  
* Every medical interview should aim to be both diagnostic and therapeutic.  
  * Diagnostic aim  
    * Identify symptoms and develop a diagnostic hypothesis.  
    * Understand the biological, psychological, interpersonal and social factors contributing to the illness.  
    * Use this understanding to develop an appropriate management plan.  
  * Therapeutic aim  
    * Allow the patient to discuss concerns and distress.  
    * Help the patient gain relief by expressing their problems.  
    * Maintain an unhurried, patient and understanding approach.  
* Encourage the patient to describe the illness in their own words.  
  * Begin with open-ended, non-directive questions.  
  * Allow the patient to provide a narrative rather than a brief factual response.  
  * Identify the patient's most important concerns.  
  * Use targeted questions later to clarify specific symptoms.  
* Open-ended questions encourage detailed descriptions.  
  * Example: "Tell me about how you have been feeling."  
* Closed questions usually elicit yes/no or brief factual responses.  
  * Example: "Have you been depressed?"  
* Use a combination of open-ended and closed questions.  
  * Start with open-ended questions.  
  * Use focused questions to assess specific symptoms.  
  * Combine both approaches to obtain a comprehensive history efficiently.  
* Develop and test diagnostic hypotheses during the interview.  
  * Form initial hypotheses while listening to the patient's story.  
  * Ask focused follow-up questions to test each hypothesis.  
  * Reject or pursue diagnostic possibilities according to the patient's responses.  
* Symptoms suggesting possible depression include tiredness, lethargy, poor concentration and loss of appetite.  
  * Ask about mood: "What has your mood been like?"  
  * Ask about general feelings: "How have you been feeling in yourself?"  
  * Ask about sleep: "How have you been sleeping?"  
* Maintain a patient, unhurried, understanding and sympathetic attitude.  
  * Avoid judging the patient.  
  * Avoid appearing shocked by the patient's responses.  
  * Approach stigmatised, sexual and intimate topics sensitively.  
  * Recognise that patients may find these topics difficult to discuss.  
  * Develop confidence through experience and practice.  
* Begin the interview with non-threatening introductory questions.  
  * Introduce yourself.  
  * Obtain basic demographic information.  
    * Age  
    * Marital status  
    * Occupation  
    * Who the patient lives with  
  * Consider discussing a neutral topic initially to help the patient feel at ease.  
* Explore the presenting problem using the patient's own description.  
  * The presenting complaint may be explicitly psychiatric.  
    * Example: "I've been feeling very depressed."  
  * The presenting complaint may be a general symptom.  
    * Example: "I have no energy and feel tired all the time."  
* Do not assume that an apparently psychiatric presentation has a purely psychiatric cause.  
  * Depression-like symptoms may be associated with hypothyroidism.  
  * Tiredness and low energy may be caused by anaemia or depressive illness.  
  * A recent serious medical illness may contribute to depressive symptoms.  
* Consider physical and psychological causes throughout the interview.  
  * Psychiatric and medical problems are closely intertwined.  
  * A comprehensive medical interview should assess both.  
* History of presenting illness (HOPI)  
  * Nature of presenting problem  
    * What is the main problem?  
    * When did it start?  
    * How has it progressed?  
    * What does the patient think is wrong?  
    * Does the patient recognise the problem?  
    * Insight into illness  
    * Corroborative history from family/partner if required  
  * Psychiatric symptom review  
    * Mood changes  
    * Depression  
      * Low mood, depression or hopelessness in the past month?  
      * Little interest or pleasure in doing things?  
      * If positive, explore depressive symptoms in detail  
    * Anxiety and excessive worry  
    * Sleep pattern changes  
    * Appetite changes  
    * Hallucinations  
    * Delusions  
  * Substance use  
    * Prescribed and over-the-counter medications  
    * Illicit drug use  
    * Alcohol intake  
    * Caffeine intake  
  * Precipitating factors  
    * Recent physical illness  
    * Recent medication initiation or changes  
    * Treatment non-adherence  
    * Recent stressful life events  
    * Events affecting family members or social network  
    * Other possible triggers  
  * Risk assessment  
    * Risk to self  
      * Suicidal thoughts  
      * Self-harm thoughts or behaviour  
      * Immediate safety concerns  
    * Risk to others  
      * Thoughts of harming others  
      * Previous threats or violence  
      * Current risk of harm  
    * Protective factors and available support  
    * Need for urgent psychiatric assessment  
    * Consider involuntary assessment/treatment under applicable mental health legislation when indicated  
    * Seek senior psychiatric or mental health team advice if significant risk is identified  
* Past history and treatment history  
  * Past psychiatric history  
    * Have you ever experienced a similar episode or psychiatric problem before?  
    * When did the previous episode occur, and how long did it last?  
    * What symptoms did you experience?  
    * Was a psychiatric diagnosis made?  
    * Were there any stressful events before the previous episode?  
    * What factors contributed to the previous episode or triggered a relapse?  
    * Have you experienced any relapses or recurrent episodes?  
    * Were you ever admitted to a psychiatric unit?  
      * When and where were you admitted?  
      * How many admissions have you had?  
      * How long did you stay in hospital?  
    * Previous non-drug treatment  
      * Have you received counselling?  
      * Have you undergone psychotherapy?  
      * Have you received electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)?  
      * Did you find the treatment effective?  
    * Previous drug treatment  
      * What psychiatric medications have you taken?  
      * Which class of medication was prescribed?  
      * What was the indication for each medication?  
      * Was the medication effective?  
      * Did you experience any side effects?  
      * Did you stop or change any medication? If yes, why?  
      * Specifically enquire about long-term adverse effects of previous antipsychotic treatment.  
  * Past medical history  
    * Do you have any previous or current medical illnesses?  
    * Have you undergone any surgery or been hospitalised for medical conditions?  
    * What medications are you currently taking?  
    * Have you recently started, stopped or changed any medication?  
    * Have you had any physical illness associated with previous psychiatric episodes?  
    * Were there any medical problems or treatment-related factors that contributed to previous episodes or relapses?  
  * Family history  
    * Has anyone in your family had a psychiatric or mental illness?  
    * Has anyone in your family experienced anxiety, depression, agoraphobia or an eating disorder?  
    * Has anyone in your family had problems with alcohol or other drugs?  
    * Has anyone in your family received treatment for psychological or psychiatric problems?  
    * Has anyone in your family attempted or died by suicide?  
    * Family structure and family tree  
      * Who are the members of your immediate and extended family?  
      * What are their ages, general health and mental health?  
      * Is anyone in the family currently unwell?  
      * How would you describe each family member's personality?  
      * How do you get along with each family member?  
      * Have there been significant family events, such as bereavement or separation?  
    * Childhood and parental relationships  
      * How would you describe your relationship with your mother and father during childhood?  
      * How caring, supportive, controlling or protective were your parents?  
      * How would you describe the relationship between your parents?  
      * What was the general atmosphere like at home?  
      * Did you feel safe, supported and cared for during childhood?  
    * Childhood abuse and adverse experiences  
      * Introduce the topic sensitively and ensure privacy.  
      * Sometimes children have unpleasant or frightening experiences. Did anything like this happen to you?  
      * Did anyone ever harm you or physically hurt you?  
      * Did anyone hit you or make you feel unsafe?  
      * Did anyone ever touch you sexually or do anything sexual that made you uncomfortable?  
      * Would you feel comfortable telling me more about what happened?  
      * Explore the patient's experience and its impact without pressuring them to disclose details.  
  * Summary and transition  
    * Previous psychiatric diagnoses, episodes and relapses documented.  
    * Previous treatments, effectiveness and adverse effects documented.  
    * Relevant medical illnesses and medications documented.  
    * Family history of psychiatric illness, substance-related problems and suicide documented.  
    * Family relationships, parental care and significant family events explored.  
    * Relevant childhood adversity explored sensitively.  
    * Proceed to developmental history.  
* Social and personal history  
  * Opening question  
    * Could you tell me a little about your background, development and childhood?  
    * What are the important things you remember from your childhood?  
    * Allow the patient to narrate freely; ask further questions to clarify relevant details.  
  * Birth and early development  
    * Were there any complications during pregnancy, labour or delivery?  
    * Were you born prematurely or did you have any problems around birth?  
    * Were there any developmental delays or significant problems in early childhood?  
    * Did you have any serious infections or head injuries during childhood?  
  * Childhood and school history  
    * What was your childhood like?  
    * How did you cope with separation from your parents or caregivers?  
    * How did you adjust when starting primary school?  
    * How did you cope with the transition to secondary school?  
    * Did you have any difficulties making friends or maintaining peer relationships?  
    * How were your academic performance and learning abilities?  
    * Were there any significant problems during childhood or adolescence?  
  * Adolescence and teenage relationships  
    * How was your experience during adolescence?  
    * Did you have close friends or feel socially isolated?  
    * How were your relationships with peers and family?  
    * Were there any significant events or difficulties during your teenage years?  
  * Adult relationships and social support  
    * Are you currently in a relationship?  
    * How would you describe the quality of your intimate relationships?  
    * Who do you usually turn to when you have a problem?  
    * Is there anyone you can confide in or rely on for emotional support?  
    * How would you describe your relationship with family and friends?  
    * Have you experienced any significant bereavements, separations or other major life events?  
    * How did you cope with these events?  
    * Have you experienced any recent changes in your social support network?  
  * Living circumstances  
    * Where are you currently living?  
    * Who lives with you?  
    * Is your current accommodation stable and suitable?  
    * Do you feel safe where you live?  
    * Are there any financial, housing or family difficulties affecting you?  
    * Are you able to manage your daily activities independently?  
  * Occupational history  
    * Are you currently employed?  
    * What is your occupation, and how long have you worked in this role?  
    * How do you get along with colleagues and supervisors?  
    * How are you coping with your workload and workplace responsibilities?  
    * Have you experienced any work-related stress or conflict?  
    * Has your mental health affected your work performance or attendance?  
    * If unemployed, what are the circumstances?  
    * How do you feel about being unemployed, and how are you managing?  
  * Premorbid personality  
    * How would you describe yourself before the current illness began?  
    * How would your family or friends describe your personality?  
    * What are your usual personality traits?  
    * Do you tend to be anxious, nervous, highly strung, perfectionistic or obsessional?  
    * How do you usually respond to stress or difficult situations?  
    * How do you cope with setbacks, conflict or disappointment?  
    * What are your usual interests, hobbies and recreational activities?  
    * What are your personal strengths?  
    * What difficulties or weaknesses do you recognise in yourself?  
    * Has your personality or usual behaviour changed since the illness began?  
* Mental state examination (MSE)  
  * General observations throughout the interview  
    * Appearance and grooming  
    * Behaviour and psychomotor activity  
    * Speech pattern and characteristics  
    * Predominant concerns and expressed thoughts  
    * Attitude towards the examiner  
    * Interaction and engagement during the interview  
  * Formal mental state examination  
    * Appearance and behaviour  
      * Note dress, grooming, eye contact, cooperation, agitation or retardation.  
    * Speech  
      * Note rate, volume, tone, fluency and spontaneity.  
    * Mood and affect  
      * Ask: How has your mood been recently?  
      * Assess subjective mood and observed affect.  
    * Thought  
      * Assess the form and flow of thought.  
      * Explore the content of thought, including abnormal beliefs and preoccupations.  
    * Perception  
      * Have you heard or seen things that other people do not seem to hear or see?  
      * Explore hallucinations or other perceptual disturbances.  
    * Cognition  
      * Assess orientation, attention, registration and memory.  
      * Assess concentration and recall as clinically indicated.  
    * Insight and judgement  
      * Does the patient recognise that they may have an illness?  
      * What does the patient think is causing the symptoms?  
      * Does the patient recognise the need for treatment?  
  * Cognitive screening when indicated  
    * Consider formal cognitive assessment if delirium, dementia or other cognitive dysfunction is suspected.  
    * MMSE  
      * Assess orientation, memory and concentration using the standardised instrument.  
      * The MMSE is copyright-protected.  
    * Mini-Cog  
      * Ask the patient to remember three words.  
      * Repeat the words up to three times if needed to ensure they have been registered correctly.  
      * Ask the patient to draw a clock face with numbers and hands showing a specified time, such as 11:10.  
      * Ask the patient to recall the original three words.  
      * Score: 1 point for each word recalled and 2 points for a correct clock drawing; total score out of 5\.  
      * Score 0–2 supports possible dementia and warrants further assessment; it is not diagnostic on its own.  
  * Clinical interpretation  
    * Integrate MSE findings with the history, physical examination and risk assessment.  
    * If delirium or dementia is suspected, assess cognition in greater detail and investigate possible underlying causes.  
* Provisional diagnosis and formulation  
  * Initial assessment  
    * Summarise the patient's presenting problems and relevant psychiatric symptoms.  
    * Establish a provisional psychiatric diagnosis based on the history and mental state examination.  
    * Consider relevant differential diagnoses.  
    * Complete a general physical examination.  
    * Consider relevant medical or physical illnesses contributing to the psychiatric presentation.  
  * Biopsychosocial formulation  
    * Biological factors  
      * Predisposing factors: family history of mental illness, genetic vulnerability, previous medical illnesses.  
      * Precipitating factors: acute physical illness, biological changes or other relevant medical events.  
      * Perpetuating factors: ongoing medical illness, persistent biological changes or other physical factors maintaining symptoms.  
    * Psychological factors  
      * Predisposing factors: low self-esteem, longstanding personality traits or psychological vulnerability.  
      * Precipitating factors: stressful events, rejection, disappointment or threats to self-esteem.  
      * Perpetuating factors: ongoing low self-esteem, maladaptive coping strategies or unresolved psychological stress.  
    * Psychosocial factors  
      * Predisposing factors: poor social support, unsatisfactory relationships or longstanding social difficulties.  
      * Precipitating factors: relationship breakdown, bereavement, work-related stress or risk of unemployment.  
      * Perpetuating factors: ongoing relationship problems, work insecurity, social isolation or inadequate support.  
  * Formulation framework: 4 Ps  
    * Predisposing factors: What made the patient vulnerable to developing the illness?  
    * Precipitating factors: What triggered the current episode?  
    * Perpetuating factors: What is maintaining the illness or preventing recovery?  
    * Protective factors: What strengths, coping strategies, relationships or support systems may help recovery?  
  * Clinical formulation summary  
    * Presenting problem: Main symptoms, duration, severity and functional impact.  
    * Predisposing factors: Relevant biological, psychological and psychosocial vulnerabilities.  
    * Precipitating factors: Recent events or circumstances associated with symptom onset.  
    * Perpetuating factors: Ongoing medical, psychological or social factors maintaining the problem.  
    * Protective factors: Personal strengths, coping abilities, supportive relationships and available resources.  
    * Risk assessment: Risk to self, risk to others and relevant protective factors.  
    * Provisional diagnosis: Most likely psychiatric disorder and relevant differential diagnoses.  
  * Management planning based on formulation  
    * Identify interventions addressing biological factors, including medication or management of physical illness when indicated.  
    * Identify psychological interventions, such as counselling, psychotherapy or assertiveness training when appropriate.  
    * Identify psychosocial interventions, such as relationship counselling, social support or workplace-related assistance when appropriate.  
    * Address perpetuating factors that may be maintaining psychiatric or physical illness.  
    * Develop an integrated management plan addressing the relevant biological, psychological and psychosocial factors.
