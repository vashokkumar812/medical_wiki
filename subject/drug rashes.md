- Drug Eruptions (Exanthems & Drug Reactions)

	- Definition

		- Cutaneous adverse reaction to a medication

		- May range from mild self-limiting rash to life-threatening multisystem disease

	- Epidemiology

		- Common cause of rash

		- Approximately 1–10% of medication reports involve rash

		- Most commonly used drugs have adverse skin reaction rates >1%

	- Classification

		- Non-allergic (predictable)

		- Allergic (unpredictable, immune-mediated)

	- Non-Allergic Drug Reactions

		- Predictable

		- Usually dose related

		- Mechanisms

			- Idiosyncratic reaction

			- Drug property effect

				- Minocycline → blue-grey pigmentation

				- Corticosteroids → striae

			- Toxic effect

				- Cytotoxics → oral ulcers

			- Exacerbation of existing skin disease

				- Beta blockers → psoriasis flare

				- ACE inhibitors → psoriasis flare

			- Therapeutic mechanism

				- Dapsone

				- Rifampicin

	- Allergic Drug Reactions

		- Unpredictable

		- Immune-mediated

		- Due to

			- Drug

			- Drug metabolite

			- Drug-protein complex

		- Can occur with very small doses

		- May have familial predisposition

			- Morbilliform

			- Maculopapular

			- Pruritic

			- Symmetrical

		- Timeline

		- Distribution

				- Soles

			- Excoriation

			- Skin loss

		- Resolution

			- Usually resolves within 2–3 weeks

		- Rechallenge

			- Usually causes recurrence

			- Often occurs more rapidly

			- Generally avoided

		- Report significant reactions to TGA

		- Medical alert identification for severe reactions

		- Educate patient to avoid future exposure

	- AMC Pearls

		- Exanthematous (morbilliform) eruption accounts for ~95% of drug rashes.

		- Typical onset is about 1 week after starting the drug.

		- Trunk → peripheral spread is classic.

		- Amoxicillin in infectious mononucleosis commonly causes a widespread rash.

		- Sulfonamides cause increased rash frequency in HIV patients.

		- Re-exposure usually causes a faster recurrence.

		- Target lesions suggest erythema multiforme.

		- Wheals that appear and disappear suggest urticaria.

		- Fever + mucosal involvement + skin detachment = think SJS/TEN.

		- SJS/TEN requires immediate hospital referral.

		- First management step for any suspected drug eruption is cessation of the offending drug.

		- Fixed drug eruption is diagnosed clinically and may be confirmed by biopsy.

		- Always ask about OTC, herbal and complementary medicines.

# fixed drug eruption
- Definition
	- Cutaneous drug reaction characterized by lesions recurring at the same site after re-exposure to the offending drug
	- Mechanism not fully understood

- Common Sites
	- Face
	- Hands
	- Genitalia

- Clinical Features
	- Rapid onset
		- Usually within hours of drug intake

	- Lesion Characteristics
		- Bright red lesions
			- May vary in appearance
		- Well-demarcated
		- Fixed location
			- Recurs at same site with repeated exposure
		- May become:
			- Dusky
			- Hyperpigmented after healing
		- Sometimes blistering may occur

- Common Offending Drugs
	- NSAIDs
	- Paracetamol
	- Sulfonamides
	- Phenolphthalein
	- Tetracyclines
	- Penicillins
	- Salicylates
	- Combined oral contraceptive pill
	- Barbiturates
	- Quinine

- Management

	- Main Principle
		- Identify and withdraw offending drug

	- Symptomatic Treatment
		- Topical corticosteroids
		- Emollients if needed

	- Antihistamines
		- Indicated only for:
			- Urticarial drug eruptions

		- Avoid in:
			- Purpuric reactions
			- Erythematous reactions
			- Vesiculobullous reactions

		- Reason
			- May delay healing

- Important Points
	- Re-exposure causes recurrence at identical site
	- Residual hyperpigmentation common after resolution
	- Careful drug history is essential

	- AMC Pearls

		- Morbilliform eruption is the commonest drug rash.

		- DRESS = rash + eosinophilia + systemic organ involvement.

		- Fever and lymphadenopathy strongly suggest DRESS.

		- Mucosal involvement and skin detachment suggest SJS/TEN.

		- Allopurinol is a classic cause of DRESS and SCAR.

		- HLA-B*58:01 is strongly associated with allopurinol-induced SCAR.

		- Stop the culprit drug immediately.

		- Do not rechallenge after significant drug eruption.

		- Normal eosinophils, normal organ function and absence of lymphadenopathy favour uncomplicated morbilliform drug eruption.
