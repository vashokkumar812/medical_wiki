## Clinical Differentiation: Type 1 vs Type 2 Diabetes

| Feature            | Type 1 Diabetes                   | Type 2 Diabetes                     |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Relative frequency | ~10%                              | 90%                                 |
| Peak age incidence | 10–30 years                       | >40 years                           |
| Onset              | Rapid                             | Insidious / slow                    |
| Presentation       | Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss | Milder symptoms, often asymptomatic |
| Weight at onset    | Low (thin)                        | High (obese)                        |
| Ketoacidosis       | Yes                               | Rare                                |
| Familial tendency  | Weak                              | Strong                              |
| Insulin status     | Deficient                         | Resistant                           |




| Test                             | Normal           | Intermediate hyperglycaemia | Diabetes            |     |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------- | --- |
| Venous blood glucose (fasting)   | up to 6.0 mmol/L | 6.1–6.9 mmol/L              | ≥7.0 mmol/L         |     |
| Venous blood glucose (random)    | up to 6.0 mmol/L | —                           | ≥11.1 mmol/L        |     |
| Oral glucose tolerance test (2h) | up to 7.7 mmol/L | 7.8–11.0 mmol/L             | ≥11.1 mmol/L        |     |
| HbA1c                            | —                | —                           | ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%) |     |



## Suggested Glycaemic Targets (mmol/L)
| Parameter | Ideal | Suboptimal / Unacceptable |
|-----------|-------|---------------------------|
| Before meals (fasting) | <5.5 | >7.7 |
| After meals (2 hours postprandial) | <7 | >11 |
| HbA1c (%) | ≤7 | >8 |


- diabetes mellitus
	- Definition
		- Chronic metabolic disorder characterised by hyperglycaemia due to:
			- impaired insulin secretion
			- impaired insulin action
			- or both

- classification
	- Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM)
		- autoimmune β-cell destruction
		- absolute insulin deficiency
	- Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)
		- insulin resistance with relative insulin deficiency
	- LADA
		- latent autoimmune diabetes in adults
		- slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes
	- Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)

- clinical features
	- classic symptoms of uncontrolled diabetes
		- Polyuria
		- Polydipsia
		- Weight loss
			- especially in T1DM
		- Polyphagia
		- Tiredness
		- Fatigue
		- Malaise
		- Nocturia
		- Propensity for infections
			- skin infections
			- urinary infections
			- genital infections
				- vaginal thrush
		- Genital itching

	- type 1 diabetes typical presentation
		- Short history
			- usually 2–10 weeks
		- Classic triad
			- thirst
			- polyuria
			- weight loss
		- Often presents in children or young adults
		- May first present with DKA
		- Hyperglycaemia in a sick child is a medical emergency

	- other symptoms
		- Nocturnal enuresis
			- more common in T1DM
		- Blurred vision
		- Visual changes

	- symptoms of complications
		- Neuropathy
			- tingling
			- numbness
			- neuropathic pain
		- Autonomic dysfunction
			- impotence
		- Arterial disease
			- myocardial ischaemia
			- peripheral vascular disease
		- Recurrent infections
			- staphylococcal skin infections

- diagnosis of diabetes
	- symptomatic patients
		- Fasting venous blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L
		- OR random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L
		- OR HbA1c > 6.5% (>48 mmol/mol)

	- asymptomatic patients
		- Require at least two abnormal results
			- fasting glucose
			- postprandial glucose
			- OGTT values

	- OGTT
		- Gold standard in uncertain cases
		- Diagnostic if 2-hour glucose >11.1 mmol/L

	- gestational diabetes
		- 75 g OGTT at 24–28 weeks gestation

- prediabetes
	- Elevated glucose above normal range but below diabetes threshold
	- Venous plasma glucose
		- 6.1–6.9 mmol/L

	- states
		- Impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
		- Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)

	- management
		- lifestyle modification
			- weight reduction
			- increased physical activity
		- medication not routinely indicated

- risk factors for diabetes
	- Age >40 years
	- Family history
	- Overweight or obesity
	- Sedentary lifestyle
	- Gestational diabetes history
	- Pancreatitis
	- PCOS
	- Hypertension
	- Ischaemic heart disease
	- Medications causing hyperglycaemia
	- High-risk ethnic groups
		 - Indian subcontinent
		 - Chinese
		 - Pacific Islanders
		 - Afro-Caribbeans
		 - Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders

- screening for type 2 diabetes
	- Indications
		- Prediabetes
		- Age >40 years
		- Obesity
		- Family history
		- High-risk ethnic groups
		- Gestational diabetes history
		- Steroid or antipsychotic use
		- PCOS
		- Previous cardiovascular disease

	- frequency
		- Every 3 years from age 40 using AUSDRISK
		- Annual screening in very high-risk groups

- diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
	- Definition
		- Acute life-threatening complication due to insulin deficiency
		- Characterised by:
			- hyperglycaemia
			- ketosis
			- metabolic acidosis

	- euglycaemic DKA (euDKA)
		- DKA with normal or mildly elevated glucose
		- Commonly associated with SGLT2 inhibitors

	- diagnostic criteria
		- pH < 7.3
		- HCO3 <15 mmol/L
		- Ketonaemia or ketonuria
		- High anion gap metabolic acidosis

	- pathogenesis
		- Increased counter-regulatory hormones
			- glucagon
			- cortisol
			- catecholamines
			- growth hormone
		- Decreased insulin
			- hyperglycaemia
			- glycosuria
			- dehydration
			- electrolyte loss
			- lipolysis
			- ketogenesis
			- acidosis

	- precipitating factors
		- Infection
		- Myocardial infarction
		- Pneumonia
		- Gastrointestinal illness
		- Insulin omission
		- Surgery
		- Trauma
		- SGLT2 inhibitor use

	- clinical features
		- Polyuria
		- Polydipsia
		- Vomiting
		- Abdominal pain
		- Dehydration
		- Tachycardia
		- Kussmaul breathing
		- Altered consciousness
		- Coma

	- investigations
		- ABG/VBG
		- Blood ketones
		- Urine ketones
		- Electrolytes
		- Osmolality
		- Renal function
		- ECG
		- CXR
		- FBC

	- management
		- Fluid resuscitation
		- IV insulin infusion
			- 0.1 U/kg/hr
		- Electrolyte replacement
			- especially potassium
		- Dextrose infusion once glucose falls
		- Treat precipitating cause
		- Frequent monitoring of:
			- glucose
			- ketones
			- acid-base status
			- electrolytes

	- complications
		- Hypoglycaemia
		- Hyponatraemia
		- Cerebral oedema
		- Arrhythmias
		- Venous thrombosis
		- Hyperchloraemic acidosis
		- Infection

- SGLT2 inhibitors
	- Mechanism
		- increase urinary glucose excretion
	- Associated complication
		- euglycaemic DKA
	- Risk factors for euDKA
		- T1DM
		- LADA
		- fasting
		- illness
		- insulin dose reduction

- important learning points
	- Hyperglycaemia may be absent in euDKA
	- Normal glucose does not exclude DKA
	- Blood ketone testing is essential in symptomatic patients on SGLT2 inhibitors
	- Early recognition and treatment reduce morbidity and mortality



- diabetes in children
	- epidemiology
		- Obese children (4–10 years)
			- ~25% have impaired glucose tolerance
		- Obese adolescents (11–18 years)
			- ~21% have impaired glucose tolerance
		- Type 2 diabetes identified in ~4% of obese adolescents
		- >30% of newly diagnosed diabetes in children/adolescents present with DKA

	- type 1 diabetes mellitus
		- common presentation
			- Polyuria
			- Polydipsia
			- Weight loss
			- Lethargy

	- unusual presentations
		- Enuresis
		- Daytime wetting
		- May mimic urinary tract infection

	- diagnosis
		- Elevated fasting blood glucose
		- Elevated random blood glucose
		- OGTT generally inappropriate in very young children

	- management principles
		- Refer to multidisciplinary paediatric diabetes team
		- High blood glucose in an unwell child is a medical emergency until proven otherwise
		- Assess for DKA in symptomatic children

- gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)
	- definition
		- New onset abnormal glucose tolerance during pregnancy

	- pathophysiology
		- Pregnancy is diabetogenic in genetically predisposed women
		- Increased insulin resistance occurs during pregnancy

	- screening
		- Universal screening at 24–28 weeks gestation
		- 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

	- diagnostic criteria
		- Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L
		- 1-hour glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L
		- 2-hour glucose 8.5–11.0 mmol/L

	- complications
		- Maternal hyperglycaemia
		- Fetal macrosomia
		- Neonatal hypoglycaemia
		- Increased future risk of T2DM

- diabetes in the elderly
	- epidemiology
		- Incidence increases with age
		- Increased diabetes-related morbidity and mortality

	- clinical considerations
		- Greater risk from intensive treatment regimens
		- Increased susceptibility to:
			- hypoglycaemia
			- adverse drug effects
			- polypharmacy complications
		- Multiple comorbidities common

	- management considerations
		- Careful monitoring required
		- Individualised glycaemic targets
		- Attention to:
			- diet
			- foot care
			- postural hypotension
			- medication safety

- diabetic nephropathy
	- major cause of chronic kidney disease
	- prevention is important

	- early detection
		- microalbuminuria
			- potentially reversible with optimal control

	- screening
		- first morning urine albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR)
			- preferred method
		- Dipstick testing unreliable
		- 24-hour urine collection impractical in general practice

	- treatment
		- ACE inhibitors first-line
		- ARBs if ACE inhibitor intolerance
			- e.g. cough
		- strict blood pressure control

- diabetic retinopathy and maculopathy
	- microvascular retinal disease
	- prevalence increases with disease duration
	- Up to 20% of T2DM patients have retinopathy at diagnosis
	- Leading cause of blindness in working-age adults

	- assessment
		- every 1–2 years
		- dilated fundoscopy
		- retinal photography
		- fluorescein angiography if needed

	- management
		- early diagnosis important
		- laser photocoagulation may prevent visual loss

			- <4.0 mmol/L
		- LDL cholesterol

		- Non-HDL cholesterol
			- <2.5 mmol/L
		- Triglycerides
			- <2.0 mmol/L

		- General target
			- <140/90 mmHg
		- With significant proteinuria
			- ≤125/80 mmHg

		- BMI

	- urinary targets
		- Urinary albumin excretion

		- Alcohol

- HbA1c
	- glycated haemoglobin
	- Normally 4–6% of total haemoglobin
	- Elevated in persistent hyperglycaemia
	- Reflects mean plasma glucose over previous 2–3 months
	- Useful marker of long-term glycaemic control
	- Monitoring frequency
		- every 3–6 months

- blood glucose notes
	- Capillary glucose approximately 7% higher than venous glucose
	- Glucometer error typically ±5%

- initiating insulin therapy
	- Shared care with endocrinologist recommended
	- Use simplest effective regimen
	- Full insulin replacement may require:
		- 2 injections/day
		- 3 injections/day
		- 4 injections/day

	- advanced technologies
		- continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)
		- smart insulin pumps
		- automated insulin adjustment systems

- insulin injection regimens
	- pre-mixed twice-daily regimen
		- biphasic insulin
		- administered:
			- before breakfast
			- before evening meal
		- Typical starting dose
			- 0.3 IU/kg/day

	- three-injection regimen
		- short-acting insulin before breakfast and lunch
		- intermediate/long-acting insulin before evening meal

	- basal-bolus regimen
		- rapid/short-acting insulin before meals
			- bolus component
		- intermediate/long-acting insulin at bedtime
			- basal component

	- rapid-acting analogues
		- may be administered with meals

- insulin administration
	- timing
		- usually ~30 minutes before meals

	- route
		- subcutaneous injection

	- preferred injection sites
		- abdomen
		- thigh/leg

	- technique
		- rotate sites
			- ≥3 cm apart
		- reduces lipodystrophy risk
		- pinch skin before injection
		- alcohol swabs generally unnecessary

- patient education
	- Never omit insulin
	- Do not alter doses without knowledge or advice
	- Monitor injection sites for:
		- lipohypertrophy
		- lipoatrophy

- sick day management
	- Have prearranged sick-day plan
	- Continue insulin even during illness
	- Additional rapid-acting insulin may be required
	- Monitor glucose frequently
		- glucose >15 mmol/L concerning
	- Check ketones if unwell
	- Seek medical assistance early

- exercise and sport
	- Encourage regular physical activity
	- Insulin dose adjustments may be necessary
	- Additional carbohydrate intake may be required
	- Careful glucose monitoring important

- glycaemic targets in T1DM
	- HbA1c
		- ≤7%
		- ≤53 mmol/mol

	- fasting/preprandial glucose
		- 4–7 mmol/L

	- postprandial glucose
		- 5–10 mmol/L

- type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) management
	- first-line management
		- lifestyle intervention
			- diet modification
			- exercise
			- weight loss

	- lifestyle principles
		- Symptoms often improve within 1–4 weeks
		- Exercise should be discussed at every visit
		- Exercise target
			- 20–30 minutes/day
		- Encourage enjoyable and social physical activities
		- Long-term success depends on:
			- education
			- adherence
			- supervision

	- multidisciplinary support
		- diabetes educator
		- dietitian

- pharmacological therapy in T2DM
	- indication
		- inadequate glycaemic control after 3–6 months of lifestyle intervention

	- first-line agent
		- Metformin
			- reduces insulin resistance

	- second-line/add-on therapy
		- Sulfonylureas
			- increase insulin secretion

	- newer agents
		- SGLT2 inhibitors
			- cardioprotective
			- renoprotective

		- GLP-1 receptor agonists
			- injectable therapy
			- promote weight loss
			- improve glycaemic control

	- other oral agents
		- DPP-4 inhibitors
			- gliptins
		- Thiazolidinediones
			- glitazones

- goals of diabetes treatment
	- achieve glycaemic control
	- prevent acute complications
	- prevent chronic complications
	- reduce cardiovascular risk
	- improve quality of life


- non-insulin hypoglycaemic agents

- metformin
	- class
		- Biguanide

	- mechanism
		- reduces hepatic glucose production
		- improves insulin sensitivity

	- duration
		- ~12 hours
		- slow-release preparations available

	- dose
		- 0.5–3 g/day

	- advantages
		- first-line therapy in T2DM
		- no significant hypoglycaemia
		- no major weight gain
		- improves lipid profile

	- adverse effects
		- gastrointestinal upset
			- diarrhoea
			- abdominal discomfort
			- nausea
			- vomiting
		- rare lactic acidosis

	- contraindications
		- severe renal impairment
			- eGFR <30
		- severe hepatic disease
		- severe cardiac disease

- sulfonylureas
	- mechanism
		- stimulate pancreatic insulin secretion

	- adverse effects
		- hypoglycaemia
			- most common
		- weight gain
		- rash
		- gastrointestinal disturbance

	- gliclazide
		- duration
			- 18–24 hours
		- dose
			- 40–320 mg/day

	- glipizide
		- duration
			- 16–24 hours
		- dose
			- 2.5–40 mg/day
		- preferred in elderly
			- shorter acting

	- glibenclamide
		- duration
			- 18–24 hours
		- dose
			- 2.5–20 mg/day

	- glimepiride
		- duration
			- >24 hours
		- dose
			- 1–4 mg/day

- α-glucosidase inhibitors
	- acarbose
		- mechanism
			- delays carbohydrate absorption from intestine

		- duration
			- ~3 hours

		- dose
			- 50–600 mg/day

		- adverse effects
			- flatulence
			- diarrhoea
			- skin rash
			- hepatic effects

- thiazolidinediones (glitazones)
	- mechanism
		- improve insulin sensitivity

	- pioglitazone

		- adverse effects

			- weight gain

	- rosiglitazone

		- adverse effects

		- increase insulin secretion
		- reduce glucagon secretion

	- mechanism

	- mechanism

		- suppress glucagon
		- slow gastric emptying

		- Exenatide
			- 5 mcg bd
			- 2 mg weekly MR
		- Liraglutide

- stepwise management of type 2 diabetes

	- initial management
		- lifestyle modification
			- diet
			- exercise
			- weight reduction

	- first-line pharmacotherapy
		- Metformin unless contraindicated
		- Starting dose
			- 500 mg once or twice daily

	- if monotherapy inadequate
		- combine with second agent

- secondary failure / step-up therapy
	- Add-on options
		- Sulfonylurea
		- DPP-4 inhibitor
		- SGLT2 inhibitor
		- GLP-1 receptor agonist
		- insulin
		- less commonly:
			- acarbose
			- glitazones

	- Approximately 30% eventually require insulin therapy

- starting insulin in T2DM
	- indications
		- HbA1c >7%
		- inadequate glycaemic control despite oral therapy
		- symptomatic hyperglycaemia

	- principles
		- Do not delay insulin initiation
		- Start low and titrate gradually
		- Optimise lifestyle and oral therapy first

	- patient counselling
		- injections less painful than expected
		- insulin often improves wellbeing and energy

	- shared care
		- referral to diabetes educator/endocrinologist may help

- suggested stepwise insulin approach
	- step 1
		- Continue oral agents
			- metformin
			- sulfonylurea
			- ± glitazone
			- ± acarbose
			- ± DPP-4 inhibitor
		- Add bedtime isophane insulin
			- 10 units

	- step 2
		- Titrate insulin to fasting glucose target
			- ~6 mmol/L
		- Increase insulin
			- by 4–5 units every 3–4 days

	- step 3
		- If larger or multiple insulin doses required
			- continue metformin
			- usually cease:
				- sulfonylureas
				- DPP-4 inhibitors
				- thiazolidinediones

		- adjunctive agents that may reduce insulin requirement
			- GLP-1 receptor agonists
			- SGLT2 inhibitors
			- glitazones

- goals of pharmacologic therapy
	- achieve glycaemic control
	- minimise hypoglycaemia
	- reduce cardiovascular risk
	- preserve renal function
	- improve quality of life

## practise tips
- Many cases of type 2 diabetes remain undiagnosed, so vigilance is important
- Follow-up programs should keep to a prepared format.
- xample formats are presented in TABLES 11.8 and 11.9 
- Hyperglycaemia is a common cause of tiredness. If elderly people with type 2 diabetes are very tired, think of hyperglycaemia and consider giving insulin to improve their symptoms
- The management of the person with diabetes is a team effort involving family members, a nurse education centre, podiatrists, domiciliary nursing service, GP and consultant. 
- If a person with diabetes (particularly type 1) is very drowsy and looks sick, consider first the diagnosis of ketoacidosis
- Foot care is vital: always examine the feet when the person comes in for review.
- Treat associated hypertension with ACE inhibitors or a calcium-channel blocker (also good in combination)
- Use a team approach and encourage joining special support groups (e.g. Diabetes Australia)
- ‘Never let the sun go down on pus in a diabetic foot’—admit to hospital
- If a foot ulcer hasn’t healed in 6 weeks, exclude osteomyelitis
- Arrange for an MRI and investigate the vasculature
- Prevention/detection of coronary heart disease should be an integral part of all consultations.

- Hyperglycaemic emergencies
	- Key clinical triggers

	- In people without known diabetes
		- Undiagnosed diabetes can cause hyperglycaemic emergency at any age
		- Important symptoms/signs
			- Polyuria
			- Polydipsia
			- Unexplained weight loss
		- Especially important in
			- Children
			- Young adults
		- Required action
			- Urgent capillary blood glucose testing

	- In people with known diabetes
		- Symptoms/signs in setting of
			- Acute illness
			- Persistent hyperglycaemia
		- Persistent hyperglycaemia
			- Capillary blood glucose >15 mmol/L
			- Duration >8–12 hours
			- Monitor every 2 hours
		- Consider potential hyperglycaemic emergency unless clearly otherwise

	- High-risk groups
		- Pregnancy
		- [[SGLT2 inhibitors]]

	- Important note
		- [[Diabetic ketoacidosis]] may occur without marked hyperglycaemia
			- Euglycaemic DKA
		- Especially in
			- Pregnancy
			- [[SGLT2 inhibitors]]

- Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
	- Acute life-threatening emergency
	- Characterised by
			- Hyperglycaemia
			- Metabolic acidosis
	- Most common in
			- [[Type 1 diabetes]]
	- May
			- Present as first manifestation of diabetes
			- Occur in [[Type 2 diabetes]]

	- Clinical course
		- Rapid deterioration possible
		- May develop altered consciousness
		- Suspected DKA = medical emergency
		- Do not delay hospital transfer

- Euglycaemic ketoacidosis
	- Defined as
			- Ketosis
			- Acidosis
			- Normal/mildly elevated glucose
	- Higher risk groups
			- [[Type 1 diabetes]]
			- [[Type 2 diabetes]]
			- Pregnancy
			- Low-carbohydrate diet
			- [[SGLT2 inhibitors]]
			- Recent surgery

- Symptoms/signs suggestive of DKA
	- Symptoms
		- Polyuria
		- Polydipsia/thirst
		- Nausea
		- Vomiting
		- Abdominal pain
		- Weight loss

	- Signs
		- Altered conscious state
		- Kussmaul breathing
			- Rapid respiratory rate
		- Ketotic/acetone breath
		- Dehydration
			- Poor skin turgor
			- Tachycardia
			- Hypotension
			- Dry mouth/tongue
			- Oliguria/anuria

	- May also have symptoms from precipitating illness
			- Pain
			- Fever
			- Sepsis

- Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)
	- Severe persistent hyperglycaemia
	- Minimal/absent ketosis
	- Profound dehydration

	- Epidemiology
		- More common in [[Type 2 diabetes]]
		- Usually older adults

	- Common triggers
		- Sepsis
			- UTI
			- Pneumonia
		- Cardiovascular events
			- Myocardial ischaemia
			- Stroke
		- Renal dysfunction

	- Clinical features
		- Neurological impairment common
		- Coma may occur
		- Higher mortality than DKA
		- Mortality often related to precipitating cause
		- May coexist with DKA

- Symptoms/signs suggestive of HHS
	- Symptoms
		- Pain/fever from underlying cause
		- Dry mouth
		- Thirst
		- Reduced urination

	- Signs
		- Altered conscious state
		- Severe dehydration
			- Reduced skin turgor
			- Tachycardia
			- Hypotension
			- Oliguria/anuria

- Risk factors for hyperglycaemic emergencies
	- Children/young people with [[Type 1 diabetes]]
	- Unstable glycaemic control
	- Omission of diabetes medications
			- Especially insulin
	- Insulin pump use
			- Pumps use rapid-acting insulin only
			- Interruption rapidly causes DKA
	- Previous DKA
	- Acute infection/sepsis
	- Pancreatitis
	- Myocardial infarction
	- Unstable angina
	- Trauma
	- Surgery
	- Burns
	- Medications
			- Corticosteroids
			- Atypical antipsychotics
			- Immunosuppressants
			- [[SGLT2 inhibitors]]
	- Alcohol
	- Recreational drugs
	- Elderly patients
			- Subtle DKA presentation
			- Higher mortality
	- Pregnancy

- Clinical assessment
	- Initial assessment/documentation
		- Temperature
		- Blood pressure
		- Heart rate
		- Respiratory rate
		- GCS
		- Urgent point-of-care testing

- Critical biochemical assessment
	- Measure both
			- Blood glucose
			- Blood ketones
	- Primary care should have
			- Capillary glucose meter + strips
			- Capillary ketone meter + strips

- Preferred testing
	- Capillary blood glucose
			- Critical >15 mmol/L
	- Capillary blood ketones
			- β-hydroxybutyrate >1.5 mmol/L

- Less preferred testing
	- Urine ketones
			- Acetoacetate
			- Critical ≥1+
	- Use only if blood ketone testing unavailable

	- Limitations of urine ketones
		- Misleading with [[SGLT2 inhibitors]]
		- Severe dehydration may prevent urine sample

- Urgent action criteria
	- Blood glucose >15 mmol/L
	- Plus symptoms/signs of DKA/HHS
	- Requires urgent consultation and/or hospital transfer

- Avoid delays
	- Hyperglycaemic crises may be rapidly fatal
	- Prioritise emergency management
	- Do not delay for
			- HbA1c
			- C-peptide
			- Islet cell antibodies
			- OGTT

- Management
	- Refer to DKA/HHS management pathways
	- Sick day management plans
			- Many patients with [[Type 1 diabetes]] have written plan
	- If immediate transfer not required
			- Implement sick day plan
			- Arrange urgent specialist/team contact
