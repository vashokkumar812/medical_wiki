- Definition
	- Protrusion of a viscus or part of a viscus through an abnormal opening in the wall containing it

- Basic Components of a Hernia
	- Neck
		- Peritoneum
	- Sac
	- Coverings
	- Contents

- Predisposing Factors
	- Older age
	- Obesity
	- Pregnancy

- Precipitating Factors
	- Increase intra-abdominal pressure

	- Causes
		- Difficulty in micturition
		- Constipation with straining
		- Chronic cough
			- eg bronchitis
		- Heavy lifting
		- Repeated straining
		- Nerve damage to abdominal wall
			- eg post-appendicectomy
- - Clinical Features

	- Symptoms
		- Lump
		- Pain or discomfort
			- Dragging pain
			- Worse on standing
			- Worse on walking
		- Testicular pain
			- Referred pain
			- Spermatic cord compression
		- Indirect hernia may refer pain to testicle

	- Signs
		- Expansile cough impulse
		- Reducibility

	- Hernial Obstruction – Features
		- Colicky abdominal pain
		- Nausea and vomiting
		- Constipation
		- Failure to pass flatus
		- Abdominal distension
		- High-pitched tinkling bowel sounds
		- Local tenderness
		- Swelling
		- No expansile cough impulse

- Types of Hernias

	- Common Groin Hernias
		- Inguinal hernia
			- indirect inguinal hernia
				-  May traverse entire inguinal canal ==> Can enter scrotum→ inguinoscrotal hernia
			- direct inguinal hernia
				- Features
					- Oblique course
					- Often irreducible
		- Femoral Hernia
			- Herniates
				- through femoral ring into femoral canal
			- Neck position
				- lies lateral to pubic tubercle
			- Course
				- Bulges forward
				- Then upward
			- Features
				- Usually small
				- Often unnoticed
				- Highest risk of obstruction and strangulation
	- Rare Hernias
		- Obturator hernia
		- Spigelian hernia
			- Low abdominal wall hernia
		- Preperitoneal inguinal hernia
		- Prevascular femoral hernia
		- Paraumbilical Hernia
			- Mouth is wide
			- Usually does not strangulate
			- Symptomatic cases
				- Surgery indicated

		- Sliding Esophageal Hiatus Hernia
			- Strangulation can occur
				- Uncommon







- Acquired Hernia – General Principles
	- Always examine both sides
	- Examine scrotum in men
	- Frequently bilateral
	- Usually due to muscular weakness
	- Commonest acquired groin hernia
		- Direct inguinal hernia



- Complications
	- Intestinal obstruction
	- Incarceration
	- Strangulation
		-  Reasons for Ischemic Strangulation
			- Narrow diameter of neck
			- Rigidity of hernia neck
			- Tortuosity of hernial track
	- Sliding hernia



- Important Notes
	- Femoral hernia easily missed in obesity
	- Large femoral hernias often irreducible
	- Reduction should be attempted in recumbent position
	- Direct hernias usually reduce easily

- Differential Diagnosis of Groin Mass

	- Hernias
		- Femoral
		- Inguinal

	- Other Conditions
		- Malgaigne bulge
		- Lipoma
		- Undescended testis
		- Spermatic cord swellings
			- Encysted hydrocele
			- Lipoma
		- Lymphadenopathy
			- Localized
			- Generalized
		- Hematoma
			- Post femoral artery puncture
		- Neoplasm
		- Psoas abscess
		- Spermatocele
		- Vascular anomalies
			- Saphenous varix
			- Femoral aneurysm

- Treatment
	- All symptomatic hernias
		- Surgical repair
	- Asymptomatic but fit patients
		- Surgery advised
	- Obstructed or strangulated hernia
		- Urgent surgery
	- surgery options
		- Laparoscopic Repair
			- Less postoperative pain
			- Increasingly preferred

	- Conservative Management
		- Indications
			- Asymptomatic patients
			- High surgical risk patients
				- eg frail elderly
		- Watch-and-wait approach
		- Trusses
			- Provide support
			- Difficult to use
			- Not definitive treatment

	- Complications of Surgery
		- Bruising
		- Numbness
		- Hematoma
		- Infection
				- 1–7%
		- Recurrence
		- Chronic pain
				- >3 months
				- May persist for years
				- Severe in ~3%
		- Rare complications
				- Infertility
				- Testicular atrophy

- Recovery
	- Light work
		- ~7 days
	- Strenuous work
		- ~14 days
