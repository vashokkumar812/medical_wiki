## Parkinson's Disease
- Common, progressive neurodegenerative disorder.
- Prevalence increases with advancing age.
- Affects multiple systems:
  - Motor system
  - Gastrointestinal system
  - Autonomic nervous system
  - Sleep
  - Mood and cognition
- Non-motor symptoms may precede motor symptoms by several years (prodromal Parkinson's disease).

## Clinical Features

- **Motor symptoms**
  - **Bradykinesia (essential for diagnosis)**
    - Slowness of movement.
    - Progressive reduction in amplitude with repetitive movements (motor decrement).
    - Momentary arrest of movement during repetitive finger and toe tapping.
    - Mask-like facies.
    - Reduced blink rate.
    - Hypophonia (soft voice).
    - Micrographia (small handwriting).
  - **Resting tremor**
    - Typically 4–6 Hz.
    - Usually asymmetrical.
    - Commonly affects distal upper limbs.
    - May initially involve lower limbs.
    - More prominent at rest.
  - **Rigidity**
    - Increased muscle tone.
    - May be associated with cogwheel rigidity.
  - **Gait abnormalities**
    - Reduced arm swing (often asymmetrical initially).
    - Shortened stride length.
    - Reduced step height.
    - Shuffling gait.
    - Difficulty turning.
    - En bloc turning (multiple steps required to turn).
    - Falls are generally a late feature.

- **Non-motor symptoms**
  - **Gastrointestinal**
    - Constipation.
    - Dysphagia.
  - **Olfactory**
    - Hyposmia or anosmia.
  - **Sleep disorders**
    - REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD).
    - Dream enactment behaviour.
    - Vocalisation during sleep.
    - Thrashing movements.
    - Excessive daytime somnolence.
    - Insomnia and fragmented sleep.
    - Restless legs syndrome.
  - **Neuropsychiatric**
    - Anxiety.
    - Depression.
    - Apathy.
    - Visual hallucinations.
    - Psychosis.
    - Cognitive impairment and dementia.
  - **Autonomic dysfunction**
    - Orthostatic hypotension.
    - Bladder dysfunction.
    - Urinary urgency.
    - Erectile dysfunction.
    - Hyperhidrosis.
  - **Others**
    - Fatigue.
    - Musculoskeletal pain.
    - Dystonic pain.
    - Neuropathic pain.
    - Sexual dysfunction.

## Red Flags Suggesting Parkinson's Disease Mimics

- Objective limb weakness (subjective weakness may occur, but formal power testing is usually normal).
- Rapidly progressive neurological deterioration over weeks to months.
- Early, recurrent falls.
- Absence of typical non-motor features:
  - Constipation.
  - Anosmia.
  - REM sleep behaviour disorder.
  - Daytime urinary urgency.
- Current use of dopamine-blocking medications:
  - Prochlorperazine.
  - Metoclopramide.
  - Antipsychotics.
- Severe orthostatic hypotension (>30 mmHg).
- Early cognitive impairment.
- Urinary incontinence.
- Poor or absent response to levodopa.

## Differential Diagnosis

- **Essential or dystonic tremor**
  - Tremor absent at rest.
  - Tremor occurs with posture or action.
  - Large, shaky handwriting.
  - May involve head, neck and voice.
  - Other Parkinsonian features usually absent.

- **Drug-induced Parkinsonism**
  - Exposure to dopamine-blocking medications.
  - Prochlorperazine, metoclopramide and antipsychotics.
  - Typically bilateral and symmetrical.

- **Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH)**
  - Prominent gait freezing.
  - Broad-based gait.
  - Urinary incontinence.
  - Cognitive impairment.
  - Ventriculomegaly on CT/MRI.

- **Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)**
  - Early recurrent falls, particularly backwards.
  - Postural instability.
  - Early cognitive slowing or personality changes.
  - Poor levodopa response.

- **Multiple system atrophy (MSA)**
  - Early, severe autonomic dysfunction.
  - Urinary incontinence.
  - Erectile dysfunction.
  - Orthostatic hypotension.
  - Cerebellar signs.
  - Poor levodopa response.

- **Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)**
  - Fluctuating cognition.
  - Early, well-formed visual hallucinations.
  - Dementia occurring concurrently with or preceding motor symptoms.

## Diagnosis

- Primarily a clinical diagnosis.
- Based on:
  - Detailed history.
  - Neurological examination.
  - Bradykinesia with resting tremor and/or rigidity.
- Limb bradykinesia is essential for diagnosis.
- Assess motor decrement during repetitive movements.
- CT/MRI:
  - Helps exclude alternative diagnoses and Parkinson's disease mimics.
  - Does not independently establish the diagnosis.
- Poor levodopa response warrants neurological reassessment.

## Management

- **General principles**
  - Refer to specialist neurology services.
  - Consider early referral to a Parkinson's disease specialist nurse.
  - Initiate treatment according to:
    - Motor and non-motor disability.
    - Impact on quality of life.
    - Patient preference.
    - Age and comorbidities.
  - Do not unnecessarily delay treatment when symptoms cause disability.

- **Levodopa + peripheral dopa decarboxylase inhibitor**
  - Levodopa/carbidopa.
  - Levodopa/benserazide.
  - Most effective treatment for motor symptoms.
  - First-line therapy in most patients.
  - Typical levodopa dose: 300–1000 mg/day in 3–5 divided doses.
  - Adverse effects:
    - Nausea.
    - Orthostatic dizziness.
    - Somnolence.

- **MAO-B inhibitors**
  - Rasagiline: 1 mg daily.
  - Selegiline: 2.5–10 mg/day in divided doses.
  - Safinamide: 50–100 mg daily.
  - Option for mild symptoms.
  - Adverse effects:
    - Nausea.
    - Orthostatic dizziness.
    - Potential serotonin toxicity with serotonergic medications.

- **Dopamine agonists**
  - Pramipexole:
    - Immediate release: 0.125–1 mg three times daily.
    - Controlled release: 0.375–3 mg daily.
  - Rotigotine patch: 2–8 mg daily.
  - Adverse effects:
    - Nausea.
    - Orthostatic dizziness.
    - Hallucinations.
    - Impulse control disorders.
    - Peripheral oedema.
    - Somnolence.
  - Start low and titrate slowly.

- **COMT inhibitors**
  - Entacapone.
  - Used in combination with levodopa.
  - Prolongs levodopa action.
  - Useful in wearing-off phenomenon.

- **Anticholinergics**
  - Benztropine: 0.5–5 mg/day.
  - Trihexyphenidyl: 0.5–5 mg/day.
  - Occasionally useful for prominent resting tremor.
  - Adverse effects:
    - Confusion.
    - Urinary retention.
    - Constipation.
    - Dry mouth.
    - Dry eyes.
  - Use cautiously in elderly patients and those with cognitive impairment.

- **NMDA receptor antagonist**
  - Amantadine: 100 mg 1–3 times daily.
  - Useful in managing levodopa-induced dyskinesia.
  - Adverse effects:
    - Hallucinations.
    - Peripheral oedema.
    - Insomnia.
    - Livedo reticularis.

## Management of Non-Motor Symptoms

- **REM sleep behaviour disorder**
  - Clonazepam: 0.5–1 mg at night.
  - Sleep hygiene.

- **Insomnia**
  - Sleep hygiene.
  - Amitriptyline: 5–25 mg approximately 2 hours before bedtime.

- **Constipation**
  - Fibre supplements: 1–2 teaspoons three times daily.
  - Macrogol: 1–2 sachets twice daily.
  - Avoid prolonged use of anthranoid laxatives (e.g. senna).

- **Psychosis**
  - Quetiapine: 12.5–75 mg in divided doses.

- **Depression**
  - Paroxetine: 20 mg.
  - Venlafaxine XR: 75–150 mg.

- **Restless legs syndrome**
  - Pramipexole: 125–750 micrograms at night.

- **Orthostatic hypotension**
  - Increase fluid intake (at least 1.5 L/day).
  - Increase salt intake where appropriate.
  - Monitor for supine/nocturnal hypertension.
  - Fludrocortisone: 100–300 micrograms in divided doses.

## Non-Pharmacological Management

- Exercise is a core component of management.
- Encourage:
  - Aerobic exercise.
  - Resistance training.
  - Balance training.
  - HIIT, where appropriate.
  - Dance and agility training.
- Early referral to:
  - Physiotherapy.
  - Occupational therapy.
- Physiotherapy targets:
  - Gait.
  - Balance.
  - Transfers.
  - Upper limb function.
- Aim to maintain independence and prevent complications of immobility.

## Prognosis

- Slowly progressive neurodegenerative disorder.
- Disease progression varies between individuals.
- Major functional changes may occur over 5–10-year intervals.
- Most patients with moderate symptoms respond well to levodopa.
- Dementia may develop in advanced disease.
- Early diagnosis and appropriate symptomatic treatment improve quality of life.

## Patient Counselling

- Explain that Parkinson's disease affects both motor and non-motor functions.
- Reassure patients that:
  - Disease progression is generally slow.
  - Effective symptomatic treatments are available.
  - Exercise and physiotherapy are important.
  - Treatment aims to maintain independence and quality of life.
- Encourage shared decision-making.
- Arrange regular follow-up to assess symptoms, treatment response and adverse effects.
