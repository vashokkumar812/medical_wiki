# in child

- Acute Seizures in Children

	- Key Points
		- Aim to identify reversible causes and manage accordingly
		- Most seizures resolve spontaneously within 5 minutes and do not require medication
		- Commence pharmacological treatment if:
			- Total seizure duration ≥5 minutes
			- Duration unknown
		- Include any pre-hospital benzodiazepine doses when calculating treatment
		- Ensure parental education regarding:
			- Seizure safety
			- Future seizure management
			- Recurrence risk

	- Background
		- Seizures occur in approximately 1 in 20 children (5%)
		- Most are:
			- Brief
			- Self-limiting
			- Stop within 5 minutes

		- Immediate treatment required if:
			- Seizure duration >5 minutes
			- Duration unknown
			- Cardiorespiratory compromise
			- Known high-risk pathology:
				- Meningitis
				- Hypoxic brain injury
				- Trauma

	- Acute Assessment
		- Assessment and management occur simultaneously during active seizure

		- Key considerations
			- Duration of seizure
				- Include pre-hospital seizure time
			- Benzodiazepines already administered
			- Previous seizure history
			- Existing seizure management plan
			- Current anti-seizure medications
			- Neurological comorbidities:
				- VP shunt
				- Structural brain abnormalities
			- Renal failure
				- Hypertensive encephalopathy
			- Endocrine disorders
				- Electrolyte abnormalities
			- Presence of focal features
			- Evidence of treatable underlying cause

		- Reversible causes
			- Hypoglycaemia
			- Hyponatraemia
			- Hypocalcaemia
			- Hypomagnesaemia
			- Meningitis
			- Drug overdose
			- Toxin exposure
			- Envenomation
			- Head trauma
			- Stroke
			- Intracranial haemorrhage

		- Age-related considerations
			- Children <6 months:
				- Higher likelihood of treatable cause
				- Consider pyridoxine-dependent seizures

	- History

		- Obtain:
			- Child's account if possible
			- Witness account
			- Video recordings if available

		- Detailed chronological description:
			- Before seizure
			- During seizure
			- After seizure

		- Ask specifically about:
			- Aura
			- Focal symptoms
			- Level of awareness
			- Recent trauma
			- Possible non-accidental injury
			- Eye deviation
			- Limb focality
			- Post-ictal phase
			- Todd's paresis/hemiparesis
			- Sleep-wake relationship

		- Relevant past history
			- Previous seizures
			- Developmental history
			- Developmental regression
			- Family history:
				- Seizures
				- Arrhythmias
				- Sudden cardiac death

		- History suggestive of epilepsy syndromes
			- Absence seizures
			- Myoclonic jerks
			- Nocturnal events

	- Examination

		- Full neurological examination
			- Focal deficits
			- Meningism
			- Raised ICP signs

		- Cardiovascular examination
			- BP measurement
			- Rhythm assessment

		- Look for underlying causes
			- Neurocutaneous stigmata
			- Microcephaly

		- Assess for toxidrome
			- Tachycardia
			- Tachypnoea
			- Hypertension
			- Hyperthermia
			- Diaphoresis
			- Diarrhoea
			- Tremor
			- Hyperreflexia

	- Red Flags
		- Head injury with delayed seizure
		- Developmental delay
		- Developmental regression
		- Headache preceding seizure
		- Hypertension
		- Bleeding disorder
		- Anticoagulant therapy
		- Toxidrome features
		- Focal neurological signs
		- Suspected non-accidental injury
		- Arrhythmia
			- Seizure may be cause or consequence
			- Consider ECG

	- Differential Diagnosis

		- Cardiac
			- Arrhythmia
			- Vasovagal syncope with anoxic seizure

		- Breath-holding spell
			- Typically occurs during crying

		- Paroxysmal non-epileptic events (PNEEs)

		- Gastrointestinal
			- Sandifer syndrome

		- Behavioural
			- Gratification disorder (infantile masturbation)

		- Psychiatric
			- Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES)

		- Benign infantile movements
			- Shuddering attacks
			- Sleep myoclonus

	- Management
		- Initial principles
			- Most seizures self-terminate within 5 minutes
			- Observe during first 5 minutes if:
				- Airway maintained
				- No cardiorespiratory compromise

		- Seizure first aid
			- Protect from injury
			- Maintain airway
			- Recovery position when appropriate

		- Active seizure management
			- Include pre-hospital benzodiazepines in dose count
			- Maximum:
				- 2 appropriate benzodiazepine doses total
			- Refer to patient-specific seizure plan if available
			- Avoid medications:
				- Known allergy
				- Previously ineffective

		- Special situations
			- Child already taking phenytoin:
				- Use phenobarbitone as alternative second-line agent

	- Acute Seizure Medications

		- First-Line

			- Midazolam
				- IV/IM/IO:
					- 0.15 mg/kg
					- Max 10 mg
				- Buccal/Intranasal:
					- 0.3 mg/kg
					- Max 10 mg
				- Notes:
					- Injection solution can be given:
						- Buccally
						- Orally
						- Intranasally
					- Intranasal route may cause:
						- Burning
						- Nasal irritation

			- Diazepam
				- IV/IO:
					- 0.3 mg/kg
					- Max 10 mg
				- Notes:
					- IV preferred
					- Do not give IM

		- Second-Line

			- Phenytoin
				- Loading:
					- 20 mg/kg IV/IO
					- Max 2 g
				- Infusion:
					- Undiluted or ≥5 mg/mL
					- 1 mg/kg/min
					- Max 50 mg/min
				- Cardiac monitoring required
				- Contraindicated:
					- Dravet syndrome

			- Levetiracetam
				- 40–60 mg/kg IV/IO
				- Max 4.5 g
				- Dilute to 50 mg/mL
				- Infuse over 5 minutes

			- Phenobarbitone
				- 20 mg/kg IV/IO
				- Max 1 g
				- Dilute to ≤20 mg/mL
				- Infuse over ≥30 minutes
				- Maximum rate:
					- 1 mg/kg/min
				- Cardiac monitoring required
				- Common neonatal agent

			- Valproate
				- 20–40 mg/kg IV
				- Max 3 g
				- Infuse over 3–10 minutes
				- Avoid:
					- Children <3 years
				- Risks:
					- Hepatotoxicity
					- Pancreatitis
					- Hypersensitivity reactions

		- Third-Line

			- Pyridoxine
				- 100 mg IV
				- Consider:
					- Age ≤6 months
					- Refractory seizures

		- Refractory Status Epilepticus
			- Requires:
				- RSI
				- Mechanical ventilation
				- Senior airway support

			- Possible infusions:
				- Midazolam
				- Ketamine
				- Propofol
				- Thiopentone

	- Post-Seizure Care
		- Recovery position
		- Maintain airway
		- Monitor for recurrent seizures
		- Investigate underlying cause

	- Investigations

		- Blood Tests

			- Blood glucose
				- All patients

			- Consider:
				- Electrolytes
				- Calcium
				- Venous blood gas

			- Indications
				- Required second-line therapy
				- Age <12 months
				- Metabolic disorder
				- Diabetes
				- Dehydration
				- Toxidrome
				- Failure to return to baseline

		- ECG
			- If arrhythmia suspected

		- Neuroimaging

			- Consider if:
				- Trauma
				- Focal seizure
				- Third-line therapy required
				- Age <6 months
				- Raised ICP
				- Stroke symptoms
				- Bleeding disorder
				- Anticoagulation
				- Persistent neurological deficit
				- Failure to return to baseline

			- Avoid routine CT:
				- Established epilepsy
				- Typical seizure
				- No other indication

		- EEG

			- Rarely required acutely
			- Not routine after first seizure

			- Consider:
				- Age <12 months
				- Known genetic condition

			- Important syndromes diagnosed by EEG
				- Self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (SeLECTS)
				- Idiopathic generalised epilepsy (IGE)

			- Usually perform after:
				- Second seizure

			- Benefits
				- Diagnostic confirmation
				- May avoid neuroimaging

	- Consult Paediatrics If
		- Age <12 months
		- Suspected infantile spasms
		- Prolonged seizure
		- Incomplete recovery
		- Intracranial pathology
		- Meningoencephalitis
		- Focal seizures
		- Post-ictal focal deficits
		- Recurrent seizures without epilepsy diagnosis
		- Frequent/uncontrolled epilepsy
		- Developmental delay
		- Significant comorbidities
		- Suspected non-accidental injury

	- Admission
		- Consider observation for:
			- All children <12 months

	- Transfer to Tertiary Centre
		- Anticipated ICU requirement
		- Cardiorespiratory compromise

	- Discharge Criteria
		- Older child
		- Returned to baseline
		- No red flags
		- Normal examination

	- Discharge Education
		- Recurrence risk
		- Seizure safety advice
		- Seizure first aid
		- Seizure management plan
		- Encourage video recording of future events if safe
		- Maintain seizure diary
		- Written information provided
		- Consider emergency buccal midazolam
		- Driving advice for adolescents

	- Follow-Up After First Afebrile Seizure
		- All children require medical follow-up
		- EEG not routinely required
		- Consider EEG if:
			- Age <12 months
			- Known genetic condition

	- Fever
	- Intercurrent illness

	- Generalised seizures
		- Generalised onset clinically
		- Generalised onset on EEG
	- Partial (focal) seizures
		- Focal brain origin
		- Usually focal symptoms/signs

		- Can cause
			- Loss of consciousness
			- Secondary generalised tonic-clonic seizure

	- Secondary generalised tonic-clonic seizure may be clinically indistinguishable from primary generalised tonic-clonic seizure
	- EEG and MRI may help distinguish
	- Preferred terminology
		- Tonic-clonic seizure
		- Convulsion

		- Absence seizures

	- Rare
		- Tonic seizures

	- Rare seizure types mainly seen in
		- Severe childhood epilepsies

		- Usually incorrect to diagnose tonic or atonic seizures in adults presenting with falls

	- Literature range
		- <25% to >80%
	- Highest recurrence risk

- treatment 
	- Treatment considerations
		- If history, EEG, or MRI indicates epilepsy
			- Treatment generally recommended
		- Early treatment
			- Reduces seizure recurrence
		- Early treatment does NOT
			- Improve long-term chance of eventual medication-free seizure freedom
		- Antiepileptic drugs
			- Control seizures
			- Not curative
	
	- Antiepileptic drug principles
		- Older first-line drugs
			- Carbamazepine
			- Sodium valproate
			- Phenytoin
		- Newer agents
			- Lamotrigine
			- Gabapentin
			- Topiramate
			- Oxcarbazepine
			- Tiagabine
		- Newer drugs
			- Effective
			- Often better tolerated
		- Older drugs
			- Remain first-line
			- Effective when titrated gradually
	
	- Drug selection according to epilepsy syndrome
		- Generalised epilepsy
			- Valproate preferred
			- If intolerant
				- Lamotrigine
				- Topiramate
			- Important pearl
				- Valproate controls
					- Absence seizures
					- Myoclonic seizures
				- Carbamazepine and phenytoin may
					- Exacerbate absence seizures
					- Exacerbate myoclonic seizures
					- Induce these seizure types
		- Partial epilepsy
			- Carbamazepine preferred
			- If necessary switch to newer agents
			- Carbamazepine superior for
				- Simple partial seizures
	
	- Phenytoin
		- Used less frequently as first-line therapy
		- Reason
			- Cosmetic adverse effects
			- Dental adverse effects
		- Still useful
			- Elderly patients
			- Once-daily night dosing
	
	- Importance of epilepsy syndrome diagnosis
		- More important than seizure type alone
		- Influences
			- Drug selection
			- Treatment response
			- Prognosis
	
	- Duration of treatment
