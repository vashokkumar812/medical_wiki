- Fertility and menstrual management in women with intellectual disability

- AMC focus
	- Ethical principles
	- Informed consent
	- Contraception choices
	- Legal framework
	- Sterilisation and termination regulations

- Core principles

	- Women with intellectual disability
		- Have same reproductive rights as all women
		- Experience similar fertility and menstrual issues
		- Consent/legal decision-making may be more complex

	- Clinical care principles
		- Maximise participation in decision-making
		- Use accessible information
		- Support informed decision-making

- Ethical principles

	- Fundamental principle
		- Informed consent required before examination/treatment

	- Key priorities
		- Respect autonomy
		- Protect reproductive rights
		- Maximise quality of life
		- Use least restrictive treatment

- Rights

	- Women have right to decide
		- Whether to use contraception
		- Which contraception to use

	- Clinical implications
		- Do not assume incapacity
		- Encourage supported decision-making

- Clinical approach

	- Focus on
		- Quality of life
		- Reproductive rights
		- Least harmful treatment
		- Same standards as women without disability

	- Provide
		- Accessible information
		- Supportive communication
		- Decision-making assistance

- Contraception strategy

	- First-line
		- Reversible contraception

	- Preferred methods
		- LARC

			- Etonogestrel implant
				- Implanon

			- Levonorgestrel intrauterine system
				- LNG-IUS

	- Advantages
		- Highly effective
		- Reversible
		- Minimal adherence requirements

	- Avoid as first-line
		- Tubal ligation
		- Surgical sterilisation

	- Irreversible procedures
		- Only after specialist consultation

- Capacity and consent

	- Some patients may lack full legal consent capacity

	- Assessment principles
		- Capacity is decision-specific
		- Capacity varies across domains

	- Important concept
		- Cognitive impairment does not exclude participation in decisions

- Capacity assessment

	- If uncertain
		- Refer for formal assessment

	- Specialists
		- Neuropsychologist
		- Psychiatrist

- Substitute decision-making

	- If patient lacks capacity
		- Consent from substitute decision maker

	- Determined by
		- Local legal/guardianship laws

	- Appropriate for
		- Minor treatments
		- Reversible treatments
		- Low-risk treatments

- When additional advice required

	- Situations
		- Patient disagrees with treatment
		- No substitute decision maker
		- Complex ethical issues

	- Consult
		- Disability specialists
		- Ethics committees
		- Legal advisors

- Permanent sterilisation

	- Includes
		- Tubal ligation
		- Procedures causing permanent infertility

	- Australia
		- Requires application to independent tribunal/statutory body
		- Based on Marion case precedent
		- Applies across all states/territories

	- Minors
		- Require court approval
		- Guardian consent alone insufficient

- Termination of pregnancy

	- Australia
		- If lacking capacity
			- Requires tribunal/statutory approval

	- New Zealand
		- Requires approval by 2 Ministry of Justice certifying consultants
		- Additional expert advice required
			- Mental condition
			- Effect of pregnancy continuation

- Sterilisation law in New Zealand

	- Non-minors
		- Legal guardian may consent

	- If disagreement occurs
		- Seek ethics committee advice
		- May require High Court decision

	- Welfare guardians
		- Cannot consent to sterilisation in minors

	- Court
		- Determines best interests of child

- Clinical decision algorithm

	- Assess capacity

	- If capacity present
		- Provide information
		- Obtain informed consent

	- If capacity absent
		- Formal capacity assessment
		- Identify substitute decision maker

	- Reversible treatment
		- Consent from substitute decision maker acceptable

	- Permanent sterilisation
		- Tribunal/court approval required

	- Pregnancy termination
		- Follow jurisdiction-specific legal process
