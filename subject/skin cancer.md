# SCC
- Definition
	- Second most common type of skin cancer
	- Malignant tumour of keratinocytes

- Epidemiology
	- Common in fair-skinned individuals
	- Usually occurs in people >50 years
	- Commonly affects sun-exposed skin

- Etiology and Risk Factors
	- Strongly associated with cumulative sun exposure
	- May arise in premalignant or chronically damaged skin

	- Premalignant Conditions
		- Solar keratoses
		- Bowen disease
		- Leukoplakia

	- Chronic Injury Sites - espescially in dark skinned people
		- Burns
		- Chronic ulcers

	- May arise de novo

	- HPV Association
		- Genital lesions
		- Subungual lesions
		- Strong association with human papillomavirus infection

	- Keratoacanthoma
		- May represent a variant of SCC

- Important Note
	- Although SCC and BCC are related to cumulative sun exposure:
		- They are not always located on sun-exposed skin

- Clinical Features

	- Early Lesions
		- Firm thickening of skin
		- Especially arising within solar keratoses
		- Surrounding erythema

	- Progressive Lesions
		- Hard nodules
		- Ulceration may occur

	- Ulcer Characteristics
		- Everted edge

- Common Sites
	- Hands
	- Forearms
	- Head
	- Neck

- Metastasis
	- Metastatic rate
		- Up to 5%

	- Higher-Risk Locations
		- Head
		- Neck
		- Genitals

	- Higher Risk Groups
		- Immunosuppressed patients

- Investigations
	- Clinical examination
	- Dermoscopy
	- Skin biopsy
			- Diagnostic confirmation
	- Assess regional lymph nodes

- Management

	- Main Treatment
		- Early surgical excision

	- Surgical Margin
		- Usually 3–5 mm

	- Surgery
		- Treatment of choice for most tumours

	- Treatments NOT Recommended as Primary Therapy
		- Cryotherapy
		- Imiquimod
		- Curettage

- High-Risk Sites
	- Ear
	- Lip
	- Oral cavity
	- Tongue
	- Genitalia

	- Features
		- Increased risk of metastasis
		- Require specialised management

	- Additional Management
		- Postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy often indicated

- Referral Indications
	- Large tumours
	- Difficult anatomical site
	- Surgery contraindicated
	- Lymphadenopathy present
	- Need for specialised surgery or radiotherapy

- Prognosis
	- Good if detected and excised early
	- Delayed treatment increases:
		- Local invasion
		- Metastatic risk

- Key Exam Points
	- SCC is second most common skin cancer
	- Characteristic ulcer has everted edge
	- Metastatic risk higher than BCC
	- Surgery is treatment of choice
	- High-risk sites require aggressive management

# melanoma
- definition 
	- malignant tumour derived from melanocytes
	- most common site
		- skin
- Risk factors
	- previous melanoma ( highest risk )
	- 
	- ≥5 atypical/dysplastic naevi

	- multiple naevi
		- ≥50
	- first-degree relatives
	- increasing age
	- male sex
	- multiple nonmelanoma skin cancers
	- immunodeficiency
	- blistering sunburns in childhood/adolescence, fair complexion/sun-sensitive skin, tanning exposure like solariums, solar skin damage; Fitzpatrick I–II


- Skin surveillance in high-risk patients
	- self skin examination		
	- clinical monitoring
		- full skin examination every 6–12 months
		- dermoscopy
			- reduces unnecessary excisions
		- total body photography
- epidermiology
	- 30 to 50 years

- Clinical features
	- site distribution
		- women
			- lower limbs
		- men
			- upper back
	- usually asymptomatic
	- may bleed or itch

- Red flag features
	- bleeding
	- ulceration
	- itching
	- satellite nodules
	- lymphadenopathy
	- new/changing lesion
	- rapidly growing nodule
		- any colour
	- non-healing ulcer/lump
	- ugly duckling lesion
		- looks different from other moles
	- dermoscopic suspicion
		- asymmetric pigmentation
		- radial streaming
		- poor clinic–dermoscopy correlation


- Variants
	- amelanotic melanoma
		- flesh-coloured papules
		- enlarging/changing lesions
		- difficult diagnosis
		- poor prognosis
			- due to delayed detection

- Prognosis
	- most important prognostic factor
		- Breslow thickness

	- Survival by thickness
		- <0.75 mm
			- \>90% cure
		- ≥4 mm
			- <30% cure

	- Sentinel lymph node biopsy
		- indications
			- thickness ≥1 mm
			- ≥0.75 mm with high-risk features

	- poor prognostic factors
		- head/neck/trunk lesions
		- male sex
		- age >50 years
		- amelanotic melanoma
		- ulceration

- Differential diagnosis
	- thrombosed haemangioma
		- vascular appearance / compressible lesion

	- dermatofibroma
		- firm “dimple sign” on lateral compression
	
	- sclerosing haemangioma
		- vascular lesion with sclerosis
	
	- seborrhoeic keratosis
		- “stuck-on” waxy appearance
	
	- pigmented BCC
		- pearly rolled border with telangiectasia
	
	- junctional naevi
		- uniform flat pigmentation
	
	- compound naevi
		- symmetrical raised pigmented papule
	
	- blue naevi
		- homogeneous steel-blue colour
	
	- dysplastic naevi
		- stable atypical lesion with long-term unchanged history
		- usually multiple
		- larger lesions
		- central darker nodule
			- “target sign”

	- lentigines
		- small uniform macules without evolution

- Facilitating early diagnosis
	- adequate lighting without shadows
	- dermatoscope
		- essential adjunct
	- magnification lamp
		- Maggylamp
- Clinical examination of skin
	- compare naevi across body
	- compare suspicious lesion with similar lesions elsewhere
	- ABCDE system	
		- A = asymmetry
			- melanoma
				- asymmetrical
			- benign lesions
				- symmetrical
				- round/oval
	
		- B = border
			- melanoma
				- irregular
				- sharply defined
			- dysplastic naevus
				- indistinct fading “shoulder” edge
			- benign lesions
				- regular border
		
		- C = colour
			- some melanomas - no black pigmentation
			- multiple colours - important malignant feature
			- colours - VIBGYOR White
			- early melanoma
				- may lack deep pigmentation
		
		- D = diameter
			- usually ≥7 mm
			- may be <5 mm
				- especially nodular melanoma
		
		- E = evolution/elevation
			- melanoma
				- changes over time
			- benign naevus
				- stable
			- elevation
				- suggests invasion
			- flat lesion may become raised
- investigations
	- Dermoscopy
		- Improves diagnostic accuracy
		- Reduces unnecessary excisions
	- Sequential digital dermoscopy imaging
		- Detects suspicious change over time
	- Total body photography
		- High-risk patients
		- Dysplastic naevus syndrome
	- In vivo confocal microscopy
		- Optical biopsy
		- Useful for
			- Amelanotic melanoma
			- Lentigo maligna
	- Smartphone melanoma apps
		- Not sufficiently accurate
		- Not recommended

- Management
	-  Contraindicated Before Diagnosis
		- Wide excision
		- Flap reconstruction
		- Curettagez
		- partial biopsy
		- cryotherapy/freezing of pigmented lesions
	- suspicious lesion
		- excision biopsy with 2 mm margin
		- if not feasible
			- specialist referral
	- if If Complete Excision Not Possible
		- Large incisional biopsy
		- Include most atypical area
	- If melanoma diagnosed
		- wide local excision required
	- Excision margins
		- Excision biopsy
			- suspicious lesion
				- 2 mm margin
		- Wide excision
			- melanoma in situ
				- 5–10 mm
			- ≤1 mm
				- 1 cm
				- consider SLNB if >0.75 mm + high risk
			- 1.01–2 mm
				- 1–2 cm + SLNB
			- 2.01–4 mm
				- 1–2 cm + SLNB
			- >4 mm
				- 2 cm + SLNB
	- Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB)
		- indications
			- thickness >1 mm
			- thickness >0.75 mm + high-risk features
		- High-risk features
			- high mitotic rate
			- ulceration
			- Clark level IV/V
			- lymphovascular invasion
	- Radiotherapy
		- Desmoplastic melanoma
		- Palliation
			- Brain metastases
			- Bone metastases
	- Follow-up schedule
		- Stage I
			- Annually for 10 years
		- Stage IIA
			- Every 6 months for 2 years
			- Then annually for 8 years
		- Stage IIB-IIC
			- Every 3-4 months for 2 years
			- Every 6 months during year 3
			- Then annually for 5 years
		- Stage III
			- Every 3 months for 2 years
			- Every 6 months during year 3
			- Then annually for 5 years
		- Positive SLNB without adjuvant therapy
			- Ultrasound every 3-4 months
			- Minimum 3 years
