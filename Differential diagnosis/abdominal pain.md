## approach
- Atypical presentations are common in
	- elderly
	- diabetic
	- immunocompromised patients.
- Neurological basis of abdominal pain
	- Three categories of abdominal pain:
		- Visceral pain
			- from internal organs
			- Transmitted predominantly through autonomic pathways.
			- Usually diffuse and poorly localised
			- Related predominantly to stretch receptor activation
		- Parietal (somatic) pain
			- Originates from the parietal peritoneum.
			- Transmitted through somatic nerve pathways.
			- More intense and accurately localised.
		- Referred pain
			- Pain perceived at a site distant from its actual origin.
			- Visceral and parietal pathways synapse with second-order neurones in close proximity within the spinal cord.
			- The cerebral cortex may interpret visceral impulses as originating from somatic pathways.
	- Clinical examples
		- Appendicitis
			- Initially activates visceral nerves.
			- Produces diffuse, poorly localised pain.
			- Extension of inflammation to the parietal peritoneum activates somatic nerves.
			- Pain becomes more intense and localised.
		- Testicular torsion
			- May initially present with ipsilateral flank pain.
			- The testis and kidney share a common embryological origin.
			- Pain may subsequently localise to the scrotum.
		- Cardiac pain
			- Recruits C4–T5 spinal segments.
			- May present as referred pain to the shoulder and arm (C4–C6) and upper abdomen (T3–T5).

- Clinical considerations and red flags
	- ABC assessment
		
		- Assess cardiopulmonary stability first.
			
		- Assess airway, breathing and circulation.
			
		- Haemodynamic instability or respiratory compromise requires urgent management irrespective of the underlying cause.
			
	- Vital signs
		
		- Blood pressure.
			
		- Pulse rate.
			
		- Respiratory rate.
			
		- Temperature.
			
		- Oxygen saturation.
			
		- Abnormal vital signs indicate physiological derangement but are not independently diagnostic.
			
	- Additional clinical considerations
		
		- Patients with abdominal pain that is not reproducible on examination and who remain distressed or clinically unwell require further assessment.
			
		- Consider cardiac, pulmonary and intra-abdominal causes, including mesenteric ischaemia.
			
		- Perform random blood glucose in diabetic patients and when diabetic ketoacidosis is suspected.
			
		- Consider urinalysis as part of the initial assessment.
			
- Acute coronary syndrome presenting as abdominal pain
	
	- Clinical features
		
		- Epigastric pain.
			
		- Nausea and vomiting.
			
		- Exertional abdominal pain.
			
		- Previous coronary artery disease.
			
		- Significant cardiac risk factors.
			
		- Food-associated epigastric pain may occur in severe coronary artery disease, including triple-vessel and critical left main disease.
			
		- Improvement with antacids does not exclude a cardiac cause.
			
	- High-risk populations
		
		- Elderly patients.
			
		- Diabetic patients.
			
		- Female patients.
			
		- Immunocompromised patients.
			
	- Investigations
		
		- ECG is the first-line investigation for suspected coronary artery disease.
			
		- A normal initial ECG does not exclude acute myocardial infarction.
			
		- Obtain serum cardiac markers.
			
		- Troponin I or T is preferred over CK-MB in the article.
			
		- A negative early troponin does not reliably exclude myocardial injury.
			
		- Continuous ECG monitoring and vital-sign monitoring are recommended during further cardiac evaluation.
			
		- Patients with abnormal ECG or cardiac markers, or persistent clinical concern, require appropriate cardiac service assessment.
			
- Respiratory examination
	
	- Chest examination, particularly auscultation of the basal lung fields, should be part of routine assessment.
		
	- Nonabdominal causes of tachypnoea include:
		
		- Hypoxia due to primary respiratory pathology.
			
		- Metabolic acidosis.
			
		- Sepsis.
			
		- Anaemia.
			
		- Hyperventilation.
			
		- Diaphragmatic pathology.
			
	- Lower-lobe pneumonia may present with upper abdominal pain as the primary symptom.
		
	- Pneumothorax and pulmonary embolism may less frequently present as isolated upper abdominal pain.
		
	- Investigations may include:
		
		- Chest X-ray.
			
		- CT.
			
		- Chest ultrasound.
			
- History taking
	
	- A comprehensive symptom history is essential for identifying the cause of abdominal pain.
		
	- Inadequate history taking and documentation are common contributors to misdiagnosis.
		
	- Language and cultural differences may affect communication.
		
	- Patients with previous coronary artery disease or significant cardiac risk factors require further evaluation for cardiac causes.
		
	- Assume that patients with exertional abdominal pain may have ACS until adequately evaluated.
		
	- Nausea, vomiting and anorexia are not specific indicators of gastrointestinal disease.
		
- Physical examination
	
	- Record blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate and temperature.
		
	- Record oxygen saturation where available.
		
	- Assess for:
		
		- Hypotension.
			
		- Tachycardia or bradycardia.
			
		- Hypoxia.
			
		- Respiratory distress.
			
		- Inadequate perfusion.
			
	- Patients with abnormal vital signs require further evaluation irrespective of the suspected cause.
		
- Laboratory investigations
	
	- No specific laboratory investigation can categorically exclude all extra-abdominal causes of abdominal pain.
		
	- Normal WCC and CRP should not be used as the sole basis for discharge when the patient remains clinically unwell.
		
	- Normal WCC may occur in:
		
		- 30–40% of elderly patients with acute cholecystitis.
			
		- 20% of patients with acute appendicitis.
			
	- Consider arterial blood gas analysis in critically unwell patients with an unclear diagnosis.
		
		- Provides a physiological assessment.
			
		- Helps identify hypoxia and acidosis.
			
		- Hypoxia or acidosis indicates significant physiological dysfunction requiring urgent attention.
			
		- Normal pH and oxygen levels do not exclude early pathology.
			
- Observation and further evaluation
	
	- Further investigations depend on individual patient circumstances.
		
	- Consider chest X-ray, cardiac markers, CT and ultrasound where indicated.
		
	- Observation for 8–10 hours has been demonstrated to improve diagnostic accuracy in borderline appendicitis cases.
		
	- Observation may be considered when:
		
		- An extra-abdominal cause is being considered.
			
		- The patient remains unwell without an established diagnosis.
			
	- Early specialist referral should be considered when the diagnosis remains unclear and the patient appears clinically unwell.
		
	- Incorrect discharge diagnoses are associated with increased complications and mortality, particularly in elderly patients.
		
- Conclusion
	
	- Diagnostic accuracy based on clinical signs, symptoms and preliminary investigations is limited.
		
	- Reported clinical accuracy is approximately 50–65% overall and as low as 30% in elderly patients.
		
	- Patients who appear unwell with unexplained abdominal pain require further evaluation for both intra-abdominal and extra-abdominal pathology.
		
	- At minimum, assess vital signs, obtain an ECG and record blood glucose.
		
	- Maintain a high index of suspicion.
		
	- The priority is correct patient disposition: the right patient, to the right service, within the right time frame.
