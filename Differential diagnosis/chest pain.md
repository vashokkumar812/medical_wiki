 - **Opening questions**
    - "Can you tell me more about the chest pain or discomfort?"
    - "Are you experiencing the pain right now?"
    - "Have you experienced similar pain before?"

  - **Site**
    - "Where exactly do you feel the pain?"
    - Central or retrosternal pain
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial ischaemia
          - Usually central rather than left-sided.
          - May be perceived as discomfort rather than pain.
        - Myocardial infarction
          - Central chest pain or discomfort.
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Gastro-oesophageal reflux
          - Retrosternal discomfort.
        - Diffuse oesophageal spasm
          - Retrosternal pain mimicking angina.
      - Airway
        - Tracheitis
          - Pain or discomfort in the throat or retrosternal region.
        - Central bronchial carcinoma
          - Persistent central chest discomfort.
      - Mediastinal
        - Mediastinitis
          - Severe retrosternal pain.
        - Sarcoid adenopathy
        - Lymphoma

    - Left-sided or localised chest pain
      - Respiratory
        - Pneumothorax
        - Pneumonia
        - Infective pleurisy
      - Chest wall
        - Muscular strain
        - Intercostal myositis
        - Rib fracture
        - Tietze's syndrome
        - Rib tumour
        - Metastatic tumour

    - Posterior chest pain or interscapular pain
      - Vascular
        - Aortic dissection
          - Pain may radiate to the back or between the shoulder blades.
          - Proximal dissection may cause anterior chest pain.
        - Thoracic nerve compression or infiltration
          - Pain may radiate around the chest from the back.
      - Musculoskeletal
        - Thoracic nerve-root compression
          - Pain follows a nerve-root distribution.

  - **Onset**
    - "When did the pain start?"
    - "Did it start suddenly or gradually?"
    - "Was it severe right from the beginning?"
    - "What were you doing when the pain started?"

    - Sudden or instantaneous onset
      - Vascular
        - Aortic dissection
          - Very sudden onset.
          - Pain is often maximal at onset.
          - Severe tearing or ripping character.
        - Massive pulmonary embolism
          - Very sudden onset.
          - May be associated with collapse, dyspnoea and cyanosis.
      - Respiratory
        - Pneumothorax
          - Sudden onset of sharp localised pain.
          - Associated dyspnoea.
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Oesophageal rupture
          - Consider following forceful vomiting.

    - Gradual or subacute onset
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial ischaemia
          - May develop with increasing exertion.
        - Myocardial infarction
          - May develop over minutes.
      - Respiratory
        - Pneumonia
        - Infective pleurisy
      - Musculoskeletal
        - Muscular strain
        - Intercostal myositis

  - **Character**
    - "How would you describe the pain?"
    - "Is it sharp, stabbing, crushing, heavy, tight, burning or tearing?"

    - Tightness, heaviness or crushing pain
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial ischaemia
          - Central retrosternal tightness or heaviness.
          - May be described as crushing, aching or choking discomfort.
        - Myocardial infarction
          - Central severe crushing or heavy discomfort.

    - Sharp or stabbing pain
      - Pleuropericardial
        - Pericarditis
          - Pleuritic pain.
        - Infective pleurisy
          - Sharp pleuritic pain.
        - Pneumonia
          - Often pleuritic pain.
        - Autoimmune pleuritis
          - Pleuritic pain.
      - Respiratory
        - Pneumothorax
          - Sudden sharp pain associated with dyspnoea.
      - Chest wall
        - Intercostal myositis
          - Sharp localised pain, worse with movement.
        - Rib fracture
          - Localised pain with history of trauma.
        - Tietze's syndrome
          - Pain associated with costal cartilage tenderness.

    - Tearing or ripping pain
      - Vascular
        - Aortic dissection
          - Very severe tearing pain.
          - Instantaneous onset.
          - Radiation to the back.

    - Burning pain
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Gastro-oesophageal reflux
          - Retrosternal burning discomfort.
          - May occur without typical heartburn.

    - Severe constant pain
      - Pleuropericardial
        - Mesothelioma
          - Severe persistent pain.
        - Metastatic tumour
          - Severe, constant and localised pain.
      - Chest wall
        - Rib tumour
          - Constant, severe and localised pain.

  - **Radiation**
    - "Does the pain spread anywhere?"
    - "Does it travel to your arms, jaw, neck, shoulders or back?"

    - Radiation to jaw or arms
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial ischaemia
        - Myocardial infarction
          - May radiate to the jaw or left arm.
          - Rarely travels below the umbilicus.

    - Radiation to the back
      - Vascular
        - Aortic dissection
          - Characteristic radiation to the back.
          - May involve the interscapular region.

    - Radiation around the chest following a nerve-root distribution
      - Chest wall or neurological
        - Thoracic nerve compression or infiltration
          - Pain follows a nerve-root distribution.
        - Thoracic zoster
          - Pain follows a thoracic dermatome.
          - May precede the vesicular rash.

  - **Duration and timing**
    - "How long does each episode last?"
    - "Is the pain continuous or intermittent?"
    - "How frequently does it occur?"
    - "Has the duration or frequency changed recently?"

    - Brief episodes
      - Cardiac
        - Stable angina
          - Usually lasts a few minutes.
          - Relieved rapidly by rest.
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Oesophageal spasm
          - May cause intermittent retrosternal discomfort.

    - Pain lasting more than 30 minutes
      - Cardiac
        - Acute coronary syndrome
          - More likely than stable angina.
          - Often severe and prolonged.

    - Continuous pain lasting many days
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial infarction or stable angina becomes less likely as the explanation for continuously persistent pain.
      - Other causes
        - Pleuropericardial disease.
        - Musculoskeletal pain.
        - Malignancy.

  - **Precipitating and exacerbating factors**
    - "What were you doing when the pain started?"
    - "Does exercise or walking bring on the pain?"
    - "Does taking a deep breath or coughing make it worse?"
    - "Does lying down make it worse?"
    - "Does movement of your shoulders or chest make it worse?"
    - "Does eating trigger the pain?"

    - Physical exertion
      - Cardiac
        - Stable angina
          - Characteristically occurs during exertion.
          - May occur when walking uphill or climbing stairs.
          - The amount of exertion required to trigger pain may be predictable.
        - Myocardial ischaemia
          - Pain may occur with increasing physical activity.
        - Postprandial angina
          - May occur when exercising after eating.

    - Pain occurring at rest
      - Cardiac
        - Acute coronary syndrome
          - Frequently presents at rest.
        - Unstable angina
          - Pain may occur with minimal exertion or at rest.
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Gastro-oesophageal reflux
          - May occur at rest.
      - Musculoskeletal
        - Chest wall pain
          - May be more noticeable at rest than during exertion.

    - Deep inspiration or coughing
      - Pleuropericardial
        - Pericarditis
        - Infective pleurisy
        - Autoimmune pleuritis
      - Respiratory
        - Pneumothorax
        - Pneumonia
        - Pulmonary embolism
      - Chest wall
        - Rib fracture
        - Intercostal myositis

    - Lying supine
      - Cardiac
        - Pericarditis
          - Pain typically worsens when lying supine.
          - Often relieved by sitting up and leaning forwards.
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Gastro-oesophageal reflux
          - Symptoms may worsen when lying down.

    - Movement of shoulders or chest wall
      - Chest wall
        - Muscular strain
          - Pain worsens with movement.
        - Intercostal myositis
          - Pain worsens with movement.
        - Thoracic nerve compression
          - Pain associated with movement.

    - Persistent coughing
      - Chest wall
        - Muscular strain.
        - Intercostal myositis.
        - Rib injury.

  - **Relieving factors**
    - "Does anything make the pain better?"
    - "Does it improve when you stop exercising?"
    - "Does sitting up or leaning forwards relieve it?"
    - "Does swallowing warm water relieve the pain?"
    - "Have you used GTN, and did it help?"

    - Relieved by rest
      - Cardiac
        - Stable angina
          - Pain usually resolves rapidly after stopping exertion.

    - Relieved by nitrates
      - Cardiac
        - Angina
          - Usually improves within a few minutes after sublingual GTN.
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Oesophageal spasm
          - May also improve with nitrates.
      - Important
        - Response to GTN is not specific for cardiac pain.

    - Relieved by sitting up and leaning forwards
      - Pleuropericardial
        - Pericarditis
          - Positional relief is characteristic.

    - Relieved by swallowing warm water
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Diffuse oesophageal spasm
          - May improve with swallowing warm water.

  - **Associated symptoms**
    - "Do you experience any other symptoms along with the chest pain?"

    - Sweating
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial infarction
          - Sweating is more suggestive of MI than stable angina.

    - Nausea or vomiting
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial infarction
          - May accompany severe ischaemic chest pain.

    - Anxiety or marked distress
      - Cardiac
        - Myocardial infarction
          - Anxiety may accompany acute ischaemia.
      - Psychological
        - Panic attack
          - May precede or accompany chest discomfort.

    - Breathlessness
      - Vascular
        - Massive pulmonary embolism
          - Sudden dyspnoea.
          - May be associated with collapse and cyanosis.
      - Respiratory
        - Pneumothorax
          - Sudden pain with dyspnoea.
        - Pneumonia
          - Pleuritic pain with fever and dyspnoea.

    - Dizziness and perioral paraesthesia
      - Psychological
        - Panic attack
          - Associated with hyperventilation.

    - Vesicular rash
      - Chest wall or neurological
        - Thoracic herpes zoster
          - Severe unilateral pain may precede the typical vesicular rash.
          - Rash follows a thoracic nerve-root distribution.

    - Right upper quadrant abdominal pain or tenderness
      - Gastrointestinal
        - Cholecystitis
          - May cause pain referred to the chest.
          - Right upper quadrant tenderness is usually present.

    - Collapse or cyanosis
      - Vascular
        - Massive pulmonary embolism
          - May produce sudden chest pain, dyspnoea, collapse and cyanosis.

  - **Specific questions for suspected angina**

    - "Can you describe the pain or discomfort? Is it sharp or dull, heavy or tight?"
      - Tight or heavy
        - Myocardial ischaemia.
        - Angina.
      - Sharp or stabbing
        - Pericarditis.
        - Pleurisy.
      - Burning
        - Gastro-oesophageal reflux.

    - "When do you get the pain? Does it come out of the blue or occur during physical activity?"
      - Predictable onset during exertion
        - Stable angina.
      - Occurs at rest
        - Acute coronary syndrome.
        - Unstable angina.
        - Gastro-oesophageal reflux.

    - "How long does it last?"
      - Brief episodes
        - Stable angina.
      - Prolonged pain
        - Acute coronary syndrome.

    - "Where do you feel it?"
      - Central retrosternal discomfort
        - Myocardial ischaemia.
        - Myocardial infarction.

    - "Does it make you stop or slow down?"
      - Yes
        - Stable angina.
          - Exertional discomfort often forces the patient to stop or slow down.

    - "Does it go away quickly when you stop exercising?"
      - Yes
        - Stable angina.
          - Rapid relief after rest is characteristic.

    - "Is it coming on with less effort or at rest?"
      - Yes
        - Unstable angina.
        - Acute coronary syndrome.
          - Change in the pattern of previously stable angina is concerning.

    - "Have you had angina before, and is this pain similar?"
      - Similar predictable episodes
        - Stable angina.
      - New or worsening symptoms
        - Unstable angina.
        - Acute coronary syndrome.

  - **Important symptom patterns**

    - Features favouring stable angina
      - Tight or heavy discomfort.
      - Predictable onset with exertion.
      - Relieved by rest.
      - Rapid relief with GTN.
      - Usually not positional.
      - Usually unaffected by respiration.

    - Features favouring acute coronary syndrome
      - Onset at rest.
      - Severe and prolonged pain.
      - Sweating.
      - Anxiety or distress.
      - Nausea and vomiting.
      - Not reliably relieved by GTN.

    - Features favouring pericarditis or pleurisy
      - Sharp or stabbing pain.
      - Not typically exertional.
      - Worse with inspiration.
      - Pericarditis is worse when supine.
      - May improve when sitting forward.
      - Pericardial or pleural friction rub may be present.

    - Features favouring gastro-oesophageal reflux
      - Burning retrosternal discomfort.
      - Not typically related to exertion.
      - May occur when lying supine.
      - May improve with antacids.

    - Features favouring aortic dissection
      - Very sudden onset.
      - Severe pain.
      - Tearing character.
      - Radiation to the back.

    - Features favouring chest wall pain
      - Localised pain.
      - Often positional.
      - Worse with movement.
      - May be prolonged.
      - Localised chest wall tenderness.

    - Features favouring pulmonary embolism
      - Sudden onset.
      - Often pleuritic.
      - May be retrosternal and mimic anginal pain.
      - Associated dyspnoea.
      - May cause collapse and cyanosis.

  - **Additional relevant questions**
    - "Have you had similar episodes before?"
      - Previous stable angina.
      - Previous myocardial infarction.
      - Recurrent oesophageal spasm.
      - Recurrent chest wall pain.

    - "Has the pain changed in its usual pattern?"
      - Pain occurring with less exertion.
      - Increased frequency.
      - Increased duration.
      - Pain occurring at rest.
      - Unstable angina or acute coronary syndrome.

    - "Do you have a history of hypertension or a connective tissue disorder?"
      - Aortic dissection
        - Hypertension.
        - Marfan syndrome.
        - Ehlers–Danlos syndrome.

    - "Have you recently had an infection?"
      - Pericarditis.
      - Myocarditis.
      - Infective pleurisy.
      - Pneumonia.

    - "Have you experienced any recent injury or persistent coughing?"
      - Rib fracture.
      - Muscular strain.
      - Intercostal myositis.

    - "Have you been exposed to asbestos?"
      - Mesothelioma.

    - "Have you had unexplained weight loss or persistent cough?"
      - Lung carcinoma.
      - Mesothelioma.
      - Metastatic tumour.

    - "Have you recently had prolonged immobility or surgery?"
      - Pulmonary embolism.

    - "Have you recently experienced forceful vomiting?"
      - Boerhaave syndrome.

    - "Have you noticed any skin rash over the painful area?"
      - Herpes zoster
        - Unilateral dermatomal pain.
        - Vesicular rash may appear after pain begins.


## chest paid child 

# child with chest pain
- Definition
	- Chest pain in children is a common presentation
	- Most cases are benign, self-limiting, or have no identifiable cause
	- Serious cardiac or respiratory causes are rare

- Etiology
	- Musculoskeletal
		- Costochondritis
		- Muscle strain
		- Chest wall pain
	- Precordial catch syndrome
	- Respiratory
		- Viral respiratory infection
		- Pneumonia
		- Asthma
		- Pneumothorax
	- Gastrointestinal
		- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
		- Oesophagitis
		- Biliary disease
		- Foreign body ingestion
	- Psychological
		- Anxiety
		- Panic attacks
	- Cardiac (rare)
		- Myocarditis
		- Pericarditis
		- Arrhythmia
		- Cardiomyopathy
		- Myocardial ischaemia
	- Vascular
		- Pulmonary embolism

- Pathogenesis
	- Depends on underlying cause
	- Musculoskeletal:
		- Chest wall inflammation or strain
	- Respiratory:
		- Pleural irritation
		- Airway inflammation
	- Gastrointestinal:
		- Oesophageal irritation or spasm
	- Cardiac:
		- Myocardial or pericardial inflammation
		- Reduced cardiac perfusion
	- Psychological:
		- Hyperventilation
		- Somatic symptom manifestation

- Most common
	- Most common cause = musculoskeletal chest pain
	- Most common identifiable causes:
		- Musculoskeletal pain/costochondritis
		- Precordial catch syndrome
		- Respiratory infection
		- Asthma
		- Gastrointestinal causes
		- Anxiety
	- Cardiac causes account for approximately 1% of presentations

- Epidemiology
	- Common paediatric presentation
	- Majority of cases are non-cardiac
	- Serious cardiac disease is uncommon in previously healthy children

- Clinical features
	- Chest pain
		- Sharp
		- Dull
		- Pleuritic
		- Positional
		- Exertional
	- Associated symptoms may include:
		- Dyspnoea
		- Palpitations
		- Dizziness
		- Syncope
		- Fever
		- Cough
		- Epigastric pain
		- Vomiting
		- Anxiety
	- Important history:
		- Onset
		- Duration
		- Site
		- Radiation
		- Aggravating and relieving factors
		- Recent illness
		- Recent vaccination
		- Trauma
		- Family history

- Examination
	- General appearance
	- Vital signs
		- Heart rate
		- Respiratory rate
		- Blood pressure
		- Oxygen saturation
	- Signs of cardiorespiratory compromise:
		- Tachypnoea
		- Increased work of breathing
		- Hypoxia
		- Abnormal pulse
		- Abnormal blood pressure
		- Distended neck veins
		- Muffled heart sounds
		- Reduced chest expansion
		- Absent breath sounds
		- Altered conscious state
	- Chest wall tenderness may suggest musculoskeletal cause

- Diagnosis
	- Primarily clinical
	- Aim is to identify or exclude serious cardiac, respiratory, or gastrointestinal causes
	- Thorough history and examination are the most important diagnostic tools

- Red flags
	- First episode severe chest pain
	- Pain radiating to arm or back
	- Exertional pain
	- Syncope
	- Dizziness
	- Congenital or acquired heart disease
	- Family history of sudden cardiac death
	- Family history of arrhythmia
	- History of Kawasaki disease
	- Connective tissue disease
	- Longstanding diabetes
	- Pleuritic chest pain
	- Haemoptysis
	- Fever
	- Chronic respiratory disease
	- Recent surgery
	- Immobility
	- Hypercoagulable state
	- Central venous catheter
	- Recurrent vomiting
	- Dysphagia
	- Odynophagia
	- Food impaction
	- Suspected foreign body ingestion
	- Suspected button battery ingestion

- Investigations
	- Initial
		- Usually none required if:
			- Well child
			- Normal examination
			- No risk factors
	- Most appropriate
		- Clinical assessment (history + examination)
	- If red flags present:
		- ECG
		- Chest X-ray
		- Blood tests
	- Consider after senior review:
		- Echocardiography
		- CT pulmonary angiography
		- Other specialist investigations

- Treatment
	- Initial
		- Reassurance
		- Simple analgesia
			- Paracetamol
			- Ibuprofen
	- Most appropriate
		- Treat underlying cause
			- Costochondritis → NSAIDs
			- Asthma → bronchodilators
			- Pneumonia → antibiotics if indicated
			- GORD → acid suppression
			- Myocarditis/pericarditis → specialist management
			- Pulmonary embolism → anticoagulation
	- Early senior involvement if:
		- Trauma
		- Critically unwell child
		- Cardiorespiratory compromise

- Prognosis
	- Excellent in most children
	- Majority have benign self-limiting causes
	- Serious cardiac disease is rare
	- Safe discharge if:
		- Symptoms improved or resolved
		- Normal vital signs
		- No serious cause identified
		- Appropriate follow-up arranged
